# KAOS — Season One: The Story So Far

The gods are real, they are contemporary, and they are running a protection racket.

Zeus lives on Olympus in a white villa with a swimming pool, a peacock, a skeet range and a
staff of terrified ball boys. Down on Earth — in Krete, in the ruins of Troy, in the Amazon
compounds — humans worship on a schedule. They confess into booths staffed by women who cut
out their own tongues in Hera's service. They volunteer, on camera, to be sacrificed. And
when they die they are buried with a coin, because the coin buys passage through the Frame,
and the Frame is where a soul is *renewed* into a better life to come.

None of that is true.

Renewal is a lie, and it is the biggest lie ever told. The Frame does not recycle souls; it
harvests them. Zeus was born mortal. He killed his father Kronos, absorbed the man's soul at
the moment of death, found the power intoxicating, and worked out how to do it again — and
again, and again, until the killing became tedious and he ordered his brother Hades to build
a machine that would do it at industrial scale. What comes out of that machine is Meander
water, and the gods drink it to stay immortal. Every temple, every festival, every consenting
sacrifice is a supply chain with a good story wrapped round it.

One person has spent thousands of years arranging the counter-move. Prometheus is chained to
a rock within sight of Zeus's house, an eagle eats his liver every day, and he narrates this
season directly to you. His plan requires three humans, in the right place at the right time,
and a prophecy. The same prophecy, word for word, given to all three:

**"A line appears, the order wanes, the family falls, and kaos reigns."**

None of the three knows what they are for. Neither, in the end, does he.

## Part I — The Season, Episode by Episode

### One

It is Olympia Day. Zeus performs the weather, checks the Meander, and thinks he sees it
blip. Hera tells him it looks normal. Then he finds a wrinkle on his forehead — *a line
appears* — and the season's engine turns over: a paranoid god decides his prophecy has begun.

In Krete, Eurydice — Riddy — wakes intending to leave her husband and cannot manage it. In a
supermarket a shoplifter is dragged out ranting that everyone steals and the gods steal
souls, and tells Riddy that today's the day, that she will leave everyone today, and that her
love is dead. The woman is right about all of it. She is Cassandra, though nobody says her
name until the finale.

Dionysus buys his father a watch and is rejected: Zeus already has one, a lucky talisman from
Hercules. Dionysus asks for a promotion, is called a lightweight by Hera, and is thrown
through a table. The Olympia broadcast delivers a consenting human sacrifice and then the
unveiling of a new monument, which has been smeared with excrement and the words *fuck the
gods*. Zeus wants to wipe out half the world; Hera talks him down and tells him to phone
Poseidon, who owns Krete.

Riddy goes to a confession booth and finds her mother behind the screen — a tacita, tongueless
for Hera since Riddy was five. She confesses that she no longer loves Orpheus, that she prayed
for a decade to have her mother back, that she knows her prophecy and does not want it. She
says she defies the gods. Hera, listening, tells her to shut up.

That evening Orpheus dedicates a new song to her from the stage. Riddy walks into the street
and is hit by a truck. At the funeral, Orpheus cannot bring himself to place the coin, and
someone else does it for him. Afterwards he buys a gun and takes it to the beach — where
Dionysus tackles him, is shot in the shoulder, does not bleed, and asks whether he would like
to see his wife again without having to die.

### Two

Ten years earlier, a young bartender is recognised by Amazon women, told he has dishonoured
the gods and his tribe, and shot dead with a crossbow. His mother is standing among them.
This is Caeneus, the second of Prometheus's three humans: dead a decade, buried without a
coin, and living entirely in the past.

Riddy crosses the Styx on the ferry, watching Hades's orientation video — death is the only
certainty, your devotion is about to pay off, everything is going to be okay. At the port the
cerberus dogs single her out, and she is taken aside and told the truth about her situation:
no coin, no Frame, no Renewal. She is *unresolved*, and she will wait two hundred years for
another chance. She insists there has been a mistake. There are no mistakes here.

Caeneus, a dog handler, is promoted to diver by Medusa alongside his friend Prue, over his
objections — he is waiting at the port for his mother to arrive, and he does not want to
leave. Medusa selected him personally and will not say why. He finds Riddy weeping at the
edge of the Lethe, where the water refuses to rise to meet her, and tells her he is the same:
buried coinless, stuck, and it gets better.

Above, Dionysus drives Orpheus at lethal speed to prove that he cannot die, and takes him to
the Cave — a venue entered through a bin, where the desperate compete on a game show for
passage into the underworld. The Fates preside, setting the questions and keeping the score.
The rounds are cruel and exact: name every freckle on your loved one; tell us about your son
without speaking; sing of your love without your voice. Orpheus is doing well until the Fates
produce the thing in his pocket. It is Riddy's coin. He took it, so that she could not move
on without him. Game over — until Dionysus buys her husband's passage anyway, with the watch
he stole from his father.

Zeus, meanwhile, works through his children's answering machines. Ares, Athena, Apollo,
Aphrodite, Artemis, Hermes. Nobody is home. Later, Hera arrives wearing a stranger's face at
the house of his pregnant lover, extracts the baby, turns the mother into a bee, and orders
Zeus to kill the newborn. He does.

### Three

Ari's birthday, told from the beginning. Ariadne is the daughter of President Minos of Krete
and the third of the three, and she has been told her whole life that she smothered her twin
brother Glaucus in her sleep when they were infants. Her mother has never let it go: there is
an annual waxwork of the boy, sculpted by the palace artificer Daedalus, and the nose is
never right.

Six of the Trojan 7 — the group who desecrated the monument — are being held in the labyrinth
beneath the palace. Minos gives them a deadline: if the seventh presents himself by midnight,
the six are pardoned. Otherwise the Minotaur.

Poseidon comes to Krete to bring it to heel on Zeus's orders, drowning a servant for fun on
the way. Ari argues with her father for mercy and is told the gods choose who rules. Her
bodyguard Theseus takes her to the Munis for her birthday — a fighting pit, a place for pain
and rage — where she watches a gladiator die and sees three women in the crowd who should not
be there. They are the Furies, and she knows what they are for.

Theseus, who is working with the Trojans, takes her to the seventh man: Nax, who is
Astyanax, Andromache's son, thrown from the walls of Troy as a child and presumed dead ever
since. He wants to surrender. His own people locked him up to stop him. Ari goes home and
asks her father for the Trojans as her birthday present, and he grants it, and the seven walk
free.

Poseidon waterboards Minos in his own shower over the pardon. There is no decision, he
explains, if I tell you to do something. At midnight the Trojans are rounded up again and the
Minotaur is released into the labyrinth. Ari runs towards Andromache's screaming and is told:
*you did this.* Her father tells her it is the will of the gods. Then the Furies arrive, and
they are pleasant, and they say they are here about her brother.

### Four

The oldest crime in the series is a flashback. Prometheus, free and in love with Charon,
makes his lover swear to protect a stranger he will one day recognise by a mark — and then
strangles him, and tells him to try not to hate him. Charon becomes the ferryman of the Styx.
The greater good, Prometheus tells us, always comes at a personal cost, and the cost is
always paid by somebody.

Orpheus enters the underworld through the bin with a fellow contestant, Anatole. Charon meets
them on the water, knows exactly who Orpheus is and what he did with his wife's coin, and
agrees to a wary truce. Mid-river he stops the boat and admits he lied: he can only take one
of them. He will wait for one of them to break. Orpheus kills Anatole. Further downriver
Charybdis takes hold of him, and Charon — seeing the mark — pulls him out. It means, he says,
that something is true.

Below, Caeneus is walking his old dog Fotis when he finds Nax outside a vast dim structure,
terrified, insisting he has already been through the Frame and that they have to help all of
them, in there. Riddy and Caeneus follow the screaming inside and find the Nothing: an
enormous space full of standing, ruined people. Among them is a woman they had helped through
the Frame that same morning.

Hades has already discovered that a soul escaped, and has chosen to hide it from Zeus.
Persephone tries to leave a message about it; Hades deletes it and leaves a cheerful voicemail
instead about excellent new divers and no problems whatsoever. Poseidon warns Dionysus that if
a mortal ever brings someone back from the dead, Zeus will destroy him and try to destroy
them all — and tells him to get that watch back. Dionysus tortures a shopkeeper for a
look-alike and passes the counterfeit to his father, claiming he found it by the gate. Zeus
wears the fake for the rest of the season. He thanks his son, promises him power, and lays
out phase two of his plan for the humans: hatred, seeded small — one rape, one dead child —
until continents burn.

Zeus also shoots several ball boys over the missing watch, and Hera phones Poseidon about a
war room and a serious escalation. And in the underworld, Hippolyta — the Amazon who fired
the crossbow — arrives dead, recognises Caeneus, and tells him that his mother gave him up
freely. Very freely.

### Five

Prometheus corrects his own myth: he was not chained for giving humans fire. He was chained
for telling Zeus he had become everything he once hated. And before any of that, a visitor
came to his door — unseen, unidentified, never explained — and told him that it was not his
fate to kill Zeus. He must kill Charon. He must regain Zeus's trust and submit to his
punishment. There is a greater plan.

The Furies take Ari to a private cinema, give her popcorn, and show her a film of the truth.
Poseidon installing Minos as president, with three rules: this is all on loan, I put you here,
and please me and I can make you immortal. Minos objecting that his prophecy forbids him
children, and reciting it — *"Your end begins in the marital bed. The first child to draw
breath will kill you dead."* Poseidon's solution: have twins, and kill the one that comes out
first. Then the twins as infants, curled together asleep, and Minos ordering Daedalus to take
the firstborn away.

Ari refuses to watch the end. She accuses the Furies of hounding people into their own graves;
they answer that the gods are the source of all her pain, settle an old favour with the Fates,
tell her she is on her own now, and advise her to talk to Daedalus. She goes home, asks her
father to recite his prophecy, and watches him detonate. Then Daedalus confesses. He killed
the baby because Minos threatened his son Icarus — and Icarus died anyway, building wings to
escape a father he had learned was a murderer. And then the correction: Glaucus never died.
Glaucus is the Minotaur.

On Olympus, Zeus and Dionysus execute phase one — earthquakes, floods, fire — and Zeus feels
vital again. Then he does the forbidden thing. He walks into Hera's bathroom, where the
tacitas' tongues are kept in a cabinet of drawers and can be replayed, and he listens, and he
hears his wife's voice in a stranger's confession. He tears the voice box out of a tacita who
will not write down what she knows. Hera meets the accusation with a masterpiece of
counter-attack — she eats his pain, she could have taken the Earth or the underworld, she is
queen and that was the oath they swore — and dares him to produce the evidence. He cannot find
it. She talks him down off a ladder and he weeps in her arms. Then she has the tacita buried.

In the Nothing, Hades finds Nax, tells him gently that there is no need to be afraid, and
extracts his soul by hand. Riddy is knocked to the ground by it. Caeneus, standing next to
her, feels nothing at all and cannot say why. Prometheus, watching: *he will soon.* Hades
recites the prophecy to himself and asks what he has done.

### Six

Caeneus tells Riddy how he left the Amazons. His friend Leos came back and was killed for it.
His mother told him she knew him, that the form did not fit the content, that her own prophecy
had foretold a boy — gave him the name Caeneus, and sent him away. They agree that they hate
the gods. They kiss. Then they compare prophecies and find that theirs are identical, word for
word, which everyone has always been told is impossible.

They reason the rest of it out between them. Everyone who goes through the Frame ends up in
the Nothing. There is no Renewal. And the family that falls is not any human family. It is
the gods.

Hades, dressed by his wife for the confrontation, goes to Olympus and tells the truth: the
prophecy is real and Zeus is making it come true; a soul escaped unfinished; the Nothing is
overloaded; the Frame must be reset to a baseline level of actual Renewal or the whole
structure breaks — the underworld, Olympus and Earth together. Zeus tells him you cannot run
out of souls, because he is king of the gods, forces him into the swimming pool, calls him
the runt, and puts a lightning bolt through him. Over his body, a vow: *I will destroy him. I
swear, I will find a way.*

Prometheus, meanwhile, talks Zeus out of destroying the Fates by pointing out that a prophecy
means only what he insists it means — and mentions, apparently in passing, that President
Minos of Krete lives in open defiance of his own. Zeus seizes on it. Make Minos kill his son,
on camera, and watch a prophecy break.

Poseidon consults the Fates, who tell him truthfully that Minos can kill his son, and that
people can occasionally defy their prophecies. Hera goes to Krete personally and threatens
Minos with his daughter's suffering. He will do it.

And Prometheus tells the audience the thing the season has been circling: Kronos, the
absorbed soul, the intoxication, the repetition, the Frame, the liquidisation, the drinking.
The lie must be maintained and the humans kept distracted, because that is all that stands
between the gods and the people they eat.

Orpheus reaches the wilderness, the final test, which nobody has ever survived. Dying of
thirst, he is offered water by one of the Fates, with the small print that drinking it means
losing his wife. He refuses, and chooses to die trying. That was the test. He passes it, walks
into Asphodel, and finds Prue, who knows exactly who Riddy is.

### Seven

Two households collapse in the same hour.

Orpheus and Riddy finally meet. He apologises for the coin and tells her he has come to take
her back to life, and she refuses him — she has a job here, and friends, and things to do.
Medusa pulls her aside and asks a different question: there are rebels down here, people who
see the gods for what they are, and would she like to help save the world from Zeus. Later,
Caeneus is the one who persuades her to go. Go back, he says. Tell them what we saw. Tell
them to live for themselves and not for the gods.

Hades receives Orpheus with real warmth — no human has ever come this far — and then refuses
him. The final test was convincing Hades, and he has not. Orpheus offers to swap places and
is denied. He may stay, dead, with his wife. In the lift afterwards Persephone tells her
husband he has just fumbled a chance to make a statement Zeus could not ignore. Hades says
that would be an act of war. Persephone says the war has begun, and sends Medusa to fetch
Orpheus's wife anyway.

In Krete, Daedalus tells Ari how her brother became the Minotaur — the dark, the
namelessness, the biting, the first killing and her father's approval — and that he made the
mask and the horns, and also a small bell, so that the boy would know it was him coming and
be gentle. Then he takes her into the labyrinth. Minos, discovering Daedalus missing, reaches
the control room and pushes the button that opens the cage, and Glaucus mauls the old man.

Ari faces her brother anyway. She says his name. She tells him she is his twin. She takes off
the mask. Then her father arrives, and she refuses to move, and she names his crime and calls
him the monster and tells him to shut his mouth about the gods — and Minos kills Glaucus with
a knife.

Olympus erupts. Zeus is euphoric and suddenly hungry. Minos, elated, tells his daughter
everything: he defied his prophecy, the gods are on his side, her brother came out quiet and
she came out screaming, and the burden of blame is what made her great. And Ari understands
that she was the first to draw breath. She kills him. Watching on television, Dionysus says he
thinks he is in love. Zeus collapses.

### Eight

Zeus dreams that he is bleeding, and a Fate tells him that gods do not bleed. He wakes certain
it was a vision, and goes to destroy the Fates. They know why he has come and help him stage
it, choosing the poolroom because they like it. They explain, first, that a prophecy needs his
belief to come to pass — the possibility exists, but without him it is nothing — and that he
should perhaps be frightened of the people moving against him. He burns them alive. As one of
them dies he sees his son's watch on her wrist.

Caeneus finds Prue and Adrian at the river and begs them to help him stop people going through
the Frame. And his mother is standing there. She shot herself the night he was buried, and
then waited ten years, because it had to be today. Today she takes him through the Frame. She
walks into the water to prove it, and he goes in after her. Prometheus, watching, panics: the
Fates promised him that Caeneus would be protected, that when he went through the Frame *the
family falls* would happen, and that Prometheus would go free. None of it is happening.

Riddy and Orpheus climb out onto the Earth together. She tells him she was leaving him the day
she died — that it had stopped being love and become need. He asks her to begin again, and at
the surface she says goodbye instead, and he drives away alone. At the mouth of the Cave a
woman is waiting for her, and gives her name at last: Cassandra. Riddy is a prophet now. She
must go to Ari and set the living free, while Caeneus does the same for the dead.

On Olympus, Zeus produces rationed bottles of Meander water — one each, none for his wife —
announces that he has destroyed the Fates, and declares that there is no more fate, only
loyalty. Dionysus confesses everything: the watch, the Cave, Orpheus, all of it, because
humans get lied to and deserve a few more years. Zeus praises him, then kills his kitten in
front of him to demonstrate that love is weakness, and sets Hera's bees on fire. He strips
Poseidon of every city including the City of the Dead, tells Persephone that Hades can collect
his own bottle, and confines Hera to the house.

Then he visits Prometheus expecting congratulations, and gets the truth instead: he had his
chance to destroy Zeus and could not do it, and that weakness is the only reason Zeus still
rules. Mid-sentence, Prometheus vanishes. The Fates — entirely alive — have freed him. Fate,
they point out, cannot be destroyed, and all he ever had to do was trust and wait.

Ari brings her mother to Glaucus's body and tells her that he was alive for all thirty of
those years, that her husband did it, and that she killed him for it. Her mother says: good.
Ari announces that she will not rule for the gods. Hera picks up a telephone and tells someone
to gather the troops and make up a spare bed. Prometheus appears inside Zeus's house to tell
him the vision was real and kaos is coming.

And in the river, Caeneus's mother speaks a single word — *kaos* — and something passes
between them, and she is back. Hades, arriving at the water's edge, says the impossible thing
out loud: you can renew human souls.

## Part II — The Threads

### The lie at the bottom of everything

Divinity in this world is not innate. It is manufactured, and it is consumed.

Zeus was born mortal, to a father who tormented him for years. He killed Kronos — violently,
personally — and at the moment of death he absorbed the man's soul. It became part of him. It
gave him power, and the power was so good that he did it again, though it took him years to
work out how he had managed it the first time. Eventually the killing became a chore, and the
Frame was built to do it wholesale. Souls go in; Meander water comes out; the gods drink it
and stay immortal. Renewal is the cover story, and the myths were written to protect it.

That makes the whole cosmology a logistics problem with a religion bolted on. The coin buys
passage. The Styx carries the dead down; the Lethe is where the crossing actually happens;
divers persuade souls into the water. Nobody can be forced through the Frame — free will is
inconvenient, which is precisely why divers exist, and persuasion is the entire job. Anyone
buried without a coin cannot cross at all, and is told to wait two hundred years for another
chance.

What comes out the far side of the Frame is the Nothing: an enormous dim space, packed with
standing, ruined people, which nobody with authority has looked at in a very long time. It
was never built for this volume. The overload made the Frame glitch, which is how Nax came
out unfinished — the first time in history — and it is why Hades has to finish the job by hand
while two of Prometheus's three humans watch him do it. Hades's warning to Zeus is
structural, not moral: the system cannot disintegrate what it is holding, so eventually it
breaks, and when it breaks it takes the underworld, Olympus and Earth with it. Zeus's answer
is that you cannot run out of souls, because he is king of the gods.

The rules that bound all this held for the whole season, and then two of them broke inside
two episodes. The dead do not come back — except Persephone sent one back, deliberately, as
an act of war. And no one can renew a soul — except a dead human just did, in front of the
god of death, and neither of them understands how.

### Olympus: a wrinkle, and what it cost

Zeus's season is a man manufacturing his own prophecy by sprinting away from it. He finds the
line on his forehead, decides the rest must follow, and then personally performs every step
of it. The Fates tell him so to his face in the last hour: the possibility exists, but without
his belief it cannot come to pass.

What it costs him is the house. He shoots his own staff over a watch that his son stole and
replaced with a counterfeit he never notices. He breaks the one oath he swore to his wife,
listens to the confessions kept in her tacitas' tongues, hears her voice in one of them, and
then cannot produce the evidence when she demands it — so she gaslights him into weeping in
her arms and has the witness buried. He puts lightning through the brother who came to warn
him the machinery is failing. He burns the Fates. He rations the Meander to his own family,
one bottle each and none for Hera, and announces that fate is abolished and only loyalty
remains. He kills his son's kitten to prove that love is weakness. He sets his wife's bees on
fire and confines her to the property. He takes every city from Poseidon, including the City
of the Dead.

By the final scene there is nobody left in the building who is not an enemy, and he has
dreamed of bleeding, which is not supposed to be possible.

Around him the others have quietly stopped pretending. Hera has run the confession economy of
the entire mortal world for as long as anyone can remember, has been conducting an affair
with Poseidon for most of it, and refuses his offer to run away because the house is hers.
Poseidon has loved her forever and will never have her, and by the end has been stripped of
everything he owned and told to grow up. Dionysus wanted a promotion and a father who could
see him; what he got instead was Orpheus, and a front-row view of what a human being will do
for love, and by the end he has chosen the humans. Hades and Persephone have gone from
covering for the system to trying to dismantle it from underneath.

### Krete: the thing under the palace

Minos received an accurate prophecy and read it wrong, and everything that happens in Krete
follows from that single misreading. He was told his end begins in the marital bed and that
the first child to draw breath will kill him. He assumed that meant the firstborn. On
Poseidon's advice he had twins and ordered the one who came out first destroyed.

Daedalus — already his prisoner, kept so his talents could not be sold elsewhere — was given
the baby and the order, and could not do it, because Minos had threatened his son Icarus.
Icarus died anyway, building wings to escape a father he had discovered was a murderer. So
Daedalus spent thirty years in a palace sculpting an annual waxwork of a boy who was alive in
the basement.

Glaucus grew up in the dark, unnamed, unspoken to. He bit the guards. Then he maimed one.
Then he killed one, and his father was *impressed*, and that was the moment a secret became a
weapon. Daedalus built the mask and the horns, and also a small bell, so that the boy would
know it was him coming and be gentle.

Ari grew up on the other side of the same lie: told that she had smothered her twin in her
sleep, watched by a mother who never forgave her for it, doing penance for thirty years for a
murder that never happened. When the Furies finally show her the truth, they do it kindly and
with popcorn, on the explicit grounds that they are not there to punish her. Their parting
advice is that the gods are the source of all her pain.

The end of it is broadcast live to Olympus. Minos, ordered by Hera and Poseidon to kill his
son, does it — and then, giddy at having beaten fate, tells Ari how the birth actually went:
her brother came out quiet, and she came out screaming. She was the first to draw breath. The
prophecy was correct the entire time; every person who read it was wrong. She kills him with
a knife, inherits Krete by default, shows her mother the body of the son she was told was
buried thirty years ago, and announces that she will not rule for the gods.

### Below: Riddy, Caeneus, and the Nothing

Riddy dies on the day she finally meant to leave her husband, and arrives in the afterlife to
discover that he took her coin so that she could not move on without him. That strands her
among the unresolved for two hundred years — and it is the single most useful thing that
happens to Prometheus's plan, because it puts her where the truth is kept.

Caeneus has been there for ten years already, dead at the hands of his own people, waiting at
the port for the mother who led them to him. He is promoted to diver by Medusa, who chose him
specifically and will not explain herself. The two of them find each other, and then they
find Nax outside the Nothing, and then they go inside.

What follows is the season's actual detective work, and the three humans never meet to do it.
They see a woman they personally helped through the Frame that morning, standing among the
ruined. They watch Hades pull a boy's soul out with his hands, and Riddy is floored by it
while Caeneus feels nothing at all. They compare their prophecies and find them identical,
which they have both been told all their lives is impossible. And from those pieces they get
the whole thing: everyone who goes through the Frame ends up in the Nothing, there is no
Renewal, and the family that falls is the gods.

Then they split the work. Riddy goes back up to tell the living, sent by Persephone over
Hades's ruling and over her own initial refusal. Caeneus stays to stop the Frame from below —
and is immediately shut down by Medusa, who tells him he does not understand the whole
picture, that Riddy was the important one, that there is a plan beyond him, and that he
should keep his head down.

He does not keep his head down. His mother arrives, having shot herself the night he was
buried and then waited a decade because the day had to be the right day, and walks into the
Lethe to prove to him the Frame is not a lie. He follows her in. She says one word, and
something passes between them, and she comes back — which no god has ever been able to do for
anyone, and which the god of death is standing right there to witness.

### Orpheus, who would do anything

Prometheus is cold about Orpheus and says so directly: he is not one of the three, and that
is exactly what makes him useful. An ego that size accepts a cosmic purpose without ever
suspecting the purpose might belong to somebody else.

He loves his wife the way a spotlight loves a stage. He writes a song with her name on it and
plays it to a crowd on the night she dies, without noticing that she had spent the whole day
trying to tell him she was leaving. He takes her coin at the funeral, which is presented as
devotion and is actually a lock on a door. Everything after that is him finding out, slowly
and expensively, what he is.

He tries to shoot himself and is tackled by a god. He fails the Cave's quiz on the evidence
of the coin in his own pocket and is smuggled through anyway on a stolen watch. On the Styx,
Charon tells him only one of them can cross and then waits, and Orpheus murders the man
sitting next to him. He is saved from Charybdis only because a Fate marked him years before
he was born into the story, and because a dead ferryman keeps a promise made to the man who
strangled him. In the wilderness he is offered water on the condition that he give up his
wife, and he refuses and lies down to die instead — and that, it turns out, is the one test
he was always going to pass.

Then he reaches her, and she says no. She has a job. She has friends. She has, for the first
time in her life, things of her own. Hades refuses to release him on principle and Persephone
overrules her husband and releases them both anyway, not for Orpheus's sake but as a
declaration of war. On the way up Riddy tells him the truth — that it had stopped being love
some time ago and become need — and he asks her to start again, and at the surface she says
goodbye.

He ends the season alive, alone, driving away. He is the only living human who has walked
into death and back out again, and nobody has any use for him yet.

### Prometheus, who was never the author

The narrator's authority is the season's longest con, and it is run on the audience.

He tells you he has a plan: three humans, the right place, the right time, thousands of years
of patience. He lies to Zeus's face in the first hour — prophecies are only for humans, the
Meander does not blip, you cannot be destroyed — and turns to you and says the opposite. He
steers Zeus away from wiping out half the world and towards stories instead. In the sixth
hour he drops the idea of Minos into Zeus's ear the way you would drop a coin into a machine,
and it detonates Krete, kills Minos, and cracks Zeus's composure.

And the price he paid to be in position was Charon. A visitor came to his door before his
imprisonment and told him it was not his fate to kill Zeus — that he must kill his lover,
regain Zeus's trust, and submit to the rock, because there is a greater plan. The visitor is
never identified. Prometheus did all of it. Charon has spent the intervening millennia
ferrying the dead and keeping, bitterly and exactly, the promise he was made to swear before
he was murdered for it.

Then, in the last hour, the whole thing comes apart in front of him. The Fates had promised
that Caeneus would be protected, that the family would fall when he went through the Frame,
and that Prometheus would go free. Caeneus goes into the water and none of it happens on cue,
and the narrator — who has spent eight episodes being the most composed voice in the series —
audibly panics.

He is freed anyway, and not by his own design. The Fates, alive after their own immolation,
let him out and tell him that fate cannot be destroyed, that all he ever had to do was trust
and wait, and that what he does with his freedom now is his own business. His last word to
Zeus is not a plan and not a threat; it is a warning that the vision was real. His own
confession is the more damning one: he had his chance to destroy Zeus, and he loved him too
much to take it, and that failure is the only reason any of this is still standing.

## Part III — Where Everyone Stands

### Zeus

Alone in his own house, having converted every relative into an enemy in a single afternoon.
He has burned the Fates, rationed the Meander, seized every city, incinerated his wife's bees
and confined her to the property. He believes he has abolished fate. He does not know that the
Fates survived, that his son has defected, or that his watch has been a fake for days. He has
dreamed of bleeding.

### Prometheus

Free, uncaged, and standing inside Zeus's house for the first time in thousands of years. His
plan has slipped its leash: the freedom he was promised arrived late, by someone else's
timing, with the explicit note that what he does with it now is his own problem. He knows the
Fates are alive. He no longer sounds like a man who knows what happens next.

### Hera

Confined by her husband's order, cut out of the Meander ration, her bees burned. Within
moments of the humiliation she is on the telephone to someone the audience never sees, telling
them to gather the troops and make up a spare bed. Nobody on Olympus knows she made that call.

### Poseidon

Stripped of Krete, stripped of every other city including the City of the Dead, and told to
grow up. He is in love with a woman who is now under house arrest, and he has just been given
a very large reason to stop being a company man.

### Hades

In the underworld, recovering from a lightning strike delivered by his brother for the crime
of telling the truth. He intends to go back into the Nothing and find a way to restore what he
harvested. And he has just watched a dead human do the thing he was certain could not be done.

### Persephone

Running the underworld and the most decisive rebel among the gods. She has already committed
the first act of war — sending a dead woman back to the living, deliberately, against her own
husband's ruling, with Orpheus released alongside her as the rider. She meant it as a message
to Zeus. He has not received it yet.

### Dionysus

On Earth with the Trojans, having confessed everything to his father and been answered with a
dead kitten and a lecture on love as weakness. He asked what they want and was told: help us
rebuild Troy and destroy Olympus. He is the only god who has ever liked humans, and he is now
committed.

### Riddy

Alive, on Earth, standing outside the Cave with the truth in her mouth and no idea how to say
it to anyone who will believe her. Cassandra has told her that she is a prophet now, and given
her the charge: go to Ari, and set the living free.

### Caeneus

Standing in the Lethe, holding his mother, who is *back*. He renewed a human soul in front of
the god of death and cannot explain how. He stayed behind to stop the Frame, was told by
Medusa that there is a plan beyond him and that he should keep his head down, and has now
comprehensively failed to do so.

### Ari

Ruler of Krete by patricide and default. She knows her father murdered nothing, imprisoned her
brother for thirty years, and killed him on the gods' instruction. She has shown her mother
the body and been told: good. She has announced publicly that she will not rule for the gods,
which is not a position anyone in Krete has ever survived holding.

### Orpheus

Alive, alone, driving away from a wife who thanked him and left. He knows the gods lie, he
knows what he did to Anatole and to Riddy's coin, and he is the only living person to have
walked into the underworld and out again. Nobody has asked him for anything yet.

### Cassandra

At the mouth of the Cave, having finally handed her burden to someone else. She has been right
about every single thing for her entire life and nobody has ever believed her once.

### Medusa

In the underworld, recruiting for a rebellion that predates Riddy's arrival and extends past
Caeneus's understanding of it. She knows more about the plan than either of them, and has said
so, and has refused to say what.

### Charon

On the Styx, having kept the promise he was murdered to make. He knows Prometheus killed him
for a plan, and he asked to be told why, and was refused.

### The Fates

Alive, unbothered, and standing in the ashes of their own execution. They freed Prometheus,
told him fate cannot be destroyed, and went back to whatever it is they do. They are the only
parties in the story who appear to be enjoying themselves.

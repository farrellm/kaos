# Chapter Twenty-Six

## The Argument

Nobody tells me it is happening.

That is not a complaint, it is the method of this chapter: what follows is what can be seen
from where I was standing, plus what was said afterwards by people who were closer, and
where the two do not agree I have kept both.

It starts at about four in the morning with the water going off.

---

Ares has had an army for four centuries.

This is the part that ought to have been obvious and was not obvious to anyone, including
his mother until she walked up to his gate with two bags. He was given war. He took it
seriously in the way that nobody in that family has ever taken anything seriously, which is
to say he built the apparatus and then maintained it, annually, for four hundred years,
without being asked and without once being visited. There are supply lines. There is a
standing order about boots.

What comes up the mountain at four in the morning is not gods. That is the thing the
accounts get wrong afterwards, and Poseidon in particular tells it wrong, twice, in my
hearing, because he was underneath the whole of it and could not see. It is about nine
hundred dead men who have been kept out of the river for four centuries on Ares's own
authority, which is a thing he has been quietly permitted to do because nobody in the
administration of the underworld ever audits an Olympian, and which explains, incidentally,
several very old discrepancies in Charon's manifests that Charon has been complaining about
for as long as I have known him.

They are extremely good. They come up the west face in the dark, and they take the terrace,
and they take the house to the second floor, and Poseidon takes the Meander itself and stands
in it, which is the correct move, which is the move that should have finished it, and for
about eleven minutes on a Tuesday morning Zeus is standing in his own garden with his wife's
son's army between him and everything he owns.

I am on the plateau. I have a view of the west face and no view at all of the garden. I want
to be exact about the limits of it: I heard the eleven minutes. I did not see them.

---

Then it stops.

Here is what I can report. It does not stop with a battle. There is no answering force,
because there is no answering force to have — the six who came back have been in the house
for a fortnight doing nothing in particular and none of them is a soldier, and Athena, who
would have been, is not speaking to anybody at all.

It stops because Zeus goes down into the Meander with Poseidon standing in it, and puts a
hand in the water, and does the thing he learned on his father four thousand years ago, and
does not finish it.

That is the report and I have had it from three people and they agree. He does not kill his
brother. He takes hold of the soul and stops, with his hand in the water, and holds it there,
and lets Poseidon feel exactly how far in it has gone and precisely how little effort is
being spent, and then he lets go and helps him up and asks whether he wants a towel.

Nine hundred dead men are still on the terrace when this happens. They can all see it.

The withdrawal is orderly and takes about an hour. Hera goes north with her son and most of
the army and does not look at the house on the way out. Poseidon goes with them, on his feet,
under his own power, with a towel.

---

They have the argument at eleven o'clock in the long room, and the argument is about what to
do next, and it is a real argument, which is what nobody expects and what makes what follows
what it is.

Apollo says the family is finished if this is what it now is. Hermes says something
conciliatory about the boots. Aphrodite, who has said nothing since she came back, says that
the whole of it is because he cannot be alone in a house, which is true and is the cruellest
thing anybody says all morning. Artemis says almost nothing. She is standing at the window
with her back to the room, which is where she has been standing for a fortnight, and when
Apollo puts it to her directly she says, without turning round, that she would rather be in
the north.

That is what she says. Nine words. She would rather be in the north.

Zeus is very reasonable about it. He asks her to say it again so that the others can hear it
properly. She says it again.

He crosses the room and he takes her face in both hands, the way you would to look at
someone you have not seen for a long time, and there is a moment there — everyone in the room
reads it as a moment, Hermes told me afterwards that he had begun to relax — where it is a
father holding his daughter's face.

Then he does it.

It takes about as long as it took on the terrace with Alpheus. Apollo is four feet away. He
does not move, and I want to be careful here because I have heard him judged for it since and
I was in the room and I do not judge him: there is nothing between the beginning and the end
of it in which a person could have moved.

She goes into him through his hands. He steps back and he is steady on his feet and slightly
out of breath, and he looks round the room at the five of them, and he says:

"Anybody else want to be in the north?"

---

Nobody answers.

Nobody answers for a very long time, and then Hermes says, "No," and that is the end of the
argument, and it is the last time anyone in that family raises the question of what to do
next in the hearing of the man it concerns.

They eat at one. All five of them. Zeus carves, and asks Apollo about a house he is thinking
of buying, and Apollo answers him — properly, in sentences, with detail about the roof — and
that is the thing out of the whole morning that I have not been able to put down since. Not
the terrace. Not the hands. A young god four hours after his twin was taken out of the world
in front of him, sitting at a table describing a roof to the man who did it, because the
alternative was to be in the north.

By evening the ones who came back have all taken rooms in the east wing, which they had not
done before, and Hermes has been given something to do.

---

I was in the long room for the whole of it.

I came in behind Aphrodite at about ten to eleven and I stood by the door and I was there for
the argument and for the nine words and for what he did with his hands, and I was there when
they went in to eat, and I did not go in.

Nobody addressed me at any point. Not one of them. Not to greet me, not to send me out, not
to ask what I thought, which was for four thousand years the single most reliable event in
that house — there was never a difficult morning in all that time in which somebody did not
eventually turn round and ask me what I thought.

He did not ask. He was six feet from me when he did it and he did not look up.

I am not putting that here because it is the important thing about the morning. Artemis is
the important thing about the morning. I am putting it here because it is the only thing I
have to report about myself, and this account has been running for a long time now on the
assumption that there was something to report about myself, and I would rather set it down
plainly than keep arranging the furniture around the fact that on the worst morning since
the curtain came up I was a man standing by a door.

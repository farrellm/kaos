# Chapter Twenty-Six

## The Argument

Nobody tells me it is happening.

That is not a complaint, it is the method of this chapter: what follows is what can be seen
from where I was standing, plus what was said afterwards by people who were closer, and where
the two do not agree I have kept both.

It starts at about four in the morning with the water going off.

That is the only part of it that reaches the plateau. There is a sound the Meander makes that a
person stops hearing inside a week and then goes on not hearing for four thousand years, which
is the sound of a thing running, and at four in the morning it stops, and I am awake before I
know why. It is a clock in a house.

I get up and go to the edge of the rock, which is a thing I can do now, and look down at a
white building in the dark with nothing wrong with it.

Then the west face starts moving.

---

Ares has had an army for four centuries.

This is the part that ought to have been obvious and was not obvious to anyone, including his
mother until she walked up to his gate with two bags. He was given war. He took it seriously in
the way that nobody in that family has ever taken anything seriously, which is to say he built
the apparatus and then maintained it, annually, for four hundred years, without being asked and
without once being visited. There are supply lines. There is a standing order about boots.

I made a joke of the boots in the autumn and I am going to put them down properly now, because
they are the reason the west face works.

A formation is finished the day the boots are. That is his own sentence, said to his mother in
a valley, in the tone of a man explaining that bread goes stale. Nine hundred dead men climb
two thousand feet of bad rock in the dark and arrive at the top able to run, and they arrive
able to run because somebody has been replacing their footwear on a rotation for four
centuries, for nothing, against a day nobody had scheduled.

What comes up the mountain at four in the morning is not gods. That is the thing the accounts
get wrong afterwards, and Poseidon in particular tells it wrong, twice, in my hearing, because
he was underneath the whole of it and could not see. It is about nine hundred dead men who have
been kept out of the river for four centuries on Ares's own authority, which is a thing he has
been quietly permitted to do because nobody in the administration of the underworld ever audits
an Olympian, and which explains, incidentally, several very old discrepancies in Charon's
manifests that Charon has been complaining about for as long as I have known him.

From the plateau it is not frightening, and I did not expect that. There is no shouting and
there are no lamps. What is visible from a mile off is a change in the grain of a face that is
not supposed to have anything on it — a slow alteration in the way the dark sits — and it takes
me a while to work out that I am looking at people. What is audible is stone.

Ares is at the bottom. He does not go up. He stands at the foot of the west face with two men
and a lamp, looking at a watch, and he is there for the whole of it. I had assumed for four
thousand years that the god of war would be somewhere near the front of a thing, being
enormous. He is at the bottom of a mountain with a lamp, on time.

They are extremely good. They come up the west face in the dark, and they take the terrace, and
they take the house to the second floor, and Poseidon takes the Meander itself and stands in
it, which is the correct move, which is the move that should have finished it, and for about
ten minutes on a Tuesday morning Zeus is standing in his own garden with his wife's son's army
between him and everything he owns.

I am on the plateau. I have a view of the west face and no view at all of the garden. I want to
be exact about the limits of it: I heard the ten minutes. I did not see them.

---

Hera comes up the road.

Not the west face. The road, on her own feet, about twenty minutes behind the first of them, in
the way a person arrives somewhere they are not worried about getting into.

The next part I have from a ball boy, in a corridor, months afterwards, unasked, and it is the
only thing anybody has ever volunteered to me about that morning.

She goes in through her own front door and she goes up to the west room.

Not the long room. Not the terrace, where her son's men are. Not the garden, where her husband
is. The west room, which is the room with the shelf in it: the bottles that went out every week
by hand and came back, Hades's with the note in it, Poseidon's without one, and hers at the far
end, uncollected, in date order, with the dates written on, undusted.

The boy says she stood in front of it for a while. He says he cannot say how long because he
was trying to get out of the room and could not think of a way to do it. He says she did not
touch any of them and was not, as far as he could tell, upset.

Then she goes back down and out, and does not enter another room in that building, and by the
time it is over she is on the road again.

I have nothing to say about this. I have not been able to make it into anything, and I am
setting it down in the condition in which it reached me.

---

Then it stops.

Here is what I can report. It does not stop with a battle. There is no answering force, because
there is no answering force to have — the five who came back have been in the house since the
autumn doing nothing in particular and none of them is a soldier, and Athena, who would have
been, is not speaking to anybody at all.

The garden I have from one of Ares's men.

That is a sentence I could not have written a year ago and I would like it left where it is.
His name is Nikanor, he has been dead since before Krete had a president, he was thirty feet
from the whole of it, and he told it to me in a canteen in the winter, patiently, twice,
because I kept asking him to go back to the beginning.

He says Zeus comes out of the house at about ten past four in a dressing gown.

He says nobody does anything, because nobody has been told to do anything about him
specifically — the instruction was the house and the Meander — so nine hundred men stand on a
terrace and in a garden and hold where they are while the king of the gods walks between them
to the edge of his own pool and looks at the water in it.

And then, he says, Zeus looks up at the west face, where they are still coming, and says, to
nobody in particular, loudly:

"Is he still angry about the car?"

Nikanor did not know what car. Nobody knew what car. He says the man next to him took it for a
joke and it was not a joke; it was a question, asked in the tone of a man who has been
telephoned about something he had understood to be settled.

It stops because Zeus goes down into the Meander with Poseidon standing in it, and puts a hand
in the water, and does the thing he learned on his father four thousand years ago, and does not
finish it.

That is the report, and I have had it from three people, and they agree on everything except
how long it took. Hermes says no time at all. Nikanor says the better part of a minute.
Poseidon has said four seconds, twice, in my hearing, and has since said fifteen.

He does not kill his brother. He takes hold of the soul and stops, with his hand in the water,
and holds it there, and lets Poseidon feel exactly how far in it has gone and precisely how
little effort is being spent, and then he lets go and helps him up and asks whether he wants a
towel.

And there is a towel.

That is the part that has stayed with me. Somebody had to go and find one — in a cupboard, in a
house that had been taken to the second floor, with strangers on the stairs — and carry it out
to the Meander and hand it to a god who was on his knees in it, in front of nine hundred men. A
ball boy did that. He did it because he was asked to, pleasantly, by name.

Nine hundred dead men are still on the terrace when this happens. They can all see it.

Nikanor's account of the withdrawal is that there was no order to withdraw. He says the order
came about twenty minutes later and that everybody had already known for twenty minutes, and
that this is the difference between an army that has been kept up and an army that has been
used.

The withdrawal is orderly and takes about an hour. Hera goes north with her son and most of the
army and does not look at the house on the way out. Poseidon goes with them, on his feet, under
his own power, with a towel.

The water comes back on in the afternoon and nobody mentions it.

---

I should put Artemis in here, since she is about to go out.

She came home in the autumn with the others, and unlike the others she came with a bag — a soft
one, the kind a person carries. She put it against the wall of the long room under the second
window on her first afternoon and it was still there in the spring, in the same place, and I
went past it something in the region of two hundred times.

I noticed it. I want that on the record, because noticing it is the whole of my contribution to
this chapter. I noticed that a god who had come home to her father's house had not unpacked,
and I filed it as somebody being slow about a thing, and got on with the day, every day, for
seven months.

---

They have the argument at noon in the long room, and the argument is about what to do next, and
it is a real argument, which is what nobody expects and what makes what follows what it is.

Apollo says the family is finished if this is what it now is, and he makes the case properly
and at length, and it is a good case: that a house which has to be defended is not a seat; that
Ares did not need to win this morning; that the demonstration was the whole object and it
worked; and that there is no version of the coming year in which they are not all standing in
this room again, in worse weather, having the same conversation with fewer people in it.

Hermes says something conciliatory about the boots. That is not frivolous, it is what he does.
The boots are the only part of the northern business that has ever been funny and he goes for
the funny part the way another man goes for a door handle, and it works for about four seconds,
which is what it is for.

Aphrodite, who has said nothing since she came back, says that the whole of it is because he
cannot be alone in a house. She says it without heat, does not develop it, and nobody answers
her. It is true, and it is the cruellest thing anybody says all morning.

Artemis says almost nothing. She is standing at the window with her back to the room, which is
where she has been standing since she came, and when Apollo puts it to her directly she says,
without turning round, that she would rather be in the north.

That is what she says. Seven words. She would rather be in the north.

She does not say it as a threat and she does not say it as a defection. She says it the way a
person answers a question about where they would like to sit.

Zeus is very reasonable about it. He asks her to say it again so that the others can hear it
properly. She says it again.

He crosses the room and he takes her face in both hands, the way you would to look at someone
you have not seen for a long time, and there is a moment there — everyone in the room reads it
as a moment, Hermes told me afterwards that he had begun to relax — where it is a father
holding his daughter's face.

Then he does it.

It takes about as long as it took on the terrace with Alpheus. Apollo is four feet away. He
does not move, and I want to be careful here because I have heard him judged for it since and I
was in the room and I do not judge him: there is nothing between the beginning and the end of
it in which a person could have moved.

She goes into him through his hands. He steps back and he is steady on his feet and slightly
out of breath, and he looks round the room at the four of them, and he says:

"Anybody else want to be in the north?"

---

Nobody answers.

Nobody answers for a very long time, and then Hermes says, "No," and that is the end of the
argument, and it is the last time anyone in that family raises the question of what to do next
in the hearing of the man it concerns.

There is nothing to clear up.

I have thought about that more than is reasonable. A person is killed in a room and something
is done about the room — that is the arrangement in every house in every country, and it is
most of what a household is for. Two ball boys came in at about half past twelve with a bucket,
because a bucket is what you bring, and stood in the doorway. There was a long table, and eight
chairs, and a soft bag against the wall under the second window, and nothing else at all.

One of them took the bag out. Nobody had told him to. I do not know what he did with it.

They eat at one. The four of them, and him. Zeus carves, and asks Apollo about a house he is
thinking of buying, and Apollo answers him — properly, in sentences, with detail about the roof
— and that is the thing out of the whole morning that I have not been able to put down since.
Not the terrace. Not the hands. A young god an hour after his twin was taken out of the world
in front of him, sitting at a table describing a roof to the man who did it, because the
alternative was to be in the north.

And he does not do it briefly. He finds things to say about that roof; he asks a question back;
he is interested in the guttering. Whatever it costs a person to do that, he does it for the
length of a lunch, at his father's table, at the age he is. At the end of it Zeus tells him he
has been very helpful.

By evening the ones who came back have all taken rooms in the east wing, which they had not
done before, and Hermes has been given something to do.

---

I was in the long room for the whole of it.

I came in behind Aphrodite at about ten to twelve and I stood by the door and I was there for
the argument and for the seven words and for what he did with his hands, and I was there when
they went in to eat, and I did not go in.

Nobody addressed me at any point. Not one of them. Not to greet me, not to send me out, not to
ask what I thought, which was for four thousand years the single most reliable event in that
house — there was never a difficult morning in all that time in which somebody did not
eventually turn round and ask me what I thought.

He did not ask. He was six feet from me when he did it and he did not look up.

I am not putting that here because it is the important thing about the morning. Artemis is the
important thing about the morning. I am putting it here because it is the only thing I have to
report about myself, and this account has been running for a long time now on the assumption
that there was something to report about myself, and I would rather set it down plainly than
keep arranging the furniture around the fact that on the worst morning since the curtain came
up I was a man standing by a door.

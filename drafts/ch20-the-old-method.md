# Chapter Twenty

## The Old Method

The Meander began to run thin on a Thursday, and I want to explain what that means before I
tell you what he did about it, because the mechanism matters and almost nobody has ever had
it described to them properly.

It does not run dry. That is the first thing. There is no moment at which a god goes to the
tap and finds nothing; the system was designed by a man who did not intend to ever
experience alarm. What happens is that it thins. The colour goes first — it is ordinarily a
thing you cannot look into, and it becomes a thing you can — and then the weight of it
changes in the glass, and then, last, the taste, which I am told goes from something like
iron to something like the water in a vase.

Eleven souls had gone back to the world at the start of the month. Since then, more. Not many
against the size of the reservoir — not a tenth of a tenth of it.

And he had a gauge. That is the part I have to put in and would rather not.

He had nine weeks of counting and a chart a ball boy had ruled up for him, and a slope, and a
figure at the bottom of it that I had watched him arrive at a month earlier and had
congratulated him on, warmly, because I had put the ledger in his hand myself. The chart said
years.

The taste came first.

That is what happened on that Thursday, and it is a great deal worse than a man noticing
something odd at breakfast, because a man who notices something odd can be told it is
nothing. A man watching a number he trusts get overtaken by his own tongue cannot be told
anything at all.

He noticed at breakfast. He put the glass down and he looked at it and he said, "Is this
off?"

---

So. Here is where we are, and here is what happens next, and I would like you to hold me to
it.

He has been frightened since the morning he found the line on his forehead, and everything
he has done since — the ball boys, the phases, the tacitas, his brother in the pool, the
Fates in the poolroom, the kitten, the rationing — is the behaviour of a frightened man
tidying. He now has, in his hand, the first physical evidence that the supply which sustains
his immortality is finite and moving in the wrong direction. There is a second line above
the first and a third running down towards the temple; he has been managing them with the
lighting in the west room.

He will be here within the hour.

He will not ask about the Meander, because he does not ask about the thing itself, ever. He
will find a reason to walk out to wherever I am, and he will sit down heavily, and he will
talk about somebody who has disappointed him for about ten minutes, and then he will turn
his face into the light and ask me whether there is another one.

And I will tell him that it is the light.

I have that ready. I have had it ready since the last time, and I have improved it since,
because the first time I said it flatly and it worked, and a thing that works flatly will
work better with a little architecture under it. I had a version about how a face changes in
the afternoons. It was good. I had used the word *foxed*, which is a word for what happens
to old paper and also a word for what happens to a man, and I had been waiting a fortnight
to put it in front of him.

That is what happens next.

---

What happened next was that he sent for Alpheus.

You will not know the name and there is no reason you should. He is — he was — the god of a
river in the south that stopped being a river a very long time ago. It silted, and then the
farmers took what was left of it for a channel, and by the time anyone thought to write
anything down there was a dry bed with a road running along the top of it and a bridge over
nothing. He kept the title. Nobody took it off him. He came up for the family occasions and
brought a cake, and stood slightly outside the group in photographs, and had, I think, some
sort of arrangement with the kitchens.

He arrived at about ten, delighted, in a jacket.

Zeus met him on the terrace and asked after his mother, who has also not existed for some
centuries, and Alpheus said she was very well, and Zeus said that was marvellous, that was
just marvellous, and put a hand on the back of his neck the way you do with a nephew, and
walked him to the edge where the ground goes down.

I was thirty feet away at the table with a glass in front of me.

He did it the way you would open something. There was no speech. That is the detail I keep
coming back to and I have decided to give it to you plainly rather than dress it: for a man
who cannot pour a drink without a preamble, he did not say one word while he did it, and it
took less time than it has taken you to read about it, and the only sound in it was the
jacket.

And then he stood there.

He stood at the edge with his back to me and his hands out slightly from his sides, and he
was breathing the way a man breathes coming up out of water, and I watched the whole of a
small obsolete god go into him through the palms of his hands, and I want to record that it
is not dramatic. There is no light. It looks like a man standing still in a garden.

Then he came back to the table and picked up the glass that had been off at breakfast and
drank the whole of it standing up, and said, "There we are."

---

He was calm.

I need to be careful about this word, so let me be exact. I do not mean he was pleased,
though he was, mildly, the way you are pleased when the man comes about the boiler and it
turns out to be nothing. I do not mean he was triumphant. Triumph I have seen and can
account for. Triumph is a mode of fear.

He was *calm*. He sat down at his own table in his own house and ate a peach and told me a
story about Hercules that I have heard a great many times, and told it badly, and did not care that
he told it badly, and looked out at his garden with the untroubled face of a man who has
remembered where he put something.

He did not turn into the light.

He did not ask me whether there was another one. He has asked me some version of that
question every week for four thousand years — through the chains, through the bird, through
every attitude he has ever taken toward me — and he did not ask it on Thursday, and he has
not asked it since, and the word *foxed* is still sitting where I left it.

---

Now, I said you should hold me to it, so let us be adults about the accounting.

I told you he would come and be frightened. He came and was not. I told you he would ask
about the line, and he asked about nothing, and had a peach. I put a prediction in front of
you an hour before the event and the event declined it, and I am not going to slide past
that, because I have spent this entire account asking you to take my word for what people are
going to do.

But I want to put two things next to it before you file it.

The first is that I was right about the mechanism and wrong only about the man's manners. The
supply *is* finite. It *is* moving. He has confirmed it himself, in the most expensive way
available, by going back to a method he abandoned four thousand years ago because it was
laborious and it upset him. A man who is content does not do that on a Thursday morning
before noon. The fear is in there. It has simply stopped coming to the surface where I can
see it and be useful about it, and I would rather it were where I could see it, and that is a
preference of mine and not a fact about him.

The second is the rule, which you have had from three separate mouths by now and which I am
not going to insult you by restating in full: without his belief, it cannot come to pass.

I am aware of the arithmetic there. I did it at the table while he was talking about
Hercules.

But belief of that kind does not leave a man. It goes quiet. It goes down into the floor of
him and waits, the way a bad knee waits, and the first cold morning it is there again exactly
as it was. He has had one good drink and a quiet Thursday. He has not stopped being the
person who spent four thousand years being frightened of his own father, and he will not, and
a plan is not undone by an afternoon.

That is what I said to myself at the table.

I have gone back over it since — the sentence, the shape of it, the way it arrived so neatly
and so soon — and I notice that it is the same sentence I made about a man at a desk who
would not look at me, in the same voice, at the same speed, and both times it let me get up
from the table and go on with the day.

---

He began telephoning his children again that week.

I have given you the other end of this already — five letters in a queen's own hand, and
nothing back. Five of the six answered him; the sixth was in the north, with the best reason
in the world for not saying so. They were in the house before the leaves were done, not
fetched and not apologised to. They came back to strength, which is the only address that
family has ever answered at.

---

The family occasion that month was smaller by one and nobody said anything.

That is the part I would keep, if you are only keeping one. Not the terrace, not the jacket.
The following Tuesday there were nine of them at a long table for a thing about the
harvest, and there had been ten, and it went the whole way through — the toasts, the
business, the interminable bit at the end where they ask each other about houses — and not
one of them said his name.

They knew. Every one of them knew by then; the story had gone round Olympus in a day and a
half the way everything does.

They ate the lamb.

He carved.

# Chapter Seven

## Technique

I am going to do something in this chapter that I have not done yet, which is to show you my
work.

Not the plan. You have had the plan, or as much of it as I have been giving you. I mean the
other thing — the thing I have actually been doing for four thousand years, in a chair, in a
garden, with a bird in the middle distance. I have been managing him.

I would like it on the record before we go any further that I am extremely good at it. It is
the only skill I have ever developed to the top of my capacity, it took me two millennia to
get right, and there is nobody else alive who can do it at all.

You are about to watch it work, and I want you watching properly, because it is going to
matter a great deal later how good I was at this.

---

The situation in the fourth week.

He had rationed them. One bottle each, delivered personally, none for his wife — you saw him
do it — and the effect of that was not obedience, because he had nobody left to be obedient at.
Poseidon was gone. Dionysus was gone. Hades and Persephone were not answering and had gone
back to work, which he took as sulking. Hera was in the north.

So there was a house with a pool in it and a peacock and about forty staff, and one god,
rationing four people who were not there.

He had them delivered anyway. That is the detail I would give you if I could only give you
one. Every week, on the day, four bottles went out from that house by hand: one to the sea,
one to the underworld, one to wherever Dionysus was understood to be, and one — and he was
absolutely rigid about this — held for his wife and not sent.

Hades's came back. It came back unopened with a note from Persephone that said *thank you, he
has what he needs*, which is nine words and is one of the great acts of violence of that year.

Poseidon's came back too, in the second week, without a note.

Dionysus's did not come back and was not opened either; it went south with a boy on a cart and
was eventually left in a room in a compound, and I am told it is still there.

By the fourth week there was a shelf.

He had put them in the west room on a shelf with the returned ones at one end and Hera's
uncollected ones at the other, in order, with the dates, and he did not refer to it and did not
allow it to be dusted, and he took people past it. That is the thing he did. If somebody came
to the house on business — a ball boy's supervisor, a man about the roof — he would find a
reason to go through the west room, and slow down slightly, and not say anything at all.

I have watched that man do a great many things over four thousand years and there is not one
of them I would rather not have seen than that.

He was drinking freely. That is the other thing about the fourth week and it is the number I
should have led with: he was on somewhere between three and four times what he had drunk in
the spring. I know because I counted, and I counted because it is my job to notice the things
he does not say.

Three to four times. And he had begun to say — not often, in passing, in the middle of other
sentences — that it tasted thin.

And there was the second line. It had arrived on the day he came off the terrace after the
business with the Fates and it had not gone, and by the fourth week it had company of its own,
a third, very fine, running down and out towards the temple. He knew. He had stopped asking me
about it, which is not the same as not knowing; it is the opposite.

---

On the Thursday of that week he told me he was going north to get her.

I want to give you the whole of what that meant, because at the time it took me about a second
and a half.

Hera had been in that compound twelve days. Ares had nine hundred men over a rise and had not
yet been asked for anything. There were five letters out and no answers back. Nothing was
ready — not the alliance that eventually came out of it, not the understanding between mother
and son, none of it — and the entire northern position at that point consisted of a queen with
two bags who had not yet finished counting.

If Zeus went north in the fourth week, one of two things happened. Either he found a walled
farm with his wife in it and burned it, which he was perfectly capable of and which ends the
book at chapter seven. Or he found the second valley.

I had, as I say, about a second and a half.

---

Here is how it is done.

**One: never take the premise off him.** He has been told no perhaps four times in four
thousand years and every one of them cost the person who did it. So you do not say *don't go*.
You say *yes* — and you say it early, and you say it with enthusiasm, because a man who
expects a fight and is handed agreement has to stop and re-plan, and while he is re-planning
he is yours.

I said it was about time. I said it warmly. He was so pleased that he described the room he
would put her in.

**Two: change the object, not the verb.** He wants to go and get her. Fine. Go and get her.
Get her *how*, though — because there are two ways for a man to bring his wife home, and only
one of them can be told afterwards as a story he comes out of well.

I asked him what he would say to her when he got there.

He did not know. He had not thought about it. He had thought about the door and about the
journey and about her face and he had not thought about the sentence, and I let him sit in
that for about a minute, and then I said the fatal thing, which is:

**Three: give him the version where he was right.**

I said: if you go north, you are a man who has been left, going to fetch what left him. That
is the story. It will be told in every hall in the world by the winter and it will be told
that way whatever happens when you get there, even if she comes back, *especially* if she
comes back.

But if she comes to you — if she comes up that road on her own feet and knocks — then you are
not a man who was left. You are a man who was proved right. And there is no version of that
which is not the better story.

He sat with it for about four minutes, which for him is a geological age, and then he said:
"She'll come."

And I said yes. She will.

And that was the north dealt with, in a garden, in under a quarter of an hour, without a raised
voice, and I have never in my life been better at anything.

---

**Four, and this is the part that actually matters: never leave him with nothing to do.**

That is the whole discipline. Everything Zeus has ever done that could not be undone, he has
done in an afternoon in which nobody had given him a task. The ball boys. The tacitas. The
bees. He is not a cruel man in the way people mean it; he is a man with enormous capability
and no interior, and an empty afternoon in him fills up with something.

So you always leave with a job in his hands.

I looked around that garden for something and I found it in about ten seconds and I was, I
remember, rather proud of the elegance of it.

I told him to count the Meander.

Not check it. *Count* it. I said that he had been running the largest holding in the universe
for four thousand years on an impression, that no king who took himself seriously would do
that, and that if he was going to be rationing his family — which was correct, which was
overdue — then he should be able to tell them to the drop what there was and what it was
doing, and that nobody had ever done it and nobody else could.

It is a perfect task. It is kingly. It requires nothing to be destroyed. It is *tedious*,
which is the real virtue, because it would take him weeks. And it kept him on that mountain
with a ledger in his hand while everything that needed to happen elsewhere happened.

He was delighted. He started that afternoon. He had a ball boy make him a chart.

---

It worked.

I want to be completely clear, because I am not going to get many of these and I intend to
enjoy this one: it worked exactly as designed and it worked for months.

He did not go north. He never went north. Hera got her season, and Ares got his mother, and
the letters went out and came back empty and she recalculated in her own time, and every piece
of that had the space it needed because of a quarter of an hour in a garden in the fourth
week.

And he stayed at home with his chart.

---

Now.

I have thought about how to put the next part and I have decided to put it plainly and then
stop, because there is a version of this chapter in which I get clever at the end and I do not
think I have earned it.

He counted the Meander for nine weeks.

He was thorough. He is not stupid — I do not think I have ever said that clearly enough in
this account, and it is a failure of mine, because a great deal of what happens later is
unintelligible if you have him down as a fool. He is not a fool. He is a man of very
considerable ability who has never once had to use it on anything that would answer back.

He counted it, and he charted it, and he came to me in the spring with a figure and a slope,
and told me what the slope meant, and he was correct.

And then he did what he did in the summer, on a terrace, to a small obsolete god in a jacket.

I gave him that. Not the killing — I want to be careful, I am not going to sit here and take
on a thing that was his. But the *knowing*. He would not have looked. He had gone four
thousand years without looking, because looking is what a man does when he is anxious about a
supply, and it had never once occurred to him that there could be a bottom to it.

I put a ledger in his hand to keep him in a garden for a season.

It was the best afternoon's work I have ever done.

# Chapter Thirty

## The Shallow End

The far end of the Nothing was not there on a Monday.

That's the sentence and I've had two years to find a better one and there isn't one, because
nothing happened. There was no collapse. Nobody heard anything. On the Sunday there was a
room with several hundred thousand people standing up in it and you could not see the end of
it, and on the Monday you could, and the end of it was about four hundred yards nearer than it
had been.

Whatever had been in the part that wasn't there is not anywhere.

I want to be careful, because this is the one place in the whole account where I'm reporting
something nobody understands, including the god who built the thing it happened in. They are
not dead. They were already dead. They are not in the river and they are not in Asphodel and
they did not go through anything, and Hades stood at that end for most of a day with a lamp
and came back and said he could not tell me whether they had been destroyed or moved and that
he was not going to guess in front of people.

Four days later it went again. Not as much. Enough.

---

He'd been saying it for a year and a half.

I want that on the record because it comes up and people get it wrong. He told his brother, to
his face, in front of the whole family, before I'd been dead a week: that the Nothing was over
capacity, that it was why the Frame had glitched, that the souls in there were not being given
the time or the space to come apart, and that it would eventually break, and that when it did
it would not stop at the underworld — it would take Olympus and it would take the Earth.

And Zeus put him in a swimming pool and told him the Frame stayed as it was and the Nothing
was his job.

So the man had been right for four thousand years about a thing he built and had been made to
live inside being right about it, and when the far end finally went he was the only person
down there who wasn't surprised, and he was not any use at all that week, and I don't hold it
against him. There is a particular way of being useless that comes from having said a thing
so many times that you have nothing left to say when it happens.

Persephone was useful. Persephone was extremely useful, and she is the reason the next part
happened in four days instead of four weeks.

---

The channel was at seventy-one feet of ninety.

That's the number I have from the boy who kept the sheet, and I have it because I asked, and I
asked because I have learned that a number in that building is the only thing nobody will
argue with.

Seventy-one, in a working of about five feet a month, with dead crews on forty-minute reliefs
because of the air, and hand tools only, because nobody knows what the Nothing is made of and
nobody was going to find out by putting a charge next to it.

Daedalus had said two years if nothing went wrong. He'd said it in a wet corridor with one
hand, to a table of people who wanted a different answer, and he had been asked whether there
was an eight-month version and had said there were several and that all of them ended with
ninety feet of rock on a working party or the river somewhere it could not be got back from.

Persephone put it to him again on the Thursday.

She did not ask for the eight-month version. She asked him which of the several was the least
bad, and she asked it standing in front of him rather than sending somebody, which I know
because I was in the corridor.

I nearly wrote that last sentence as *Riddy's account has her asking it in front of him*, which
is his construction, about me, and I have caught myself doing it perhaps four times this month
and I don't like it at all.

Daedalus took a day and came back and said: the last
nineteen feet, at speed, and lose the stepping at the far end, and go in at a worse fall than
he wanted.

He said it would probably come down.

She asked him where. He said the third bend, and that he'd been unhappy about the third bend
since the spring, and that he could tell them where and not when.

She said do it, and I want to be exact about the next bit because it is the only time I have
ever seen that woman look for somebody's face. She looked at Hades. He didn't say anything.
And she wrote it down, and that was the decision, made in a corridor in about a minute and a
half by a woman with a pencil, which is how every decision in this story has been made and I
have stopped finding it strange.

---

It came down on the ninth day at the third bend.

There were three men under it. Aristeas, Kimon, and a man called Bakkhos who had been down
there since the beginning and who had been in the argument about the lamps.

They are dead. That is the thing about the underworld that I cannot make anybody up here
understand and I have given up trying to at parties. Nothing happened to them. They were not
killed and they were not hurt and they are not gone. There are three men under about forty
feet of fallen rock two hundred feet below Asphodel, and they are conscious, and they will
still be conscious in four hundred years if nobody gets them out.

It took nineteen days to get two of them out.

Bakkhos is still there.

They are working on it. Daedalus has a plan for it that involves coming at it from a different
side and it is on the list, and Prue has him on a sheet with a date, and every few months
somebody goes and knocks so that he knows the knocking is still happening. I asked whether he
can hear it. Nobody knows.

I've been asked whether it was worth it and I'm not going to answer that, because the person
asking is always somebody who has decided in advance and wants me to make them feel better
about it.

---

The water went in on the twelfth day and nobody let it in.

That's the part people never believe and it's the part Daedalus had been telling them since he
drew it on the back of four sheets of accounts paper. You do not open a channel. You cut it to
a fall and at some point the rock between the river and the hole gets thin enough and the
river finds out, and after that it is going in, and there is no arrangement of men or gods or
paperwork that stops a river once it has been told where the low ground is.

They were still clearing the third bend when it started.

It did not arrive as a flood. It came through at the far end as a wet patch and then a run and
then a foot of water moving slowly, and it went into that room the way water goes into
anything, which is by finding out where the floor is.

And it kept going in, and by the second week the floor of the Nothing was the shallow end of
the river.

I have been in there once, since, and I will do it properly somewhere else, and what I will
say here is that it is knee-deep and slow and it is exactly the same water they had been doing
all of it in at the Lethe since the autumn, and it is not dramatic, and the light is the same
awful nothing it always was, and there are several hundred thousand people standing up in it.

---

Now the thing Caeneus said in a room the previous spring, which nobody understood at the time
and which I have quoted at people ever since.

They had all been solving the wrong problem. Everybody in that argument — Hades, Persephone,
Medusa, me — had been working on how fast souls could be brought out to the people waiting for
them, because that was the bottleneck and the bottleneck was a god with a bad back going in
and out of the worst place in the world four times a day until he had to sit down.

Daedalus's channel ended that. Nobody retrieves anybody now. The water goes to them.

Which means, as Caeneus said, quietly, at the end of a presentation, without any edge on it at
all: *then that's the number. Not the rock.*

The number is how many living — I mean living in the sense that anybody down there is living —
how many people can be asked to walk into the worst place that has ever existed, in the dark,
past several hundred thousand standing figures, and stand in cold water in front of one of
them and say their own name and then a dead person's name and then something particular, out
loud, until a direction appears.

And then walk back out and go to work in the morning.

He'd been asked how many he thought would do it and he said he had no idea and that he was
probably the wrong person to ask, and that the thing he did know was that the first forty
would be the hardest, and after that it would either stop entirely or it would not stop at all.

---

It was not forty.

Prue had the list ready because Prue always has the list ready. She had been working on it
since the summer and she had it by sector and she had it in an order, and her plan — a good
plan, a careful one, the plan of a woman who has been organising things for sixty-one years —
was to start with about forty, on the Tuesday, and see.

Four hundred and some came on the Tuesday.

They came because of the dock. That's the whole of it and I've thought about it for two years
and I cannot improve on it. Two hundred thousand people had spent twenty-three days in the
autumn standing still in a place where standing still was the entire strategy, and had watched
a forty-one-year man go into the Frame off the edge of a dock and had not dispersed, and had
found out — most of them for the first time in a century of being told to wait — that they
were the ones who did everything and that nothing at all happened when they stopped.

You do not un-learn that. They came off the back of it knowing exactly what they were, and
when somebody put a notice up asking for people to go into the Nothing and say a name, four
hundred of them turned up with their sleeves rolled down against the cold, and stood in a queue
outside the worst place in the world, in a corridor, with a steel door standing open at the end
of it that had taken nine days to fit and had nobody sitting in front of it any more.

The first one in was a woman from the fourth sector called Melite, who had been down there
ninety-odd years waiting for a brother, and who went in at about nine in the morning with her
shoes in her hand because the boy on the door had told everybody the floor was uneven and she
had taken it seriously.

She was in there forty minutes. She came out and sat on the step and put her shoes back on and
somebody gave her a cup of the tea that tastes of nothing, and she said it was fine, and that
the hard part was not the walk in and was not the people and was that you have to say the
particular thing out loud and there is no way of doing that quietly.

Then she asked whether she could go back in on the Thursday for somebody else's.


Prue read the list out and then stopped reading the list out.

I asked her afterwards how she'd managed it and she said she hadn't managed anything, and that
it was like the urn, and that once people have decided a thing needs doing your whole job is to
stop getting in their way and make sure there's tea.

It did not stop.

That's the answer to the question he asked. It did not stop entirely and it did not stop at
all, and it has not stopped since, and it is still going on this evening while I write this,
in shifts, off a clipboard, in forty-minute reliefs, because the air is bad and the water is
cold and there is a rota.

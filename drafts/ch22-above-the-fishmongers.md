# Chapter Twenty-Two

## Above the Fishmonger's

I had been told there were people.

I want to be accurate about the size of what I knew, because I have spent this account being
accurate about the size of what I knew and it has generally been larger than other people's.
Not this time. What I had was a sentence from Riddy, three weeks old, delivered while she was
doing something else: *there are others.* I had taken it to mean sympathisers. The word I used
in my own head was *sympathisers*, and I would ask you to hold on to that word, because I am
going to come back to it and when I do you will see it for what it was.

She said I could come if I wanted. That was the phrasing. If I wanted.

---

It is above a fishmonger's on the harbour road in Heraklion.

You go through a side door that is propped with a crate, and up a staircase that turns twice
and is too narrow for two people, and the smell comes up the stairs with you the whole way and
is in the room when you get there and is presumably in everything anybody in that room owns.
There is a hand-lettered card by the street door advertising a room for hire by the hour, which
is not a cover. It is genuinely for hire. Two of the evenings in the week it is a choir.

I had, I discover, expected somewhere.

Not a palace. I am not a fool and I had not built a palace in my head. But I had expected — a
cellar, perhaps. Something with an aspect of the clandestine about it, a sense of a thing being
*held*, and instead there were chairs stacked against the wall and a tea urn with a handwritten
note about the tap, and a woman in an apron putting out cups before anybody else arrived
because she always does it and it has stopped being a favour and become simply what she does.

Her name is Ekhi. She runs the shop below and she has run the room above it for twenty-two
years, and she is the chair, and Cassandra defers to her in the way you defer to someone whose
house it is.

---

There were fourteen of them.

I counted. I counted twice, once from the door and once when they had all sat, which is a thing
I have done in rooms for four thousand years and which has never before produced a result I
could not read. Fourteen people between about nineteen and about eighty. A man with a bad chest
who had come in from a village and would be going back on the last bus. Two sisters who did not
sit together. A boy who took the notes and had clearly been taking the notes for some time. A
woman who said nothing at all for two hours and then said one thing.

I could not place a single one of them.

That is not a figure of speech and it is the thing I would put in front of you if I could put
only one thing. I have known the significant people of your world on sight for four thousand
years — not by cleverness, by *inventory*, because there have never been very many and I have
had nothing else to do. I looked around that room from a chair by the urn and I did not know
one face, one name, one grandmother, one thread. Fourteen people had been doing something
consequential for the whole of my imprisonment and I had a word for them and the word was
*sympathisers*.

---

What they do is keep prophecies.

Not interpret. That is the first thing Ekhi said to Riddy and she said it the way you explain
the tap on the urn: we do not interpret, we keep. A prophecy is written for one person and
belongs to them and is nearly always misread by them, and the room's four hundred years of
experience is that the misreading is where the damage lives — so the work is to hold the words
exactly, uncorrupted, and to put them in front of the right person at the right hour, and then
to shut up.

They hold them in two forms. There is a book, which is not one book, it is a shelf of them in a
cupboard behind the chairs, and there is memory, because books burn. Every prophecy in the
cupboard is also in at least three heads in that room, and the boy with the notes had nine of
them by heart and was working on a tenth, and was tested on a Thursday.

Four hundred years of continuous operation. I asked, because by then I had begun to need to
know, and Ekhi went into the cupboard and came out with the oldest thing they have, which is a
scrap in a hand nobody in the room can read any more, and the room's own tradition is that it
predates Troy.

Predates Troy.

I was still free when Troy fell. I was free and in love and living on the coast and I had not
yet had a knock at my door, and while I was doing that, somewhere, some of these people's
great-great-grandmothers were already sitting in a room somewhere else keeping a list.

---

Now, I am going to place a question here and go somewhere else with it for a moment, and then
come back to it, and I want to say plainly this time that that is what I am doing.

I have done it to you perhaps two dozen times. It works. It has worked on you every time and
you have never once caught me at it, and I have been rather proud of that, and I find I no
longer want it undisclosed. So: the question is what they wanted me there for.

Somewhere else, then.

Riddy sat in that room for two hours and was not, for the first time since Cassandra handed her
the job on a road outside a bin that is welded shut now, the only one holding it. That is what
I had gone to watch without knowing it. They took what she said, wrote it down,
cross-referenced it against three things in the cupboard, corrected her on a detail of the
Frame — corrected *her*, who has stood in the Nothing — and asked her to come back on the
Tuesday.

She was completely undone by it. She hid it extremely badly. At one point she went and stood by
the window with her back to the room in a manner I recognised from somewhere and could not
place until much later.

And about ninety minutes in, the man with the bad chest said to the boy, across the room, about
a woman in a village whose name I did not catch and whose prophecy the boy had by heart: "Not
yet. Tell her not yet. Today's not the day."

He said it the way you'd say a bus time.

Riddy put her cup down.

Because she has heard that sentence twice in her life. Once from a shoplifter outside a
supermarket who told her she would leave everyone, and once, secondhand, from a man she loved
in the underworld describing his mother walking into a river ten years late, saying *today's
the day*, saying *it has to be today*.

It is not mysticism. It never was. It is a phrase used by people who work together, in the way
that people who work together always end up with a phrase.

---

The cupboard has an entry for Caeneus's mother.

It is not long. It gives her name, which I am not going to put here. Not because he has lost it
— he has it, and says it easily, and it is one of about four things of hers he still has.
Because the order in which he finds things out has stopped being mine to arrange. It gives the
prophecy she carried for her child, in full, in her own hand. It gives the date she was told
the boy would have to die, and by whom, and it gives — in a different hand, added later — the
date she was told to wait, and the length of the wait, and a single line confirming she had
accepted it.

She was one of them. She had been one of them since before he was born.

The betrayal was not a mother's failure of nerve on a bad night. It was work. It was assigned,
and recorded, and she did it, and then she shot herself and went down and stood at a port for
ten years keeping an appointment.

Riddy read it twice and then closed it and put it back on the shelf herself, and asked Ekhi
whether anyone had ever told him, and Ekhi said no, and Riddy said, "Right," and that was the
whole of the conversation, and I have thought about that "right" a great deal since.

---

Back to the question.

They wanted my name.

Not my counsel and not my intentions — my name, for the book, because that is the procedure and
has been for four hundred years. I will come to that. First: Ekhi asked me two things in two
hours. The first was whether the god of death could be relied on, and she asked it briskly, as
a matter of operational fact, the way you would ask whether a supplier is sound. The second was
whether I would mind moving my chair, because the man with the bad chest needed to be nearer
the window.

Nobody asked what I thought should be done. Nobody asked about the plan — and I notice, writing
that, that I have written *the plan* four times this week and *their plan* once, and that the
once was in this room, and that nobody in it would have understood the distinction or found it
interesting.

At the end the boy came over and asked, politely, whether I would consent to being written
down. Not my counsel. Not my intentions. My *name*, and the date, and the fact that I had been
in the room, because that is the procedure and it has been the procedure for four hundred
years, and they have a cupboard, and I am now in it.

I said yes.

I sat in a chair by a tea urn above a fishmonger's while a boy of about nineteen wrote my name
in a book with a number of other names in it, most of them of people I have never heard of, all
of whom were doing this before I was, and I understood — not gradually — that the thing I have
spent four thousand years being is *one of the sources*.

The room stood down at nine, which is when it stands down. The man with the bad chest got his
bus.

# Chapter Twenty-Eight

## The One Who Has to Talk

Right. Me, then.

I'm not going to do the thing he did. The bit at the start where you get told what sort of
chapter it's going to be, and how much it's going to cost him to tell you, and how brave he
is being about it. I sat through a lot of that. Some of it was even true.

Here's where we are. I'll do it fast because none of it is interesting, it's just necessary,
and I've noticed that when people make the necessary part interesting it's usually because
they want something.

---

Cassandra says I'm a prophet.

I've asked her four times what that's supposed to mean and I've had four different answers,
which she says is because it means four different things, and I say that's not an answer,
and she says no, it isn't. She's not being difficult. She's just been doing this for a very
long time and she's stopped expecting the conversation to go anywhere.

As far as I can work out, it doesn't come with visions. I don't get pictures. I don't wake
up knowing things. What I've got is worse and stupider than that: I know one enormous true
thing, and I can't put it into another person's head. That's the whole gift. That's it.
That's what she handed me on the road outside the Cave like she was giving me a parcel.

I've had a year of it now. I've said it in temples. I've said it in the square in Heraklion
with a crate under me and eleven people listening, four of whom were waiting for the crate.
I've said it to priests. I've said it to my own aunt.

They don't disbelieve you the way you'd think. That's what nobody tells you. They don't
argue. Arguing would be something. They *agree* with you and it doesn't go in. It's like
pouring water on a table.

I'll give you two and then I'll stop.

The priest was in Kydonia and he cried, properly, and had to sit down to do it. He asked me
questions for an hour and they were better questions than mine. At the end of it he said he
had not heard anything in thirty years that he believed more, and he thanked me, and he
walked me to the door, and at six he did the evening service exactly the way he has always
done it. Coin and all. Two hundred people come on a Thursday and he is sixty-one and he has
a house.

I don't think he was a coward. I've had a year to decide that and it's the answer I keep
getting. A thing can go all the way in and still have nowhere to come out.

My aunt asked whether I'd been sleeping. Then she asked whether I'd seen anybody about it,
and then she gave me lunch, and then at the door she said that my mother would have known
what to do with me. Which is true, and is not about any of it, and I stood on her step for
a while afterwards.

So no, I don't know what a prophet is. I know what one does. One talks.

---

What's happened, quickly:

Zeus has put the Nothing behind a wall. Not a metaphor, an actual guard on an actual door,
which is the first honest thing that building has ever had on it. Caeneus can't get near
the river. That's eleven days now.

Zeus has also gone back to killing gods directly, which he hadn't needed to do in about four
thousand years, and which he is apparently enjoying, and which — this is the part that
matters and nobody wants to hear it — is *working*. He's steadier than he's been since I
died. When people tell you the man is falling apart, understand that he's falling apart the
way a fire falls apart.

Hera has troops and a son and a grievance and she is going to move, and when she does it
will not be for us, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

Ari has Krete, more or less. Daedalus is building a door that he says will take longer than
we have, and he is right, and he's building it anyway, which I've decided is the only
posture available to any of us.

Orpheus is singing it.

I want to be careful here because I'm told I'm not fair about him. He goes out in front of
four thousand people and he sings what happened to me — the water that wouldn't rise, the
place under the world, all of it, no metaphor, nothing softened — and they weep and they
buy it and they sing it back at him, and then they go home and put a coin in a drawer for
their mother.

Because it's a song. Because when he does it, it's beautiful, and when a thing is beautiful
you're allowed to feel it without doing anything about it.

I stood at the back of one of those. I don't recommend it.

---

And then there's the part that none of that is about, which is that there are two hundred
thousand of us down there.

I say us. I've been alive for a year and I still say it and I've stopped correcting myself.

Three of them you'll need. Prue has been dead sixty-one years and does the chairs and the lists
and the notices and the urn, and is not waiting for anybody, which she will tell you
cheerfully, and which took me about a year to hear properly. Adrian has been dead forty-one and
is the most boring man I have ever met and is the reason I got through my first week. Medusa
runs the divers, has no time for anyone, and is the only person down there who has been right
about all of it from the beginning and has said so to nobody.

They are not a resistance. Nobody recruited them and there is nothing to join. They work
the port and the turnstiles and the boats and the canteen and the laundry and the dogs, and
they have done it for as long as there has been a building, in exchange for two hundred
years at the end of which nothing happens.

Hold on to that. I didn't, and it was in front of me the entire time.

---

The other thing you should know about is the book.

Caeneus is paying for every soul he brings back, and what he pays in is what's already
happened to him. It goes backwards and it goes in order. Leos went first. Then the
compound, then the dog, then the crossing, then his mother — he lost her about a year
after we got her out, which if you have any sense of proportion at all is the single filthiest
joke this universe has managed, and it's managed some.

So there's a book. It's a ledger, actually, one of Charon's, he had crates of them.

I go down twice a week. Persephone takes me — she crosses the way other people get a bus,
and she has never once made me ask twice, and it is a favour and not a service and I am
aware of the difference every single time. Tuesdays and Fridays. She waits at the top of
the steps and reads something and does not ask me about any of it.

And I read him back to him.

It's not moving. Don't make it moving. It's admin. You do a page and a half or he gets
tired, and there's an order to it that works and an order that doesn't, and you learn to put
the bad ones in the middle because he can't take them at the end of a session. He listens to
it the way you'd listen to a story about a cousin. Sometimes he laughs in the right place,
which is the worst of it, because it means the shape is still in there even when the man
isn't.

He asks good questions. He asked me once whether he'd been kind. I said yes. He said, "Do
you think I'd have said that about myself?" and I said no, and he said, "Then it's probably
true," and wrote it in the front himself, in handwriting that's still his, which I looked at
for a while after he'd gone to sleep.

---

Now. You.

I'm aware there's somebody there. I've been aware of it for a bit, the way you're aware of a
draught.

I'm not going to work on you. That's the whole reason I'm the one doing this and not him,
and I'd like it on the record before we go any further. He *worked* on you. He picked what
you found out and when, he kept back the bits that made him look bad, he did that trick
where he tells you something's coming so you'll sit still — and I'm not saying he lied,
because I don't think he ever did, quite. I'm saying he arranged.

I've got nothing to arrange. There's nothing in it for me. Nobody has ever believed me and
I have stopped structuring my life around the possibility, so you can have the lot.

Starting with the end, since that's the one he'd have saved.

**We win.** Not cleanly and not for free, and one of us pays for it in a way that no one in
this story ever properly acknowledges, because by the time it's finished he can't follow the
acknowledgement.

By the end of what I'm about to tell you, Caeneus will not know who I am.

He'll be kind about it. That's the thing I've had to make my peace with in advance, and I
haven't, and I'm going to tell it to you anyway in the order it happened, and when we get
there I'm not going to make a speech about it. I want you to know now so that you're not
waiting for one.

That's what I've got instead of craft. Everything, immediately, in the order it happened.

You'll believe it or you won't. Genuinely, either is fine. You'd be the first, is all.

---

One more thing and then we'll go.

There's a flat above a chandler's on the east side with a balcony about the size of a
door — I've got it because Ari's people found it for me and I let them, which I'd have died
before doing a year ago.

There's nothing on it worth looking at. Pots. Some thyme that's doing well and a lemon thing
that isn't.

And in the evening, when it's still warm, I go out and I water them.

It's in the book — it's on page one of the book, it was the first thing he ever told me he
missed, before I knew him well enough to know what it cost him to say it, a balcony in a
city he'd been thrown out of, because nothing ever grew in the place he was born.

He can't remember telling me.

So I water them at evening, when it's warm, so that when I read him page one I can tell him
what it smells like. He likes that part. He always asks me to do that part again.

Right.

Eleven days he's been off the river. Here's what we did about it.

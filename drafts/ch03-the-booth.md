# Chapter Three

## The Booth

They buried Minos on the fourth day and they did it beautifully, and I have never seen a city
make less sense to itself.

Understand the difficulty. The doctrine of that place — stated on the steps of that palace
every Olympia for as long as there has been an Olympia, stated by the dead man himself to his
daughter four days before she killed him — is that **the gods choose who rules**. It is not a
sentiment. It is the constitutional position, and everything else in Krete rests on it, and it
is the reason there has never been a vote.

So: a president is stabbed to death in his own labyrinth by his own heir, and the heir walks
up and takes the chair, and now you must go out and say something to two hundred thousand
people.

If the gods chose him, they have now unchosen him, and by what.

If the gods chose *her*, then the gods chose a patricide, and every child in that city is
taught that Renewal is earned by obedience.

And if the gods did not choose either of them, then somebody had better take down the
monument.

What the priesthood came up with, after four days of what I am told were extremely bad-
tempered meetings, was that the president had been *called*. That is the word. Called, in the
night, by Olympus, in a manner not given to men to understand, and his passing was a summons
and not a death, and his daughter had been placed in her position by the same hand that
summoned him.

It is not a good answer. Nobody in that square thought it was a good answer. But it has the
enormous advantage of requiring nothing to change, which is the only quality anybody was
shopping for that week.

---

They gave him the full rite.

Coin, priest, the whole of it — *Hades, Lord of Death, we offer you this mortal; accept his
coin and grant him safe passage through the Frame; bless his glorious transition, the Renewal
of all human souls.* Two hundred thousand people said *vero* and meant it, and a choir did the
thing the choir does, and his wife stood at the front in black and did not once look at the
box.

There was exactly one person in that square who knew where he was actually going.

She was about two-thirds of the way back on the north side, in a borrowed coat, and I was
about forty feet from her, and neither of us said anything to anybody.

Ari was thirty yards in front of her and up a flight of steps.

That is the closest the two of them came for another five weeks, and I have thought about it
since as a piece of theatre I would have been proud of if I had arranged it, and I did not
arrange it, and it did not do anything.

Ari read the words she had been given. She read them properly, in a black coat, in a voice
that carried, and she did not add anything and did not look at her mother and did not look at
the box, and at the part about the glorious transition and the Renewal of all human souls she
said it exactly as written and did not pause.

Riddy watched her say it and told me afterwards that she had spent the whole of that minute
trying to work out whether the woman on the steps believed a word of it, and had not been able
to tell, and had found that encouraging.

---

Riddy had been in Heraklion for six days by then and had achieved nothing whatsoever, and I
want to explain why.

I had put her back on Earth with the largest fact in the world in her possession and a
prophet's instruction to take it to the ruler of Krete, and the difficulty, obviously, was
going to be *access*. Getting a woman in a borrowed coat in front of a head of state.
I had a number of thoughts about how that might be managed and I was rather looking forward to
managing it.

The difficulty was not access.

The difficulty is that Eurydice of Heraklion is dead. She died in the street at a concert
given by the most famous man in the country, in front of several thousand people, four months
ago, and there was a funeral, and there was a song, and the song is still being sung in that
city every night of the week by people who cry at the chorus.

So a woman who looks like her cannot get anywhere near a fact.

---

She was recognised nine times in six days. She counted, because she is the sort of person
who counts, and I have the number from her.

Not one of them held.

That is the thing I have not been able to get out of my head since, and it is worth more than
anything else in this chapter, so I am going to give you the shape of it rather than a list.

Somebody sees her. They stop. There is a moment — you will have seen this moment on a person's
face — where the whole thing is there and available, and they say something like *sorry, has
anyone ever*, or *my god, you're the absolute image of*, and she says yes, it's me, I'm
Eurydice, I was dead and I came back, ask me anything.

And they laugh.

Not unkindly. Nobody was unkind to her all week. They laugh and they say *don't*, and one man
said "That's not funny, my sister loved her," and a woman gave her some money.

Because *dead* is the one thing everybody in that country has been trained on since birth. It
is the most thoroughly established fact in their possession, more established than the
government. And the thing about a very well-established fact is that a person standing in
front of you contradicting it does not damage the fact. It damages the person. She stopped
being a dead woman and started being a woman with a problem, in the length of time it takes to
laugh.

She said to me later that it would have been easier if one of them had called her a liar.
Nobody did. Nobody once did.

There was, of course, a way to fix the whole thing in an afternoon, and I put it to her on the
third day and she said no, and I put it to her again on the fifth.

Orpheus knows. Orpheus drove her out of the ground himself and stood on a road with her and
was thanked and left, and Orpheus is the most recognisable human being in that country. One
sentence from him in one public place and the recognition problem is over — not solved,
*over*, because a thing that man says about that woman is believed in Krete the way rain is
believed.

She would not have it.

I assumed it was pride, and said so, which she took better than I deserved. Then I assumed it
was tenderness — that she did not want to put a man who had walked through death for her in
the position of being useful — and I was so pleased with that reading that I offered it to her
out loud.

She said: "If he says it, it's his."

I did not understand and I asked her to explain and she declined to, which is the correct
response to me on most days. I understood it about ten months later, in a square, at the back,
watching four thousand people learn her life from a stage.

She was not protecting him and she was not being proud. She had done the arithmetic in
Heraklion in the first week and got the right answer, and I had a year to work out what she
meant and I only got there when it had already happened.

---

She went to the booth on the fifth day.

I did not follow her in. I want that noted because I followed her almost everywhere else that
week, and I stood outside that one, and I have never entirely worked out whether that was
decency or squeamishness.

Her mother was there. Still there. Same booth, same hours.

Hera left Olympus with two bags and did not take a single tacita, and everybody who noticed
that at all read it as an abandonment and therefore as an ending.

It is not an ending. It is only an abandonment.

The booths are still open. The women still sit in them, all day, every day, in every city;
the doors still open at the hour and the attendant still says *may speech to the wordless give
you comfort, may the goddess Hera cradle you in your mortal pain*; and people still come, in
numbers, and say the worst things they have ever done into the dark.

And there is now nobody at the other end.

Four thousand years those confessions went somewhere. They went to a woman on a mountain who
drank them like a cordial and used what was in them, which was monstrous, and which was at
least a use. They now go into a room, and stop, and the woman in the room cannot answer and
cannot repeat them and has no one to give them to, and the next person comes in.

Nobody has told them. Nobody is going to. There is no mechanism by which anybody *would*.

---

I got the inside of it from Riddy afterwards and she gave it to me flatly, in about ninety
seconds, and would not do it twice.

She sat down and did the form of it — the greeting, the *vero* — and then she said: it's me.
I'm alive. I'm actually alive, I'm not a vision, and I know that's not possible and I need you
to hold it anyway for two minutes.

And then she told her.

All of it. The coin and the water that would not rise and the two hundred years and the room
under the world with the people standing in it, and what happens at the end of the Frame, and
that there is no Renewal and has never been any, and that the man they had just buried with a
coin and a choir was at that moment somewhere she had personally stood.

She said it took about four minutes and that she did it badly.

Then she asked her mother to write, because they can write, and there was a pad, and she put
the pen into her hand.

And her mother wrote: *Did it hurt?*

---

That is the whole of it. She wrote three words about a truck and put the pen down and took her
daughter's hands.

Riddy said she sat there for a while and then said no, it was quick, which she says is
probably true and which she does not actually know, and then the hour ended, because the
booths run to an hour, and the attendant knocked.

I asked her — and I am going to put this down exactly as it happened, because I have had a
year to think about what it says about me — I asked her whether her mother had understood the
part about the Frame.

And she looked at me and said: "She understood all of it."

And I said something about how that was a start, and about the network of tacitas being an
extraordinary distribution problem if we could find a way to move information through it, and
that a woman who has been silent for twenty-five years is a person nobody watches.

She let me get to the end of it.

Then she said, "She wanted to know if it hurt," and went to get her coat.

---

I have been over that exchange more times than is useful.

At the time I filed it as her being tired, and I made a note about the tacitas — a good note,
a sound operational note, and I still think there is something in it and so, incidentally,
does Ekhi.

What I did not do, in the whole of that week, was consider that a woman had been given the
largest fact in the world by her own daughter and had not been able to use it for anything at
all, because there was something nearer.

She had understood every word of it. She had simply had a question of her own, and it had been
sitting in her for four months, and it got there first.

# Chapter Thirty-Nine

## The First Thing on the List

Krete voted in the summer and Ari lost.

I'll give you the number first, because a number is the only thing nobody argues with. A
hundred and sixty-one thousand people voted, which was every adult in Krete and about nine
thousand Trojans, and she lost it by four thousand two hundred to a man called Sostratos who
had run the water board for nine years and has never in his life said anything anybody wrote
down.

That is the whole event. It took a day. There was no trouble anywhere, and the priesthood —
who had spent four bad-tempered days after her father died inventing a doctrine in which
Olympus *called* him — stood on the steps of the palace and read out a number, which is the
single largest thing that has happened to that city in four thousand years and took about
forty seconds.

---

Nobody in Krete had ever run a vote and I do not think anybody has properly written down
what that involves, so I am going to, briefly, because it took four months and about six
hundred people and it is the only part of the whole business that anybody enjoyed.

There was no register. Why would there be. The constitutional position of that country for
four thousand years was that the gods choose who rules, which is not a sentiment, it is the
reason the machinery does not exist — no rolls, no boxes, no rule about who counts as an
adult, no rule about what you do when two people give the same name at the same table.

They had to build all of it from nothing in a summer.

And here is the part I have not been able to stop turning over. The one list Krete did have
was the Trojan registration — the thing Hecuba took apart in front of Ari's own chamberlain,
the system that follows a Trojan for life and that under a third of them were honest on,
because being honest on it got you moved. It was the most complete record of persons in the
country and it was complete because it was designed to be a leash.

They could not use it. Andromache would not have it in the building, and she was right, and
so the first act of the first vote in the history of that country was to destroy the only
document that could have made it easy.

What they used instead was the temple attendance lists, because there was nothing else, going
parish by parish with clerks and a boy with a box, and adding everybody who was missing off
the census and the harbour rolls and, in two villages in the south, off somebody's memory.

So the first free vote ever held in Krete was counted off a religious register by people who
had spent a year being told the religion was a machine for taking their souls, and not one
person on either side of that room mentioned it, and I have decided they were right not to.
It got the number. That was the job.

---

She lost because of the franchise. I want to be exact about how, because there are two
versions in circulation and one of them is comfortable.

The comfortable one is that Krete punished her for giving it away. That is true and it is
most of it. Half her administration had told her it would end her when she agreed to it in
nine minutes across a table, and they were right, and being right about it has not made any
of them pleasant to be in a room with since.

The other one is Andromache's, and Andromache did the arithmetic afterwards because
Andromache always does the arithmetic, and she gave it to me without softening it.

A little over half the Trojan vote went to Ari. The rest went to Sostratos, or to nobody, or
to a man in the south nobody has heard of who stood on the harbour question. They did not
vote as a block, and she said that this was the correct outcome, and that anybody who had
expected otherwise had not given them a vote, they had bought one.

Hecuba's entire comment on the count was that she had waited twelve years to be able to
disappoint somebody in a polling station and that it had been worth the wait.

---

I asked Ari about it a few weeks after, which is the only period in which you could get a
straight sentence out of her, because by then she had nothing to be careful with.

She said she had spent thirty years being told what she was for, and a year and a half
refusing it, and that she was extremely good at refusing it — better than anyone had ever
been at it, she said, and she was not boasting, she was doing the accounts.

And that it had not occurred to her at any point that refusing and governing were two
different jobs, and that she had only ever practised one of them.

She also said Sostratos would be all right. She said he was not a bad man and was not
anybody's, which in that building is the whole qualification, and that he would keep about a
third of what she had started and let the rest go.

Then she said a third was more than her father left anybody, and asked whether I wanted the
rest of the wine.

---

They filled in the labyrinth in the autumn, and she was not the president when they did it.

Andromache put it on a list in a room in Heraklion two years ago — *then that is the first
thing on the list* — and somebody wrote it down, and the four hundred people who had been
living in the cells got housing on the east side in the spring, which was the condition, and
after that it was simply an item that had not been done yet.

Sostratos did it in his second month. I am told he was mildly surprised to find it there and
did not query it. That is the thing about a list.

Daedalus did the specification, because there was nobody else on Earth to ask. He is
seventy-something now and has one hand and most of the sight in one eye, and he sat down
with a boy holding his instruments and worked out how to destroy the most famous object in
the world properly.

The only thing he ever said to me about it was technical, and he said it the way he says
everything technical, which is the only way he speaks without apologising: that you cannot
pour concrete into a thing like that, because it finds the voids and does not fill them. It
had to go in lifts, from the bottom, over seven weeks. And the room with the button in it
had to come down first and separately, because otherwise you are sealing a working mechanism
into a solid block, and in four hundred years somebody will cut into it and find a switch.

It took seven weeks and there was no ceremony and nobody made a speech, and the square above
it is a square.

He asked, near the end, whether anybody wanted the mask. Nobody had thought about the mask.
It is in a drawer in Heraklion and as far as I know it is still there.

He did not mention his son and I did not ask.

---

Ari went down in the autumn, on the rota, with the shift that was on.

Nobody talked her into it. That is the one thing in the whole of this that nobody had to
talk anybody into, and I want it on the record because almost everything else in this account
took a committee.

Here is her difficulty. She had four minutes of him. She took a mask off a man in a corridor
and said the word *twin* and he said it back, and then her father came in with a knife. Four
minutes, out of thirty years of a waxwork with the nose wrong. You have to stand in that
water and say something particular, and she did not have anything particular, because they
never gave her any.

So she used somebody else's.

Daedalus kept nine words for thirty years. He said them in a room with the shutters half
closed on the day they brought him up, in order, to the only person who was ever going to
want them. She said her own name, and then his, and then she said the other eight.

It took about six minutes. Caeneus was standing where the water is deepest, doing the thing
he does, and did not know who either of them was.

Nobody thanked her. She got out and put her shoes on and had a cup of the tea that tastes of
nothing with a man from the fourth sector who did not know who she was either, and came up on
the crew boat, and that was that.

---

Dionysus was at the count.

He is still in the south and he is still doing the weather, which he has now been doing for
two years, which is longer than that man has ever done anything. There has not been a bad
harvest where his people are. There was a week last year when it rained on one side of a
valley and not the other and he has never once explained it to anybody.

When she lost, he was the only person in that building who was straightforwardly pleased to
see her afterwards, and he told her so, at length, in front of her staff.

He has never said the other thing and I do not think he is going to. I asked him once,
badly, and he said, "Nah," and then talked to me for twenty minutes about a well.

He is the last god on Earth doing anything at all for anybody. What he does is rain, on one
side of a valley, for farmers, and they have a name for him down there that is not his name,
and he has never corrected them.

---

Orpheus played Heraklion in the autumn and was believed.

I want to be careful with the word, because he has been believed for a year in the sense
that people cry and buy it and sing it back. That is not what happened in the autumn. What
happened in the autumn is smaller and it is the thing he actually wanted, and I was at the
back of it, which is where I am for all of them now.

About forty minutes in he had the order wrong. He put the water refusing to rise before the
coin was taken, which is backwards — the coin comes first, the coin is *why* — and he had
never had it wrong before and I have no idea what happened to him that night.

And a woman four rows from the front said, not loudly and not unkindly, "It was the coin
first."

And about two hundred people round her made a small noise of agreement, because they knew.
Because they have known for a year. Because it is not a revelation any more, it is a thing
that happened, and the room had the material and the room was better on it than he was.

He stopped. He said, "Yes — sorry, yes." And he went back four lines and did it in the right
order and nobody in that square thought anything of it at all.

That is what being believed is. It is not a room weeping at you. It is a room correcting you,
because the thing has stopped being yours.

He did not enjoy it. I watched him not enjoy it from the back of a square for the length of a
song, and he got through it, and he was very good, and afterwards he did the forty people and
the arms and the eyes shut.

He found me by the wall on the way out. He asked whether I was all right and he meant it —
he always means it, that has never been the problem with him — and I said I was, and he said
good, and then he told me the dates.

Sparta, then two in the north, then Athens in the spring, where they voted to hold him and
have now asked him back.

He has never asked. Two years, and he has never once asked me whether he could have it. I
have stopped waiting for it and I have not stopped noticing, and those turn out to be
different things and you can do both indefinitely.

---

One more, and it's small.

Fotis is alive.

I'm putting it down flatly because every single person I have told this to has assumed the
dog died, and it would be tidier and it isn't what happened. He is old. He is grey to the
shoulders and he does the port in the mornings only and sleeps under the desk in the
afternoons with his feet out, and Charon feeds him things he is not supposed to have and
gets annoyed if you mention it.

Caeneus walks past him most days.

He has asked whose it is twice that I know of, about a year apart, and both times somebody
told him, and both times he said "Ah, right," and was pleased about it, and went on with what
he was doing.

He is fond of him. He calls him *you* and *boy* and *mate*. He is very good with him, in the
way that he is very good with everybody now.

And the dog knows him.

That's the part I can't do anything with. Every morning, at the desk, in front of everybody,
with nobody watching for it and nobody making anything of it.

The dog gets up.

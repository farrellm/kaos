# Chapter Thirty-Two

## The Weather

I wasn't there for any of this and I want to say so once at the top rather than keep
apologising for it.

What I've got is his book, which by that winter was four volumes and a box, and Hera, who
told me a good deal of it eventually in pieces and not in order and never when I asked. Where
they disagree I've gone with her, because he was writing and she wasn't.

I'll say one thing about the book and then leave it alone. He is very bad at this part. He
gets four pages into the failure of the largest object in the world and you realise he is
describing a noise, and then you realise he is going to go on describing it, and it is
genuinely the only piece of writing that man ever did that I would keep exactly as it is.

---

Here's the mechanism, and it's short, and I'll do it myself because nobody in this story ever
said it out loud to anybody.

The Frame stopped taking people in the autumn.

Not because anybody stopped it. Adrian stood on a dock for six days telling arriving souls
the truth and then other people took over doing it, and something like a hundred and ten
thousand people came off those ferries and were told, on arrival, plainly, what was at the
other end. Almost none of them went in. There was no interdiction and no order and nothing to
countermand.

So for twenty-three days nothing went into the Frame, and then the Stoppage ended and it went
on being almost nothing, because you cannot un-tell a hundred and ten thousand people a thing
and the ones who came after that got told too.

And a river that has been running for four thousand years on a continuous supply is a river
with about a season in it.

Nobody upstairs connected the dock to the tap. That's the part that still gets me. They knew
there was a strike, and Hermes had sat in a corridor through the middle of it, and not one
of them ever put together that the men who were not being processed were the men the river
was made of. They thought it was a labour problem. It was the accounts.

---

The other thing to say is why him and not the rest of them.

They'd all been dry for a year. He rationed them in the summer — one bottle each, none for
his wife — and Hades sent his back with a note, and Poseidon's came back in the second week
without one, and the whole thing was supposed to be a leash and turned out to be a
diagnosis. A year off it and they were all fine. Thin, maybe. Bored. Fine.

Because they'd found the floor of what they actually were, and they'd been standing on it
since the summer, and it turns out there's a floor.

He hadn't. He was on three or four times what he'd been on in the spring and had been going up
all year, and there was no floor under him at all, because there was hardly anything down
there that wasn't the river. Four thousand years of taking, and what you get at the end of it
is a man who is mostly made of the thing he took.

So when it went, it went out of him and not out of them. That's the whole of it. The one who
took the most had the most to lose, which is not a moral, it's just what the arithmetic says.

---

The sound goes off on a Wednesday and does not come back.

There's a noise the Meander makes that you stop hearing after about a week and then go on not
hearing for four thousand years, and it had gone off once before, at four in the morning in
the spring, when Ares's men took it and Poseidon stood in it. It came back that afternoon and
nobody mentioned it.

This time it goes off in the middle of the day and the silence just continues.

He is at the long table when it happens and he is telling somebody the story about Hercules.

I've never heard it. Prometheus says it is about a wager and a wall and that it is not funny
and never was, and that he had it perhaps two hundred times over four thousand years, and
that on the last occasion it was going badly even by its own standards.

He stops in the middle of it.

Not dramatically. He stops the way you stop when somebody comes into the room behind you. And
he sits there for a moment and then he asks, perfectly pleasantly, whether somebody could go
and have a look at the river.

Somebody goes. Somebody comes back.

He doesn't ask again. That's the bit. He never asks about it again, not that week and not
ever, and he never finishes the story about Hercules, and he never tells it again either.

---

The weather goes first.

He went out the next morning and did the thing he had done every day of his life, which is a
small private thing with the sky that he did the way another man cracks his knuckles — not for
anybody, not for a festival, just because it was there and he was.

Nothing happened.

The book says he tried four times over the following week, at the same hour, on his own, and
that on the fourth occasion he stood out there for about twenty minutes afterwards, and then
came in and asked what there was for lunch.

Then he stopped trying, and Prometheus's note on it is the only clever thing in that whole
volume: that the stopping was worse than the failing, because a man who keeps trying still
thinks it's a fault in the world.

After the weather it was the travelling. He could be at the other end of the Earth in the
time it takes to decide to be, and then he could do it once a day, and then he could do it if
he sat down afterwards, and then he asked for a car.

---

The children came in over about ten days.

Not together. Nobody sent for them and nobody arranged it and there was no summons, because
there was nobody left in that house who could summon anything. Athena, Apollo and Aphrodite
were already there and had been since the autumn. Ares came down from the north. Hermes came
back from wherever he had gone after he folded his chair and walked out of the underworld,
which nobody has ever established and which he has never said.

That's five. Five is the number, and it's their number, not mine. That house has counted
itself in sixes for a very long time and it went down to five in the spring in a long room
with a bag against the wall, and nobody has ever said Artemis's name in it since.

---

Ares walked up the road on his own in the second week.

Nine hundred men over a rise in the north and he came down without one of them, on foot,
having last been at that house in the dark at four in the morning with the west face moving
behind him. He did not come round to the terrace. He came up the road and in at the front,
which is what he'd done in the spring, and I've decided that isn't a point he was making. It's
the way in.

His father was on the steps.

Hera has this from Ares and she gave it to me in about six words, and then I got the rest of
it out of the book, which for once has more.

Zeus was delighted to see him.

That's it. That's what happened. He came down the steps at a speed he could not really afford
by then and took hold of his son by both shoulders and told him he looked well, and asked
whether he'd eaten, and said that they'd put him in the east wing with the others and that
Athena had got the whole thing running like a — and then couldn't find the end of the
sentence and let go of it, which he did a lot by then.

He never mentioned the army. Not that day and not once in the whole of that winter, and Ares
was in that house for three months.

I want to be careful here because there are two readings and I don't know which is right.
Either he had decided that a thing which had not worked had therefore not happened, which is
a manoeuvre he'd been running on himself since before there were countries. Or he'd genuinely
put it away — the way you put away a row with somebody you've got sixty more years with —
because his son had come, and that was the file it went in.

Ares says he doesn't know either. Ares says he stood on the steps for a while working out
whether he was being handled, and concluded that he wasn't, and has since said that the
second one is worse and that he'd rather not be asked about it again.

What he did on the first afternoon was go round the house with the man about the roof.

He'd taken it in the spring — he knew which parts of it would hold people and which wouldn't,
because he'd had to, and it turns out that if you have assaulted a building you know more
about it than the people who live in it. So he went round it. He found four things wrong with
it and had two of them fixed by the end of the week, and one of them was a lintel over a door
in the staff corridor that had been going for a decade and would have come down on somebody.

Nobody asked him to do that and he didn't tell anybody he was doing it.

Dionysus was four hundred miles south with the Trojans.

Nobody sent for him. It isn't that they decided not to; the count was five and it had never
included him, and it never occurred to a single person in that building that there was a sixth
living child of that man to be told.

He found out from a song. Orpheus had the failure of the Meander inside a fortnight — I don't
know who told him and I've stopped trying to work out how anything gets to that man — and
Dionysus heard it from four rows back in a town whose name he can't remember, and left in the
middle, and I have that from him and I'm not going to do anything with it.

---

They came to watch.

I've thought about how to put that and there isn't a kinder way that's also true. Not one of
them lifted a hand against him. Not one of them helped him either. Ares had nine hundred men
over a rise in the north and he came down without them. Hermes, who is the god of getting
places, took nine days to get there.

They sat in that house through the worst winter any of them will ever have, and they watched
their father stop, and when it was over they went away again — except Athena, who stayed, and
Apollo, who stayed a while.

And I've been told twice now that I'm hard on them, by people who mean well, so let me be
exact about what I think. I don't judge them for not helping him. There was no helping him.
The river was gone and there was nothing to put in it and the only thing anybody could have
done was find him something to drink, and finding him something to drink is how Alpheus died
and how Artemis died.

What I'll say is that they came a long way to be in a room with a man they were afraid of, and
sat there while he came apart, and none of them went home first to get anything.

---

Athena spoke on about the fourth day.

She hadn't said a word to anybody since the spring. Not since her sister was killed — before
that; she was already not speaking at four in the morning on the day of the assault, hours
before any of it, and nobody has ever been able to tell me what started it. Not sulking. She
simply stopped, and it had been most of a year, and the household had built itself around it
the way a household does.

What she said was that the kitchen order needed doing because nobody had done it since the
autumn and there were forty staff in that building who were being fed out of what was there.

That's it. That's what she said. She said it to a man about the roof, in the corridor, in
passing, and then she went and did it, and then she did the next thing, and by the spring she
was running the whole house and nobody had appointed her and nobody thanked her.

Prometheus writes it up as significant. He has a paragraph about how it was her first
utterance in nine months and what it meant that the first thing she reached for was
administration.

It isn't significant. There were forty people in that house who hadn't been ordered for. Half
of what happens in this story happened because somebody looked around and noticed that a thing
needed doing and that they were the only one who had noticed.

---

I'll finish with the bit I can't get comfortable about, and then we'll go down to the water,
where the actual work was.

He was frightening until about the end of that winter.

Everybody wants him to have stopped being frightening on the Wednesday the sound went off,
because that would make a shape. He didn't. He had the old method and he had a house full of
gods and he was hungrier every week, and everyone in that building knew exactly what he was
and exactly what he could still do, and they stayed anyway, and they were right to be there
and they were right to be afraid.

Two of them found out how right in the winter.

The first was Poseidon and I'll do him properly when I get there.

The second was a ball boy nobody has a name for, in the second week, over nothing, and I only
know about it because there is a line in that book, in the middle of a page about something
else, that says *he shot a boy on the Thursday* and then goes straight on.

I've read the page four times looking for the rest of it.

That's all there is. One clause. Four thousand years of that man telling you what everything
meant and then, at the end, when he's stopped being able to arrange things, you get the
truest sentence in the whole account, which is the one where somebody dies in the middle of a
paragraph about something else and nobody says anything.

# Chapter Fourteen

## The Name of It

I have been waiting four thousand years for the room I am about to describe, and I would like
you to come into it with me in the correct frame of mind, so I am going to tell you what it
is before I tell you what it looked like.

This is the chapter in which the thing becomes real.

Everything before it — everything, the whole of the first part of this account, the girl in
the road and the coin and the boy at the port and the woman who waited ten years — all of that
was arrangement. Getting the right people into the right places. I have said so from the
beginning and you have taken my word for it, correctly, because it was true.

Arrangement is not a plan. A plan is a room in which people who can actually do things agree
on what is to be done, and I had never been in one. In four thousand years I have been in
perhaps two hundred rooms with Zeus in them, and not one of those was a plan; they were
weather. This was the first.

I knew it would happen that week. I did not know it would happen on a Tuesday in an office.

---

The office belongs to Persephone and is on the third floor of the administrative block above
the port, and it has a window onto nothing in particular, and a filing system that has been
in continuous use for longer than most nations. There is a photograph of Hades on the desk,
turned slightly away from the visitor's chair.

There were seven of us.

Hades, who had been into the Nothing three times that fortnight and looked it. Medusa, who
had come straight from a shift and did not sit. Charon, who came because Persephone asked him
to and who did not once, in two hours, look at the part of the room I was in. Caeneus. A woman
from the Lethe rota whose name is Ione and who is in this account exactly once, here, and who
said the most useful thing anybody said all afternoon. Myself.

That is six. I am aware.

---

They started where everyone starts, which is with what they have.

What they had was one event. Not a capability — an *event*, singular, three weeks old: a man
had gone into a river after his mother and she had come out of it, and nobody in that office
including the man could say why, and the god of death had been standing in the mud four feet
away and could not say either.

So the first hour was the obvious hour. Was it repeatable. Nobody knew. Was it him or was it
her or was it the water or was it the day. Nobody knew. Hades had four theories and had
crossed out one on the way up the stairs. Medusa asked whether they could simply try it, and
Hades said not yet, and Medusa asked when, and Hades said that was a fair question and did not
answer it.

And underneath that hour there was a second conversation that nobody was having out loud, and
it was this: *what is it for.*

Because you can hold a thing like that and mean four entirely different things by it. You can
protect it, which is Hades's instinct, and which means never using it and keeping the man
somewhere quiet. You can study it, which is also Hades's instinct and sits badly next to the
first. You can *spend* it — restore a few, the ones you can reach, a small mercy at the edge
of a very large crime, and take the comfort. Or you can take it to Zeus and trade it, which is
what a sensible administration does with an asset, and which was said out loud, once, by a
man in that room who is not a coward and who was working through the options the way you are
supposed to.

Four things. All defensible. And a room of tired, decent, frightened people going round them
for an hour and a half without anybody being able to say the fifth thing, because the fifth
thing was so large that saying it made you responsible for it.

---

I had a phrase ready.

Of course I did. I have had a phrase ready for this room since before there were rooms, and I
had refined it over a period that I am not going to state because it embarrasses me now, and
it was — I am going to write it down, and I would ask you to read it in the spirit in which
it was meant —

*the great restitution.*

That is what I called it. Out loud, in an office, to seven people, one of whom was holding a
mug. I said that what was in front of us was not a rescue and not a repair but the great
restitution, the returning of what had been taken across the whole span of it, and I said a
further sentence after that about the arc of the thing which I am not going to reproduce.

It went perfectly well. Nobody laughed. Hades said, "Yes," in the way he says it, which
commits him to nothing, and Medusa looked at me for slightly longer than was comfortable and
then looked back at Persephone, and the conversation continued from about where it had been
before I spoke.

I want to be fair: it is a good phrase. It is a genuinely good phrase and if you say it aloud
you will find it has a shape in the mouth.

It is also nine syllables of nothing. You cannot put a rota under it. You cannot tell a
handler at a turnstile to go and do the great restitution and expect them to know which end
of the river to stand at.

---

Ione said the useful thing at about the ninety-minute mark, and she said it as a complaint.

She said that whatever they decided, somebody was going to have to tell the unresolved,
because word had already gone round Asphodel that a dead woman had come back out of the
water, and there were fifteen hundred people down there who had heard it and were going to
start asking, and she was the one on the desk when they asked, and she would like to know
what she was allowed to say.

That is the whole of her contribution. She wanted a line for the desk.

And it changed the room, because it is very difficult to keep having a philosophical
conversation once somebody has pointed out that fifteen hundred people are already waiting for
the answer to it.

---

Persephone had not spoken for forty minutes.

I had noticed and had not thought about it, which is the difference between noticing and
paying attention, and I am going to leave that sentence there without ornament.

She put her cup down and said:

"We empty the Nothing."

Four words. A subject, a verb, an object, and no adjective anywhere near it.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Medusa said, "All of it?" and Persephone said, "All
of it," and Medusa said, "Right," and got out a notebook, which is the single most alarming
thing I have ever watched a person do.

---

And Hades did not object.

I want to give that its weight, because if you have been reading carelessly you might think
the sentence belongs to Persephone and the chapter ends there. It does not. The sentence was
hers. The decision was his, and he made it by keeping his mouth shut.

He built the Frame. He was told to and he did it, and he has said out loud in front of his
whole family that the dead do not come back, and he said it to a mortal who had crossed the
entire underworld to ask, and he refused him, and meant it. It is not a policy. It is the one
thing he has instead of a defence: that there are rules, and he keeps them, and the rules are
what stands between him and being his brother.

*We empty the Nothing* is the end of that. It is the whole of his working life declared a
crime and then declared reversible, which is worse, because a crime you cannot undo is a
tragedy and a crime you can undo is just a thing you have not undone yet.

He sat there and he did not say no.

Persephone did not look at him while he did it, which I have thought about since. She looked
at the window onto nothing in particular and she let the silence go on for as long as it
needed to, and when it had gone on long enough that it had become an answer, Medusa wrote the
date at the top of the page.

Caeneus said one thing in the whole meeting. He said, "How many are in there?"

And Hades said he did not know.

And Caeneus said, "All right," and that was the last anyone heard from him that afternoon.

---

They had a rota by six.

That is the part I would put in front of you if I could only keep one thing from that day.
Not the sentence. The rota. Within four hours of the largest decision taken in this story by
anybody, there was a sheet of paper with names on it and a column for the river and a column
for the Nothing, and an argument about whether Tuesdays worked for Ione, and Medusa saying
that if they were going to do this they would need somewhere to put people afterwards and had
anyone thought about that, and nobody had.

I stood by the window and watched a god, two dead women, a ferryman and a soul handler
convert the moral centre of the universe into an administrative problem in under four hours,
and I have never been more certain that it was going to work.

---

So. The plan.

My plan, which I have been building since before your species buried its dead in the ground
with anything in their hands, entered its operational phase on a Tuesday afternoon in an
office above a port, and I was there for all of it, and it is now a thing with a name and a
rota and a start date.

I am going to write the name here so that we both have it.

*We empty the Nothing.*

Four words, said by a woman who had not spoken for forty minutes, in a room I had been waiting
four thousand years to be in, while I was holding nine syllables that I had been polishing
since before the Trojans.

I did not think anything of it at the time. Why would I. It is the same objective. It is
precisely and exactly the same objective, phrased by somebody with less patience for the
music of a thing, and if you had asked me that evening whose plan it was I would have told
you without hesitating, and I would have been telling you what I believed.

I have gone back to that office since. Not literally; there is nothing to go back for. But I
have gone over the two hours a number of times, and there is one detail I keep arriving at
and cannot get past, and it is not the sentence and it is not Hades's silence.

It is that when Medusa opened the notebook and asked what to head the page, nobody looked at
me.

# Chapter Thirty-Eight

## The Manifest

There is a woman I put in there.

I've been carrying that for two years and I've never written it down, and I'm going to do it
now because this is the chapter where I go in, and because you should know before we get to
the door that I am not going in as a visitor.

I was never a diver. I want that clear, because it would be tidier if I had been and it isn't
true. I was coinless, I was two hundred years from a door that didn't exist, and I had no job
at all. What I had was a friend who was a diver, and on my third day dead I stood on the
platform at the Lethe next to him while he talked a stranger into the water.

That's the job. You talk to somebody who is frightened until they can bear it and then you
walk them in, and he was very good at it, and I watched him do it and thought he was kind.

That afternoon the two of us followed a boy's screaming through a doorway in a wall at the back
of the building and found a room, and she was standing in it.

And one of us said, *we just sent her through the Frame.*

I've never been sure which of us it was. I've decided it doesn't matter, on the grounds that
whoever said it was right, and that I was standing there, and that *we* is the correct word and
I have never once wanted a different one.

I have never known her name.

---

Right. The port, because everything in this chapter goes through it and it is not what it was.

There is no fare.

That's the headline and it took about a week to arrange and four thousand years to become
possible. The coin bought passage through the Frame, and there is nothing at the other end of
the Frame to buy, so there is nothing to charge for, so nobody is charged. The turnstiles are
open. The dogs still work the lines because the dogs are good at it and like it, but they are
not looking for anything now, and the handlers walk them because a dog that has worked for
nine years does not enjoy being retired.

Medusa runs it. Formally, on paper, with a title, which she finds funny and has said so.

And Hades does the arrivals.

Not a video. Him, on the dock, four times a day, in person, for as long as the boat takes to
empty.

I want to say what that is, because if you have been reading carefully you will already have
it and if you haven't it will go past. There is a recording of that man that every human being
who ever died has watched on the way over — the ferry, the bridge deck, the PA — and he is
gentle in it and he is reassuring in it and he tells them that death is the only certainty and
that their devotion is about to pay off and that everything is going to be okay.

He made that. He wrote it and he sat for it.

So now he stands on the dock, and the boat comes in every four hours, and he tells them that
there is no Renewal and never was, that it was a machine for taking souls and that he built
it, and that he was told to and did it, and that nothing is going to happen to them here and
nothing is going to be asked of them, and that somebody will find them a bed.

He does it in about ninety seconds and it is not a performance and it does not get easier. I
have watched him do it perhaps thirty times. He has never once said *I love you* as a preamble
to it, which anybody who knows him will understand is the whole measure of the thing.

Some of them shout at him. That's the part I'd give you. They arrive frightened and they get
told the truth by the god of death standing in the rain in a coat, and about one in twenty
shouts, and he takes it, and then he goes and does the next boat.

---

Up here, nothing has changed.

I want that on the record next to the rest of it, because it is the thing that most annoys the
people I tell this to.

They are still burying people with coins. Every day, in every city, the bearer is named and the
coin is placed and the priest asks Hades to accept it and bless the glorious transition and the
Renewal of all human souls, and two hundred thousand people say *vero* on the holy day and mean
it.

The coins arrive at the port in the pockets of the dead and there is nothing to do with them, so
there is a box. It is not a small box.

The booths are still open too. Same hours, every city, the attendant still saying the words
about the wordless and the goddess cradling you in your mortal pain, and people still going in
and saying the worst things they have ever done into the dark to a woman who cut her tongue out
for a queen who has stopped listening and lives in a house with a dry river in it.

There is no mechanism by which anybody would be told. That's all it is. Nobody is keeping it
going and nobody is stopping it, and the machinery of a religion that has nothing at the far end
of it takes rather longer to run down than you would think, and in the meantime it is completely
indistinguishable from the real thing.

---

So. The manifest.

I went to Charon in the spring and asked him whether he had the boats from my week.

He said of course he had the boats from my week. He said he has every boat since he started,
which is four thousand years of crates in a room behind the desk that nobody has ever asked to
see, and that if I had come to him about it a year earlier he would have had it out in a
morning, and that he was busy now, and that he would do it on Thursday.

He did it on Thursday.

It's a ledger. A mark for every fifty and a longer one for every hundred, and then the lists,
in his hand, with the day and the boat and the bearer where there was one. My name is in it. I
have seen my own name in Charon's handwriting with a line through the coin column and it is a
strange thing to look at over a cup of tea.

The woman went in on my third day. So she came in on my second, or the day before that, which
narrowed it to two boats and about four hundred names, and the four hundred narrowed to
seventeen who went into the water on that shift, and the seventeen narrowed to one when Caeneus —

no. When I asked Prue.

I keep doing that. Caeneus was on that shift and he was on the platform and he would have known
her in a second, and I have caught myself starting that sentence four or five times in the last
year and I let this one stand so you could watch me do it.

Prue was on that shift too. Prue remembers everybody.

Her name was Kleite. She was fifty-one, she came in on a Tuesday from a village about forty
miles east of Heraklion, and she had a coin because her sister-in-law had done it properly.

---

The next part is admin and it took four days and it is the best four days I have had since I
died.

Prue's list is not one list, it's about nine, and one of them is the one she started in the
autumn: everybody in Asphodel who has told anybody they are waiting for somebody, by sector,
by name, cross-referenced against the manifests when there's a match. It is written in
pencil. It has been wrong twice.

There was a man in the sixth sector called Bion who had been down there forty years.

He is her brother. Not sister-in-law's — hers, older, dead a long time before she was, of
something ordinary, and he had been at the port for the first four of those years and then
had stopped going, the way people do, and had not put his name on anything since the notice
went up because he had assumed the notice was for people who knew where their person was.

I went and found him myself. I am not going to make that grand: I went to the sixth sector
with a piece of paper and knocked on a door and a man in a cardigan answered it and I said I
think we've found your sister.

He said, "Kleite?"

And then he stood in the doorway for a while.

---

The Nothing has water in it now.

Knee-deep, moving slowly, the same water as the Lethe, because it is the Lethe — it comes
through ninety feet of rock at a fall of about one in forty and it goes in on its own and it
has not stopped since the winter. The floor of the worst place that has ever existed is the
shallow end of a river.

The light is the same. That's what nobody tells you and what I could not tell you either the
first time, which is why I said I'd do it properly somewhere else and this is somewhere else.
It is not dark in there. It is worse than dark. It is the light you get in a corridor at four
in the morning, and it does not come from anywhere, and it is on all of them.

There are several hundred thousand people standing up in it.

You walk in past the ones who are not yours. That's the part. Bion had his shoes in his hand
because everybody does, and I had a lamp I did not need, and we went in through the doorway in
the wall that Caeneus and I walked through by accident two years ago because a boy was
screaming, and there is a boy on the door now with a list and a chair who says good luck and
means it.

It took us about half an hour to find her, because we had a sector and a rough position from
the day and because Prue's people had started marking, which sounds monstrous and is the
kindest thing anybody has done in that room.

She was where she was. She has not moved in two years. Whatever is done to a person in that
place is done and then it stops, and they stand.

Bion stood in front of her in the water and said his own name, and then hers, and then
something about a wall behind a house and a thing that had happened to a bird, and he had to
go back and start again twice.

Somewhere in the fourth or fifth minute she turned.

Not physically. Nothing you could point at. I have now had this described to me by about forty
people and I have felt it once and I still cannot put it in a sentence, and the best anybody has
managed is the one I have already given you, about somebody in a crowd starting to walk towards
you.

And Caeneus, standing behind her in the water where it is deepest, doing nothing at all, took
hold of it and carried it.

She went in about four seconds. There was no light. She was there and then there was nobody in
the water and there was a sound like a room going quiet.

Bion said thank you to nobody in particular, and got out, and put his shoes back on.

---

Right. The word.

*A line appears, the order wanes, the family falls, and kaos reigns.* Four of us had that. Two
of us are dead and one of them doesn't remember having it, and the fourth was so frightened of
the last clause that he spent two years doing every single thing required to bring it about.

Here is what it turned out to mean, and it is not what he thought and it is not what I thought
either.

It does not mean disorder. There is more order down there now than there has ever been. There
is a rota. There are reliefs. There are nine lists in pencil and a woman who has been doing the
chairs for sixty-one years and a boy on a door with a chair. Nothing about that room is
disordered; it is the most carefully run thing I have ever stood in.

It means **order that nobody owns.**

That's the whole of it. For four thousand years every arrangement in the world had somebody
sitting at the top of it taking a percentage — the temples, the coins, the booths, the
festivals, the whole apparatus of being told to wait — and it all *worked*, and it worked
beautifully, and the working was the crime.

And the gods called the alternative chaos because from where they were standing that is
exactly what it looks like. An arrangement you are not skimming from is indistinguishable, from
the top, from no arrangement at all. Zeus looked down at a world that had stopped paying him
and could not see anything in it. He was not lying about that. He genuinely could not see it.

The fourth clause was never a catastrophe. It is a description of what a place looks like when
the people doing the work are also the people who decide what the work is for.

He spent two years being terrified of a rota.

---

Bion goes in most weeks now.

Not for Kleite. She's gone; she's a child somewhere on the north coast by now, on one of
Prue's other lists, and he knew that was the arrangement before he went in and did it anyway
and has never once mentioned it since.

He goes in for other people's. He's very good at it — he's calm, and he doesn't hurry them,
and he can stand in that water for two hours without getting strange about it, which about half
the escorts cannot.

I asked him why, on the way out, the second or third time.

He said somebody had come and knocked on his door.

# Chapter Thirty-Four

## In the Way

Poseidon died in the long room on an afternoon in the middle of winter and it took about four
seconds and there was nothing in it that anybody could have used.

I'm putting it there because that's where he'd have put it last, and because I said at the
start that you could have everything in the order it happened and I've been keeping to it.

Here's the rest.

---

He was starving.

That's the whole cause and I'd rather not dress it up into something with more moving parts.
The river had been dry a month. He was made of it more than any of them and there was nothing
to put in him, and he had a method — he'd had it since he was a mortal boy who hated his father
— and the method needs a soul, and the only souls in that house were gods and staff.

He'd already used it twice that year. A minor god called Alpheus in the autumn, on a terrace,
in less time than it takes to say so. His own daughter in the spring, in that same long room,
for saying she'd rather be in the north.

He also shot a ball boy in the second week of the winter, over nothing, which gets one clause
in that book. That's a different thing and I want it kept separate: shooting somebody gets you
nothing at all. It isn't appetite. It's just what he was like on a Thursday, and it had been
what he was like on a Thursday for four thousand years.

So this is not a man snapping. It is the third time in a year, and everybody in that house knew
the arithmetic, and the reason five of his children came and sat in it anyway is a thing I've
stopped trying to work out.

---

There were four people in the room. Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, and Prometheus by the door, which by
that winter was where he lived.

Hera was at the head of the table doing correspondence, which is what she did every afternoon,
because ninety letters had gone out to the governments of the world and two of them had been
answered to the wrong person and she was going through the rest of it anyway.

Prometheus's account and hers agree on everything up to the point where he stood up.

They do not agree on what it was.

She says he knew exactly what he was doing. She has never moved off it. She says he had been
looking at her for about a week the way he used to look at the glass, and that on the afternoon
he stood up he crossed the room like a man going to fetch something he had decided on, and that
he said her name first.

The book says he was confused. It says he had been asking the staff what day it was since
before the turn of the year, that he stood up in the middle of the afternoon without any
apparent reason and started across the room, and that Prometheus does not believe there was a
decision anywhere in it — that what was left in that man by then was the oldest reflex he had,
and it went off, the way a hand closes.

I can't arbitrate. They were both in the room and neither of them has any reason to lie to me
and I've read the page and I've heard her say it, and the honest position is that the
difference between those two accounts is the difference between a murder and a catastrophe, and
I do not know which one happened, and I've decided I can live without knowing.

What I'll say is that they agree on the sentence.

He said her name. Both of them have it. He said *Hera* and then something after it that neither
of them caught, and he was about four feet away and moving.

---

Poseidon got in the way.

Not heroically. Both accounts are very clear about this and I've thought about why they both
went out of their way to be. He was standing between the table and the doors because that is
where he happened to be standing, and he put an arm out and stepped across, the way you break
up something at a party, and he said:

"All right. All right —"

That's it. That's all he said and he didn't finish the second one.

It went the way it always went. Through the hands. Prometheus has written that it is not
dramatic and there is no light and it looks like two men standing close together, and he has
now written that sentence three times about three different people and I believe him.

Four seconds. Hera was through the doors before it finished.

---

Now the part I have to do carefully.

It bought him about a month.

I want to be exact about what I mean, because the arithmetic here is the whole book and the
book is what people get wrong. Hades put what was in Zeus at hundreds of thousands and then
said that was a guess and that he'd rather not guess. Poseidon was one. He'd been dry for a
year and a half by then, off the river since the second week of the summer when he sent his
bottle back without a note, and whatever four thousand years had put in him had been going out
of him the whole time, steadily, since before any of this started.

So: hundreds of thousands, and then one.

There's a page in that book where Prometheus tries to work out the exchange rate and it's the
worst thing he ever wrote. Columns. He gave it up halfway down and the second half of the page
is blank and then the next entry starts and it's about something else.

Nobody knows. What's on the record is that he was steadier for about four days, and then he was
worse than he had been before it — noticeably, straight away, and it never came back up. There
are theories. One is that a god isn't food. Another is that there wasn't enough of him left to
hold anything and it went through him like water through a sieve.

I don't know and neither does anybody and I'd rather leave it there than give you the tidy one.

He killed his brother and it made him worse. That's the finding.

---

The sea was fine.

I've saved that because it took me longest to understand and because when I finally did
understand it I had to go and sit down.

The god of the sea died on a Tuesday afternoon in a house on a mountain, and the sea did not do
anything. There was no storm and no stillness and no wrong tide anywhere on Earth. The fishing
was ordinary. I have checked this — properly, with people whose living depends on noticing, in
three ports, because I could not believe it — and the answer everywhere was the same, which is
that the week Poseidon died was an unremarkable week for weather at sea.

It was never his.

That's the thing nobody up there has ever said out loud, and I don't think it's because they're
hiding it. I think not one of them has ever had the thought. They were given portfolios four
thousand years ago in a distribution that has never been reviewed, and the portfolios were the
arrangement, and the arrangement was between them, and the sea was never consulted and never
depended on him for anything and did not notice him going.

Which makes the first one and the last one the same.

Alpheus was the god of a river in the south that silted up centuries ago, with a road along the
bed of it and a bridge over nothing, and he kept the title because nobody took it off him and
he came up for family occasions with a cake. Everyone in this story who has ever mentioned him
has mentioned him as a joke — the obsolete one, the one with a title and no river.

He wasn't the exception. He was the demonstration. The god of the sea and the god of a ditch
had exactly the same relationship to the thing they were named after, and the only difference
between them was four thousand years of everybody agreeing not to check.

---

Hera did not do anything about it.

She could have. That's the thing to hold on to. She had a house, a son with an army she'd
already stood down, four of Zeus's children in the east wing who would not have lifted a finger
for their father, and a husband who by then could not have crossed a garden at speed. Any
afternoon that winter she could have finished it, and she had already had a hundred chances
before that one, and she took none of them, including this one, including on the day he did
that in front of her.

I've read everything I can find about why and none of it is any good, including mine.

What she did was go back to the correspondence.

Not that day. The following morning. She sat at the head of the long table with a year of
unopened post and went through the rest of it in her own hand, and by the end of the week she
had done all ninety and had started on the second round, and she ran that household through the
winter without anybody being able to point at a single thing she did differently.

Except one.

She started walking the Meander.

The dry bed of it, out past the terrace, the length of it and back, most afternoons, alone, for
the rest of the winter and into the spring. Which is exactly what he had been doing since the
week she took the house, every day, for no reason anybody could give.

Nobody said anything to her about it. Athena knew. The staff knew, because the staff know
everything and are the only people in that building who have ever known everything. Nobody
mentioned it.

That's the second thing about that woman I can't make into anything, and I've now got both of
them, and they can stay where they are, one in each chapter. I've had people line things up
next to each other and produce a theory at me for two years and I know exactly what it's worth.

---

Ares was in the house.

He'd been in it three months by then and he'd had that man living in his own compound in the
north for a year before it — fed out of his kitchen, in his spare bed, with nothing to do and
no city and nowhere else to be. That was Ares's household and Poseidon was in it and got fed,
because that is what that man does with people who turn up.

What he did on the afternoon was ask what the arrangements were.

Because there aren't any. That's the thing. There was no body. There is never a body; it goes
in through the hands and what is standing there stops being there, and there is nothing to
clear up, and Prometheus has now written that twice and both times it is the worst line on the
page.

So there is nothing to do. No coin, no ferry, no port, no dock, no queue, no handler, no name
read out. A god does not go anywhere when that happens to him. He goes into the man who did it,
and then the man who did it has a bad month, and that is the whole of the ceremony.

Ares had spent nine days that same winter standing on a dock while nine hundred men went
across, and he knew every one of their names and said all of them, and it took as long as it
took and nobody hurried him.

And then his mother's — no. He was a man who lived in his house for a year and ate his food.
And there was not one single thing available to do about it, not a word to say and nowhere to
stand, and I think that is why he went back north in the spring and I don't have any evidence
for that at all.

---

There's one more thing and it isn't mine.

Poseidon told Dionysus, in a boat, a long time before any of this, that he had loved her
forever and that it felt like his heart was outside his body. He said it to buy silence and he
got it: Dionysus was blackmailing him at the time, and took the secret instead, and then kept
it. He kept it for the whole of the following year while his father burned everything he had,
and he did not part with it until the spring, when he told me, not making a point.

I've put it in because it's true and because it happened.

It doesn't go anywhere. He didn't die for love. He died because he was standing between a table
and a door with a drink in his hand, and he put an arm out the way you would at a party, and he
said all right twice.

Those are separate facts about the same man and I've stopped needing them to be one thing.

---

Prometheus was by the door for the whole of it.

He does not claim otherwise and he does not build himself an excuse, and I'll give him that
much: the entry is four lines long and the last one is *I was six feet away and I did not move,
and I want to record that I did not want to.*

I've read that sentence a number of times and I still can't tell whether he's confessing or
whether it's the first honest thing.

He went and found him afterwards, though. That's the next chapter and it's his and I'll keep
out of it.

---

Zeus asked where Poseidon had gone.

Twice that week. Not performing, not covering, not making anybody say it — genuinely, the way
he'd been asking what day it was since before the turn of the year, in the middle of some other
conversation, pleasantly, the way you ask after somebody who has stepped out.

The first time, one of the staff told him.

The second time, nobody did.

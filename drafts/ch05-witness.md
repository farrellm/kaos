# Chapter Five

## Witness

The car got him about ninety miles and then stopped, on a coast road, in the afternoon, with
about four miles of nothing in either direction.

He sat in it for a while. Then he got out and looked at it, in the way men do, as though the
looking were diagnostic. Then he pushed it about thirty feet, for no reason he has ever been
able to give, and left it half on the verge with the door open and the keys in it, and walked
back the way he had come.

I want to establish something about the state of him, because everything he does for the next
three months proceeds from it and it is not grief. Everybody assumes grief. He had not lost
her that morning; he had lost her in a street four months earlier and then found her and then
been thanked and let go on a road, and a man can survive that, and he was — this is the part
nobody believes — in reasonable order.

What he did not have was a single person who knew what had happened.

---

Consider it from inside.

He had gone into the underworld alive. He had crossed the Styx, and killed a man on it, and
been taken by Charybdis and let go, and walked a desert until his mouth split, and refused
water from a Fate, and stood in a room in front of the god of death and asked for his wife
back and been refused, and been sent back anyway by a woman in grey who liked his music.

He is the only living human being to whom any of that has ever happened.

And then he came up through a bin behind a building on the industrial road in Heraklion, and
his wife said goodbye and got out of the car, and he drove ninety miles, and there was nobody.

Riddy would not say it. Not out of cruelty — she had her own reasons and they were good ones
and I have given them to you — but the effect on him is the same as if it had been. Dionysus
had gone. His mother, or Calli, or whatever the arrangement is there, had been told a version
and had believed it in the way you believe a man who has had a very bad year.

So he went back to the Cave. Not to go down. I want that clear because everybody who tells
this now has him going back to go down again, and he was not; he had been told no by Hades in
person and he is many things but he is not a man who needs to be told twice by a god.

He went back for a witness.

Polyphemus saw it. The Fates set the questions and kept the score. There is an entire room of
people down there who watched him lose a quiz and then get in anyway, and a keeper who took his
wedding ring and put it in a drawer, and if he could stand in front of one of them and have
them say *yes, you did that*, then he could go home.

---

The bin is welded.

Not locked. Welded, properly, all the way round, by somebody with a torch and time, and then
painted over the weld in a colour that does not match, which is the detail I keep.

The door in the wall behind it is bricked. Not boarded — bricked, with mortar, and the brick
is the wrong brick and the wall has been made good badly. There is a card gone from beside the
door where a card used to be, and a rectangle of clean paint where it was, and nothing written
anywhere by anybody explaining any of it.

Polyphemus is gone. I have looked, since, more than once, and I can tell you that a man of
that description with that eye is not in Krete, is not in Athens or Sparta or Thebes, and has
not been seen by any of the four people I found who used to work the room. The Cave employed
about eleven people. Every one of them was paid up to the end of that week and told there
would be a message and there has never been a message.

I stood in front of that bin for about a quarter of an hour.

And I read it, and I want to put my reading down here in full, because it is a good example
of a competent man being completely wrong in a way that looks like insight:

Somebody had closed the door because the door had been used. A mortal had gone through it and
come back, and whoever administers that end of things had drawn the obvious operational
conclusion and shut the route before there was a queue. Sensible. Tidy. Precisely what I would
have done.

I was pleased with that. I remember being pleased with it. I noted that it meant the Fates
were being careful, and that being careful meant they were still in business, and I went off
about my afternoon.

It did not occur to me — it did not occur to me for eight months, and when it did occur to me
it was in a room above a fishmonger's with a pencil mark on a cupboard door in front of me —
that the reason a venue closes and pays everybody up to the end of the week and never sends
the promised message is not always security.

Sometimes it is that the act has stopped.

---

There was a woman at the bin.

She was there when he got there and I am fairly sure she had been there the previous day,
because there was a folding stool.

It was Anatole's wife.

You have met her. She was at the table at the Cave with her husband when he told the story of
their son without speaking, with the wheels and the hands, and made a room of drunks cry. She
is the one who said *please help him, help Anatole, look after each other*. She is the one who
said *we have a lot to lose*.

Her husband went through that bin with Orpheus and did not come back, and there is no
institution anywhere on Earth that will tell her anything about that, and so she had been
coming to the industrial road and sitting on a stool in front of a welded bin, because it is
the last place he was.

She recognised Orpheus immediately. Everybody does.

---

He told her.

I did not expect him to and I would not have advised it, and I want to be clear that he did
not go there to do it; he went there to be recognised by a keeper. He got a widow on a stool
and he told her the truth inside about four minutes.

All of it. The rule about the boat — that Charon takes one, that it is designed to make you do
it, that they had made a pact on the shore and shaken hands on it. That he broke the pact
first. That her husband said *but you said, it was your idea* and that he said *I know, but
it's you or me*, and that he pushed him under and held him there and that it took some time
and that he has the whole of it and cannot put it down.

He did not soften any of it, and he did not do the thing he does, which is to arrange it. He
told it flat and badly and out of order and stopped twice.

And when he had finished she sat on her stool for a moment and then she said:

"Thank you for telling me."

---

That is all she said about it.

She asked two questions afterwards. The first was whether it had been quick, and he said not
very, because he had just spent four minutes deciding not to lie to her and could not start
then. The second was whether their son was all right down there, and he said he did not know,
and that he had not seen him, and that he was sorry.

Then she asked whether he needed a lift anywhere, because she had a car and he plainly did
not.

He said no. She folded the stool and put it in the back and drove off, and stopped after about
forty yards and reversed, and wound the window down, and told him that she had liked the
song about his wife very much and had it on a record, and then drove off properly.

---

He waited for Dionysus for about five weeks.

He never said so. He would not have put it that way and would deny it now. But he stayed in
Heraklion, which he had no reason to do, and he was in every day at the same hours, and twice
in that period he went and stood outside a bar on the harbour road that he had no business
being outside, and Calli — who books him and has booked him since he was nineteen — asked him
who he was expecting and got no answer.

Understand what that god was to him. Dionysus found him on a beach with a rifle under his chin
and got shot for it and did not mind. Dionysus drove him at a wall to prove he could not die.
Dionysus paid his father's watch to three women to buy him a door, and lost a kitten and a
hive and a household over it, and told him to his face that his love was greater than death
and appears to have meant every word.

He is also the only other creature in the world who saw the whole of it and could say so.

He did not come.

Not that week and not that month and not, in the end, at all. He was four hundred miles away
in the south by then with a great deal on and a new set of people who needed him and who were
letting him be useful, and he had found — I want to be fair to him, because I like him and
because it is not a small thing — an actual purpose, of exactly the kind he had spent the
whole of the previous year asking his father for.

The trouble with a man who takes you on as his purpose is that a purpose is a thing you can
be finished with.

I do not think he ever decided not to go back. I have asked him twice and he was, both times,
genuinely surprised by the question, and the second time he said, "Is he all right?" — and
meant it, and was already looking at something else while he said it.

---

I have been asked what happened to her and the answer is nothing.

She did not go to the police, who would have laughed. She did not tell anybody, as far as I
can establish, for over a year. She did not come back to the industrial road. She sold the
stool with a lot of other things in the spring and moved to her sister's in the east and works
in a chandler's — not that chandler's; there are a great many chandlers — and that is the whole
of it.

And Orpheus walked back into Heraklion with no witness, no keeper, no route, no punishment and
no wife, and did not touch an instrument for three months.

People think the three months were about Riddy. Some of it was.

But I have sat with this longer than is good for me, and I have come to the view that what put
that man on the floor of a room for a season was standing on an industrial road being thanked
by a woman whose husband he had drowned, and understanding, on the spot, that absolutely
nothing was going to happen to him about it. Not from her. Not from a court. Not from Olympus,
which does not care and has never once cared. Not from the man's family, who would have been
within their rights and who instead offered him a lift.

There is no mechanism. He had gone all the way to the bottom of the world and back and found
out that at the end of it a man simply carries what he did, in the ordinary way, for as long as
he lives, and that nobody is coming to take it off him or to make it worse.

He has said since that he would have preferred the court.

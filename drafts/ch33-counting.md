# Chapter Thirty-Three

## Counting

There is no throne.

I want to get that out of the way because everybody I have ever told this to has a picture, and
the picture has a throne in it, and there isn't one and there never was. That's a human idea.
We put a chair in it because we needed somewhere for the power to be sitting.

What's actually up there is a house. A big white one with a pool and a terrace and a peacock
and a long room with a long table in it, and forty staff, and a river running through the
grounds that isn't a river.

The river was the seat. That's the whole arrangement and it took me a while to understand it
properly. Everything that made that man the king of the gods came out of a tap, and everyone
who wanted any went to him for it, and that is what a court is — it isn't loyalty and it isn't
awe, it's a queue. The reason nobody ever took Olympus in four thousand years is not that it
was defended. It's that taking it would have got you the tap.

So in the winter, when the tap had been dry for about a month, Hera came and took Olympus.

And nobody stopped her, and it was worth nothing, and she stayed anyway. I'll do the three
parts in that order.

---

She came up the road.

Same as the spring. On her own feet, in daylight this time, with Poseidon behind her and about
forty people who were not soldiers, because the soldiers weren't the point of this trip.

Her son was already in the house. He'd come down on his own a fortnight earlier, on foot,
without telling her he was going, and she found that out when she walked into the hall and he
was standing in it. Neither of them has ever said a word to me about that and I've asked them
both.

Nobody was on the gate. There has never been anybody on the gate. That house has never in its
existence had a guard on it, because for four thousand years the idea of walking up to it was
not a thing that occurred to anybody, and that turns out to be the entire security arrangement,
and it had held.

She went in through her own front door for the second time in a year and this time she did not
leave.

I've heard people call it a siege. There was no siege. She spent fifteen months in a valley in
the north building a position, and she came down and put her bags in a room, and by the end of
the week she was signing things.

---

Now. Her husband.

Because he was in that house, and he stayed in it, and this is the part everybody gets wrong.

He was delighted.

He came out when he heard the cars and he was, by every account including Prometheus's and
including hers, straightforwardly pleased. He told the staff she'd come back. He said it that
day and he said it the following week and he was still saying it in the spring, and there is no
version of the winter in which anybody corrected him, because what would you say.

She didn't correct him either.

I asked her about that, much later, on the only occasion she ever let me ask her anything
directly, and what she said was: "He was going to say it whatever I said."

Which is true and is not an answer, and I've noticed that everybody at the top of this story
has that same trick and that none of them thinks of it as a trick.

---

Right. What she got.

I'm going to do this as a list because that's how it came to her, and because the shape of it
is the point.

**The Meander.** Dry. Not low — dry, a bed with stones in it and a smell for about a fortnight
and then not even that. That's the asset. That's the thing the whole building is arranged
around and the reason there are forty staff and a peacock, and it is a ditch.

**The cities.** Every city on Earth, taken personally by her husband in the summer, on the
grounds that Poseidon was lazy. There had been no reports in a year, because reports were
Poseidon's and Poseidon was in the north being fed out of her son's kitchen. She found the
correspondence in the west room in a wooden tray and it was a year deep and none of it had been
opened.

So in her second week she wrote to them. All of them. In her own hand, because that is what she
does when she means it.

Nothing came back.

I don't think I need to say very much about that. She wrote five letters in the north a year
earlier, to her own children, in her own hand, and got nothing, and she wrote about ninety in
the winter to the governments of the world announcing that the arrangement had changed and
naming herself, and got nothing. Two of them wrote back to Zeus. Not out of loyalty. Out of
filing.

**The tacitas.** This is the one I'd have thought was worth having.

The booths are still open. They've been open the whole time — every city, every hour, doors
unlocked, the attendant saying the words about the wordless and the goddess cradling you in
your mortal pain, women sitting in the dark all day taking the worst things people have ever
done. Nobody stopped it when she left in the summer. There's no mechanism by which anybody
would.

So she went up to her own bathroom, which her husband broke his oath to get into and which
nearly ended the marriage, and she opened the cabinet, and the drawers were all still there and
the whole apparatus still worked perfectly. She sat and listened to a few.

And they were exactly what they had always been. A man about his brother. A woman about a
child. Somebody about money. The private truth of the mortal world, coming in at the rate it
comes in, which is constant, because people do not stop.

And it did nothing for her.

It's not that she'd gone off it. It's that what those confessions were *for* was knowing things
a man in this house did not know, and having them when she needed them, and the man in this
house was downstairs asking the staff what day it was. She had an intelligence network of
several hundred thousand people and there was no longer anybody to have it about.

She shut the cabinet. As far as I know she has never opened it again, and the booths are still
open, and nobody has told them, and I have thought about walking into one and telling them and
I have not done it, and I don't have a good reason.

---

**The house.** Forty staff, a roof, a lintel over a door in a staff corridor that her son
fixed. That she could actually use, and did, and by the spring Athena was running it and
running it well.

**And her husband**, in it, deteriorating, who was going to have to be somewhere.

That's the inventory. A dry ditch, a year of unopened post, a listening apparatus with nothing
worth listening to, a house that needed a lintel, and a very large man who thought she had come
home.

---

Poseidon came with her and I should say what he did there, because in about a month he isn't
going to be doing anything.

Everybody expected one thing and got another. The whole of Olympus and a fair proportion of the
underworld had spent a year assuming that the moment those two were in a building without Zeus
standing over it they'd be conducting it in the open, and that the taking of the house was
really about that.

They weren't. They didn't. As far as I can establish — and I asked, and I asked her, and she
answered it the way she answers things — that was over, and had been over since before the
spring, and neither of them appears to have decided it.

What it had been was two people getting something out of the same arrangement, and the
arrangement was that there was a man on that mountain who had everything and gave nothing, and
being in a bed with the person he had it all off was a way of taking a piece of it back. When
he stopped having everything, it stopped being that. It was just two very old people who knew
each other extremely well.

So he was in the house and he had no portfolio and no city and nothing to be, and what he did
with his days was go and stand in the Meander.

In the bed of it. Where it wasn't.

He'd stood in the water there in the spring while nine hundred men held the terrace, which was
the correct move and the last time he was ever a force in anything, and then his brother put a
hand in and took hold of him and stopped, and let him feel exactly how far in it had gone, and
let go, and asked if he wanted a towel.

He went out to that ditch most days. He walked the length of it. Nobody knows what for. He was
the sea for four thousand years and he had wanted every one of the things that river paid for,
and at the end of it he spent the winter walking up and down a dry bed in a garden that
belonged to a man who had held his soul in one hand and put it back.

Hera says he seemed all right. She says it in a way that means she wasn't looking.

---

There's a thing Ares did that week that I want in.

He asked her what to do with the nine hundred.

They'd been standing in a valley in ranks for four hundred years and then they had gone up a
mountain in the dark and taken a terrace and stood on it and come back down again, and that was
the war, and it was over, and he had them and there was nothing further to have them for.

She didn't know. She said so. Hera has said perhaps four things to me that I would call honest
and one of them is that she had never once in four thousand years had to think about what
happens to a thing after you've used it.

So he took them down himself.

Nine hundred men, four hundred years out of the river, walked to the Styx in the winter with
the god of war at the front of them, and Charon put a table on the dock and did them in about
nine days, and there was a discrepancy of nine hundred in his manifests that has been sitting
there since before Krete had a president and it came out in a column, and I'm told he was very
pleased about the column and said nothing about anything else.

Not one of them went through the Frame.

They got told on the dock, the way everybody had been getting told since the autumn, by whoever
was standing there — and they were four hundred years dead and had been in ranks the entire
time and were, by every account, extremely calm about it. They asked questions. They asked good
ones. And then nine hundred men who had spent four centuries doing exactly what they were told
declined to do the one last thing, and went and got put on the strength instead, and that is
the largest single intake Asphodel has ever had in a week.

Nikanor stopped. He's the one who told Prometheus about the garden. He's a handler at the port
now and he's good at it.

Ares stood on the dock for the whole nine days. Charon says he didn't say much. Charon says he
knew the name of every one of them and used it, and that this took a while and that nobody was
in a hurry.

---

She would not go into the west wing.

I'm putting it here with nothing around it because that's how it sits. From the day she moved
in, she did not go into the west wing of that house, and she went everywhere else in it,
including the room her husband was in.

There's a shelf in there. I know that much. I don't know what she'd want with a shelf.

I asked her once. She said something that wasn't about the west wing, pleasantly, and then
asked me a question about my mother, and by the time I'd finished answering it we were
somewhere else. I didn't go back to it and I'm not sorry.

Everyone I've told has explained it to me within about four seconds. All of them are guessing.

---

She sat at the head of the long table.

That's the chair. There isn't a throne but there is a chair, at the head of that table, and he
had sat in it for four thousand years and carved out of it, and in the winter she sat in it and
she carved.

And she counted.

That's what she does. I've been told about the north — the nine days on the road, the two bags,
the second valley, nine hundred men standing up as she went past — and I'm told the last thing
she did that afternoon was count, because there was more than she'd thought there was.

She did the same thing in the winter at the head of that table with a year of unopened post in
front of her, and got a different number.

She stayed anyway.

Four months, in a house with a dry river and a dying man in it, running a household she could
have walked out of any morning, and everyone in this account has a theory about why and most of
them are about love and I don't believe any of them.

Here's mine, and it's worth what everybody else's is worth.

She spent four thousand years being told the house was the consolation prize. She could have
had the Earth or the underworld and she took queen, and every single thing she did after that —
the bees, the confessions, the arrangement of the myths, the babies, all of it — was work done
to make the consolation prize into a real position.

And then she got it, and it was a ditch and a lintel.

And she was not going to be a woman who left the moment it stopped being worth having. That's
not love. That's not even pride, exactly.

I think she just wasn't going to give them the sentence.

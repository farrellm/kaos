# Chapter Twenty-Three

## Stock

The boy is tested on Thursdays and on the Thursday he was short.

Not wrong. He had nine by heart and he had all nine, word-perfect, in the flat voice they use
for it, which is deliberate — Ekhi taught him to strip the music out of them, because a
prophecy said beautifully is a prophecy somebody starts interpreting. He got through the nine
and stopped and Ekhi said, "And?"

And he said there wasn't an and.

They put on a tenth every quarter. That is the rhythm of the thing and has been for longer
than the room has existed — a new one arrives, it goes in the cupboard, it goes into three
heads, the youngest holder takes the newest because he will have it longest. He had been
waiting since the autumn for his tenth and none had come, and he had assumed, because he is
nineteen, that he had done something wrong.

Ekhi went into the cupboard.

---

Here is what the cupboard says, and I am going to give it the way it was given, in the
order it landed, because the order is what did the damage.

Nothing has arrived since the ninth of the month before last.

They do not know where prophecies come from. I want that on the record, because it startled
me and I had assumed — for four thousand years, without ever once examining it — that
somebody down here understood the delivery mechanism, and nobody does. They arrive. That is
the network's word for it and it is the whole of the network's theory. A child has one, or a
mother has one for a child, and it comes to the room by one of four hundred years of small
established routes, and it goes in the book.

Ekhi keeps the intake on the inside of the cupboard door in pencil, one stroke a quarter, and
the strokes go back further than the shelf does because the door is older than the shelf.

The last stroke is eight months old.

The one before it is where it should be. And the one before that. The rhythm is the rhythm
for four centuries and then it stops, in the middle of a year, on a date that I recognised
before anybody in the room said it, because I was standing on a plateau that night watching
the smoke come off a municipal building.

---

They took it well. That is the thing I keep going back to.

There was no scene. The man with the bad chest asked whether it might be a delivery problem —
routes, war, the roads out of the south — and two people were sent to check and came back on
the Thursday after and said no. One of the sisters said the obvious thing, which is that a
network that keeps prophecies and receives no prophecies is a network with a finishing date,
and Ekhi said yes, and wrote it down, and moved to the next item, which was about the room's
rent.

Riddy told me afterwards that she had wanted somebody to shout. She said it in the way you
say a thing you are ashamed of. I did not tell her that I had wanted it too.

---

Now I am going to have to explain something, and I would like to do it quickly and get out.

The Fates are alive.

I have known since the night it happened. I saw them standing in the ash of their own
immolation, entirely well, mildly entertained, and one of them unlocked me and made a joke
about waiting, and an hour later I sat at Zeus's table and let him tell me he had solved
the problem of fate, and I said nothing. I have set down my reason for that, and I gave
myself a better one, and I have not looked at either since.

So I said it in the room. It was the one useful thing I had.

I expected — I am not sure. Relief is what I expected. What I got was Ekhi putting her pencil
down and looking at the cupboard door for a while and then saying: "So it isn't damage."

Because if three women were burned to death in a poolroom and the writing stopped, that is a
murder, and murders can in principle be avenged and their effects in principle reversed. But
if the three women walked out of the fire and the writing stopped anyway, then the stopping is
something else. It is a decision, or it is a consequence, and either way it is not a thing you
can fix by winning.

The room understood that faster than I did. They understood it at the speed of people who
have spent four hundred years being the ones who find out.

---

The second thing happened on the same evening and it was Riddy's, and I was not much more
than furniture for it.

She had asked, the week before, whether she could read in the cupboard. Not a particular
thing. Just read. Ekhi had said yes on the condition that she put everything back herself,
which is the room's only rule about the shelf and is about hands, not secrecy.

She had got about a third of the way along when she started taking three of them out at once
and putting them on the table side by side.

I do not know how to say this in a way that does not sound like a trick, so plainly: there
are prophecies in that cupboard that cannot be true if Renewal is real. Not many. She found
three that night and a fourth on the Tuesday. They are unremarkable little things about
unremarkable people — a woman told what she would find, a man told what he would not have
again, a child told where it would end — and every one of them is nonsense in a world where a
soul goes through the Frame and comes back as something new, and every one of them is plain,
flat, obvious sense in a world where it goes through the Frame and is drunk.

They have had all four of those in the cupboard for between ninety and three hundred years.

And nobody put them on the table together, because putting them on the table together is
interpretation, and they do not interpret. It is their oldest rule and it is the reason the
words in that room are uncorrupted after four centuries and it is why they are the only
people on Earth who could be trusted to hold them.

It is also why they have been sitting on the shape of the largest crime in the world for
three hundred years without seeing it.

Ekhi did not defend the rule. She looked at the four of them on the table for a long moment
and then said that they would need a second procedure, and asked Riddy whether she would
write it, and Riddy said yes, and that was that. No speech. A woman in an apron changing four
hundred years of practice between one item and the next because the evidence was on her table.

I have watched gods take a century to move a lunch.

---

Right. The question.

I have been circling it for four pages and I am going to put it down now, and I want to say
in advance that I do not have the answer, which is not a device this time.

Every prophecy that will ever exist is now in that cupboard, or in a head, or in a person
somewhere who has not yet been told and does not know they are carrying the last of
something. There will be no more. The stock is the stock.

And I was told to wait.

I was told it at my own door, by a woman with a pencil behind her ear, in a sentence I have
carried for four thousand years and have quoted to you more than once with a certain amount
of pride: *all you had to do was trust, and wait.* I have understood that, for the whole of
my imprisonment, as an instruction to be *good at* something. Patience as a discipline.
Waiting as an act.

But if the thing you are waiting for is being made, then waiting is neutral and costs
nothing. And if the thing you are waiting for has stopped being made, then waiting is not a
strategy at all. It is expenditure. Every year of it is a year of a stock going down, and
four thousand years of it is —

is what?

That is the question. How much of it did I spend? Not of my own life; I do not care about my
own life and neither, I suspect, do you by now. How much of *theirs*. How many quarters went
past that cupboard door, one stroke at a time, while I was being admirably patient on a rock
about it.

I do not know.

I have tried to work it out twice since and both times I have found myself doing arithmetic to
avoid the sentence underneath the arithmetic, which is this: I have never once, in four
thousand years, considered that the instruction might have had a cost attached to me
obeying it.

The room stood down at nine, which is when it stands down. Ekhi put the cups in the sink.

# Chapter Eight

## Useful

The last thing that happened on the night everything ended was that my friend's youngest son
stood in a field in the south and asked a group of people what they wanted.

You were there for it. *What do you want? Tell me, and I'll do it.*

I want you to hold on to how good that is. It is not a god's sentence. A god does not ask;
a god arrives with a portfolio and confers something and waits to be thanked, and if you have
ever wondered why the temples are full of people saying *please* into the dark, that is the
reason — nobody up there has ever once opened with the question.

He opened with the question. He had lost a cat and a mother he never identified and the whole
of his family in about nine hours, and he went south and found the people his father had
spent a generation grinding down, and he asked them what they wanted and undertook to do it
before he had heard the answer.

They said: help us rebuild Troy, and destroy Olympus.

And he said yes, and meant it, and did not ask a single further question, and that is the
whole of the trouble and it took him five weeks to find out.

---

First, the good part, because there is a good part and it lasted and it is still going.

They put him to work.

I had expected — I will be honest with you, I had expected a season of theatre. I have known
that boy since he was made and I have watched him want things, and what he has always wanted
is to be at the centre of a room, and there is a version of the south in which he stands on a
cart every evening being adored by grateful people and does nothing whatsoever.

Andromache put him on the water.

There are four compounds in the south and two of them had no water that summer, which is a
sentence I have written before in this account about a different woman's decision, and it is
the same summer and the same two compounds and that is not a coincidence, it is simply the
condition of the place. He walked the four of them with a man called Iason who has dug wells
for thirty years, and Iason showed him where the water was not, and he put it there.

Then the weather.

He is good at it. I want to say that plainly because I am about to be unkind about him for two
pages. He is not much of a god by the standards that family uses, and the standards that
family uses are worthless, and the specific thing he can do is bring rain onto a particular
piece of ground without taking it off the piece next to it, which is a finer instrument than
anything his father has ever held.

There was no bad harvest in the south that year. There has not been one since. Nobody up there
has noticed and nobody down there talks about anything else.

---

Now the trouble.

He thought it was about Troy.

That is the entire misunderstanding and I did not spot it either, and I was in and out of that
compound for five weeks and I heard the conversations. *Help us rebuild Troy, and destroy
Olympus.* He heard a grievance. A large one, an old one, a twelve-year-old one with a wall in
it and a horse in it and a great many dead, but a grievance of the kind that has settled into
the shape people carry — and you can serve a grievance like that for the rest of your life
with wells and weather and never be asked for anything sharp.

He was in mourning himself, which did not help. He had a kitten four weeks earlier and his
father killed it in front of him to demonstrate a principle, and he still had the sling. I want
to record that without doing anything to it. He wore it under the coat and I do not believe
he was aware of it.

So he had a grief, and they had a grief, and he had assembled a picture in which they were the
same size and the same age and could be worked on with the same instruments, which are time
and usefulness and rain.

Nobody corrected him because nobody knew he needed correcting. That is the mechanism and it is
not sinister and there is no villain in it. Every single person in that compound had watched
seven coffins go into the ground on the day after the pardon. It was the largest thing that had
ever happened to any of them. It did not occur to one of them that the god eating at their
table did not know, any more than it would occur to you to tell a man in your own kitchen what
year it is.

And he did not ask, because he had already promised.

That is the thing I keep coming back to. He undertook the whole of it before he heard the
answer, and once you have said *tell me and I'll do it* there is no comfortable moment
afterwards in which you say *sorry, what is this actually about*. He had made the asking into
a grand gesture, and a grand gesture is a thing you can only do once.

---

He found out in the fifth week, in the ordinary way, from a boy of about fifteen.

There is a piece of ground at the top of the eastern compound with seven graves in it. They are
new. They have stones, small ones, and they are laid out slightly apart from the rest of that
ground because there was an argument at the time about whether they should be at the front.

He had walked past them. Every day, for five weeks, and it is not a large compound and it is
on the way to the road.

The boy was doing something with a fence and Dionysus stopped to talk to him, because he stops
to talk to everybody, and asked him whose they were.

I was about forty feet away. I have thought about the forty feet.

The boy told him. All seven. He knew all seven names and he said them in the order they were
buried, and the last of the seven was Astyanax, who they had got back and lost again in the
same year, and the boy said it the way you say a thing everybody knows.

And then he said the bit that did it, which was: "He was the one who did the monument."

Because Dionysus knew that. He had heard it the way everybody in that country heard it, because
for two days it was the only thing on: that there had been a seventh Trojan, that the seventh
had put the shit on the monument, that he had been hidden by his own people, and that Minos had
offered the six their lives if he came forward. He had simply never put the boy in the ground
with the name he had heard, because nobody had ever said them in the same sentence to him, and
because the pardon had been the last part of the story he was told.

He thought the seventh had been pardoned. So did everybody who was not in Krete that
midnight, which is most of the world, and none of the rest of them had promised anybody
anything.

---

He went to Andromache.

He went straight there, across the compound, at speed, and he did the thing that I would have
told him not to do and that I would have been wrong to tell him not to do.

He offered to fix it.

I want to be exact about what he said, because I have it from two people and they agree. He
said he was sorry, and that he had not known, and then he said: *I'll get him back.*

Not *I'll try*. He is not built for *I'll try*.

And then he stood in that doorway and worked out, over the following minute, in front of her,
that he could not. He has been told he cannot by every single person who has ever discussed
it with him. His uncle has told him. I have told him. He knows the rule better than he knows
any other rule in the world, because it is the only one that has ever been enforced against
him — and it had not been in the room when he opened his mouth, because when you love somebody
the rule is never in the room.

He had to take it back. In her house, standing up, having offered it.

---

Here is what she did, and it is why this chapter is called what it is.

She was not angry, and she did not forgive him, and both of those would have been
survivable.

She was not surprised.

She said — and this is verbatim, and I have it from her, because I asked her about it a year
later in a different building and she remembered it exactly — she said: "No. I know. It's all
right."

And then she asked him whether he was going to be at the eastern compound on the Thursday,
because the well.

That is the whole of it. He offered her the largest thing in his possession, discovered in
front of her that it was not in his possession, and she moved to the next item, because there
was a next item and the next item was a well.

She had never thought he could. Not for a moment, not on the first night in the field, not
when he made the promise. She had heard a god say *tell me and I'll do it* and she had
understood it precisely as an offer of labour, because she is a practical woman who has
buried everybody, and she had asked for the two things she wanted and had been quietly
sorting them ever since into the parts he could actually do.

Wells. Weather. A harvest.

He wanted to be loved and he wanted to matter, and he had spent his entire existence being
given the first in place of the second by people who found him charming and useless. And in a
field in the south a woman who had lost everybody looked at him and correctly assessed his
capacities and put him on the water, and it was the first time in his life that anybody had
done him the courtesy.

It is also the coldest thing that has ever been done to him and neither of them has ever said
so.

---

He asked about Ari once, in that fifth week.

Not by name. He asked me whether I had been to Krete lately and whether there was any news
out of the palace, in a voice arranged to sound like a man asking about the weather in another
country, and I said that there was a great deal and gave him about four minutes of it, and he
listened to all of it and asked one further question and then did not raise it again.

She does not know he exists. I want to keep saying that at intervals because it does not
change and because everybody who hears about this expects it to.

---

I gave him three weeks.

That is my prediction and I am putting it down because I have asked you to weigh these as they
arrive. I thought he would go.

Not out of cowardice — he is not a coward, he has been shot for a stranger. I thought he would
go because everything that boy has ever done has been in exchange for something, and the
exchange had just failed. He had come south to be needed by people who wanted a god, and he
had found out in a single afternoon that they wanted a utility, and that the thing they most
wanted was the one thing he could not do, and that they had known that about him before he
did.

There is nothing in that for him. There is no version of it that ends with him at the centre
of a room. I gave him three weeks and I would have given him two if I had not liked him.

He is still there.

Two years, four hundred miles from anybody who has ever been fond of him, doing the wells and
the weather on a rota, for people who are pleasant to him and do not defer to him and have
never once asked him to do anything he cannot. He has missed everything. He was not at the
strike on Olympus and he was not in the house in the winter, and when the sound of the Meander
went off nobody in that building thought to send for him, and he found out from a song.

I have been wrong about a number of things in this account and I will be wrong about a great
many more, and this is the only one so far that I have been glad about.

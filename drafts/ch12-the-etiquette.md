# Chapter Twelve

## The Etiquette

Medusa went down into Asphodel in the sixth week to stop people talking, and I went with her,
and I would like to say at the outset that she knew it would not work before she started and
went anyway, because that is the difference between her and everybody else in this account.

The problem was the version.

You will remember that thirty people were standing at the Lethe on the night a woman came out
of the water, and that about half of them were dead and off shift and had come down for the
last crossing, because there is not a great deal on. By the fourth day it was in the canteens.
By the sixth week it was in every sector and it had been through several thousand mouths, and
what was arriving at the far end of that was no longer a report.

It had become a story about a god.

That is the shape it took, and it took it without anybody deciding, and it is the shape those
stories always take: that Hades had come down to the river and raised a woman up. It is
tidier. It has somebody in charge of it. It requires nothing of the person hearing it except
that they be impressed, and it is — and this is the part that had Persephone up at four in the
morning — *entirely compatible with everything the establishment has ever told them.* A god
did a wonder at the water. Fine. Wait your two hundred years.

The truth is that a dead handler with no coin held on to his mother and would not let go, and
that nobody, including him, knows how.

Medusa said the difference between those two was the whole war and that she was not going to
sit in an office while the wrong one set.

---

I had not been in Asphodel properly before.

I want to be exact about that, since I have spent this account telling you I know where
everybody is. I had been *through* it. I had crossed it, at speed, on my way to the Lethe and
the office above the port, perhaps forty times since I came off the rock, the way you go
through a district on the way to somewhere with a name.

It has a canteen. It has a games room and a basketball court and a laundry and several hundred
blocks of accommodation, and it has a smell of the same soup at the same hour, and it is
neither a prison nor a camp and it does not look like suffering, and that is the first thing
that has to be understood about it or nothing else in this book makes sense.

They eat. They do not need to. They sleep, in beds, at night, and there is no night. They
shower. They queue for the shower and there is an argument about the queue. And they salt
their food, which is the detail I have never been able to put down since Adrian explained it
to me, standing up, with a tray in his hands, in the manner of a man giving directions to a
tourist.

You salt it because you salted it.

That is the whole of the etiquette and it is not a superstition and it is not denial. A person
is a set of things they do, and when everything else has been taken off you the things you do
are all that is left of the doing, and if you stop salting the food then in about a year you
are something that stands in a room. They have all seen that happen. There is a word for it
down there that I am not going to write, because Adrian would not say it either, and used a
gesture instead.

He also told me, in the same tone, in the same minute, that the ones who ask questions get
into trouble.

Keep that.

---

Now. The two hundred years.

I am going to spend some time on this because I am, by a very considerable margin, the most
qualified being in this story to explain it, and because I have never seen anybody else get it
right.

A sentence with a date at the end of it is not a punishment. It is a job.

That is what nobody up there has ever understood, and it is why Olympus has been able to run
this for four thousand years with four to six hundred staff. You do not have to guard a person
who has a date. The date does the guarding. The whole of their attention goes onto the far end
of it, and they organise themselves around the waiting the way a household organises itself
around a birth — and the organising is elaborate, and it is *satisfying*, and it eats the
years.

You get the arithmetic. Everybody down there can tell you their number to the month and will,
unprompted, on a first meeting, the way the living tell you what they do.

You get the calendars. Not written; they do not need writing. You get people who know that
they are through the first sixty and that sixty is more than a quarter and that a quarter is
the hard part, and who have decided that the hard part is over, and who are wrong and will
decide it again at eighty.

You get the industry. There is an enormous amount to arrange when you are waiting for
somebody: the meeting itself, which is rehearsed; what will be said; where they will sleep;
what to warn them about first. There is a woman in the fourth sector who has a room allocated
for a brother who has not arrived, and has had it for the better part of a century, and moves
things in it.

And you get the thing underneath all of it, which is that not one of them is idle and not one
of them is doing anything. Two hundred thousand people conducting, with real discipline and
considerable ingenuity, an activity with nothing at the end of it.

I watched a canteen of them for most of an afternoon and I could have described every one of
them to you inside a minute.

---

Medusa did not make a speech.

I had expected one, and I had — this is the sort of thing I was still doing — thought about
what I would have put in it. What she did instead was work the sectors for nine days, one at a
time, in the canteens, at the hour when the shift changes and there are two shifts in the room,
and she talked to people the way she talks to people, which is briskly and without any warmth
on the surface of it and with a great deal underneath that she would deny.

She was not recruiting rebels. I want that clear because she used the word *rebels* to Riddy
on a platform a few weeks earlier and everybody who hears about this expects a cell and a
password.

She was recruiting for accuracy.

Her reasoning is the best piece of thinking anybody did that season, she did it in about a day,
and nobody has ever credited her with it. No power on Earth or under it can stop two hundred
thousand people talking in a canteen — so you do not suppress the wrong version, you put the
right one into the hands of the people everybody talks to, early, and let it be the boring
one.

So she went looking for the reliable.

Not the angry. She was specific about that and she was asked about it and her answer was that
an angry man tells you a better story than the one he was given, every time, without meaning
to, and that she had no use for anybody's improvements.

Not the leaders either, and there are leaders down there, and I could have named them for her
and offered to.

She wanted the ones everybody talks to and nobody watches. The ones who are in every room
because of the chairs. The ones incapable of exaggeration.

---

She got about sixty of them over the nine days, and two of them are why this chapter exists.

Prue has been dead sixty-one years and I had, at that point, entirely failed to see her.

I had her filed. I want to be honest about the filing: handler, promoted with Caeneus, chatty,
lonely, faintly ridiculous, kind, a prophecy about a dark-haired creature and a winter fall
that she has made fit a pony and her insomnia. All of that is accurate and it is the sort of
inventory I have been keeping on people for four thousand years and it is worth nothing.

Here is what Medusa saw in about four minutes in a canteen, which I had not seen in ten years.

Prue knows everybody.

Not a great many people. *Everybody* — by name, in every sector, including the ones nobody
likes and the ones who have stopped coming out of their blocks, because she does the chairs
for anything that happens and doing the chairs for sixty-one years means you have been in
every room in the underworld and have asked every person in it whether they are all right for
a cup. There is no register of the dead. There is no list of who is where; the establishment
has sectors and numbers and does not have names.

Prue is the list. She has been the list for decades and nobody had noticed, including me, and
including her.

Medusa noticed in four minutes and did not tell her, and told her nothing whatsoever about
why she was being asked, and simply gave her the accurate version and asked her to have it
right.

Prue was thrilled. She is thrilled by most things. She asked whether she should write it down
and Medusa said no and she said "No, quite right," and then went and told about nine hundred
people over the following month, correctly, in the right order, every time, with the boring
bits left in.

---

Adrian is the other one and Adrian is the reason I have written this chapter at all.

He was a clerk of some kind — he has told me twice and I have not retained it, which I notice —
and he is fussy and slow and courteous and I have never once been interested by anything he has
said to me, and I include in that a four-thousand-year acquaintance with the god of the sea.

His wife came down about a year after he did. She had a coin. He did not.

I want to give that its proper size, because everybody hears it and moves on. They were in the
same building for a fortnight. Then a diver stood in the shallows and did the job, which is
persuasion, which is the whole job — and she went into the water believing what everybody in
that queue believes, and he stood on the bank and watched her go, and was pleased for her,
and has been in a canteen ever since, with the better part of two centuries still on the
clock.

He has never once, in the whole of that, said a word against it. He explains the salt to new
arrivals. He tells them the ones who ask questions get into trouble. He is, by any measure the
establishment would recognise, the single most successful product that machine has ever made.

Medusa gave him the accurate version in a canteen at the shift change.

And he listened to all of it, and thought about it in the way he thinks about things, which is
visibly and slowly and with his hands folded, and then he asked her a question.

He did not ask how the man had done it. That is what everybody asks; that is what fifteen
hundred people in that sector were asking, and it is a question about a wonder, and a question
about a wonder has nowhere to go.

He asked: **"Where was she coming back from?"**

---

Medusa told me afterwards that she had gone down there with nine days of material prepared and
had not prepared for that, and that she stood in a canteen holding a tray and could not think
of a single reason not to answer it.

So she answered it.

She told him there is a room behind a doorway in a wall at the back of the building, and that
it is not small, and that what is in it is people, standing up, and that they have been through
the Frame and are what is left afterwards, and that this is where Renewal goes and there is no
other place it goes to.

Adrian said, "Right."

Then he asked whether it was the same for everybody or only for the ones without coins, and
she said the same for everybody, and he said "Right" again, and then he said thank you, and he
took his tray back, because you take your tray back.

---

I have thought about that exchange more than anything else in the first part of this account.

That question is the most dangerous thing that has happened underneath the world in four
thousand years, and it is more dangerous than the woman in the river, and I knew it in the
canteen.

Because the rumour was about a miracle and miracles are containable. Olympus has been
containing miracles since before there was an Olympus; a wonder is a thing that happened once,
to somebody else, at the water, and you may be impressed by it and then go and queue for the
shower.

A question about the destination is not containable. There is no answer to *where was she
coming back from* that does not end in that room, and once one man in a canteen has the room
in his head he is going to describe it to somebody, because he is a decent man and it is the
sort of thing you tell people, and the person he tells is going to ask him how he knows, and
he is going to say a Gorgon told him at the shift change, and that is not a rumour any more.
That is provenance.

It took a year to arrive, and for months nothing happened at all. I went back down four or
five times expecting something and found a canteen and a queue for the shower and a man
explaining the salt.

When it came it did not come as a rising. It came as an enormously boring man at the edge of a
dock for six days, telling frightened strangers the truth, plainly, in the right order, with
the boring bits left in — which is exactly what he was handed in a canteen in the sixth week by
a woman with no time for anybody, who picked him because he could not embroider.

---

She had sixty of them by the end of the nine days and she never used them for anything.

There was no network and no instruction and nothing to activate. She gave sixty people an
accurate account and went back up to the platform and got on with the divers, and as far as I
can establish she never spoke to fifty-eight of them again.

I asked her, on the way up, what it was for.

She said that she did not know, and that when a thing is going to be said either way you may
as well have it said right, and then she asked me whether I was coming down on the Thursday
because she was two short on the late.

I said I would see.

I did not go. I had a great deal on that Thursday and I have no memory whatsoever of what it
was.

# Chapter Fifteen

## Volunteers

I put these people where they are.

I want that in front of you at the start, because by the end of this chapter I am going to
have said it once more and it is going to sound different, and I would like you to have
the first version to compare it against.

---

The plan — my plan, the thing I have been building since before your language existed — went
into operation on a Wednesday in a corridor under the world, and it did not work.

They had picked the soul carefully. That was Hades. He had been into the Nothing four times
that week, which is four more times than in the preceding two centuries, and he came out
each time greyer and with more notes, and what he had settled on was a woman about ninety
years dead, from a fishing town, chosen on the grounds that she was near the front, that she
had been in there a comparatively short time, and that nothing about her was complicated.

They took Caeneus down. Medusa went, and Persephone, and I went, and Hades stood at the door
of the place with his hands behind his back like a man at the opening of a building he is
not sure of.

And nothing happened.

He did what he had done at the river. He put his hands on her — carefully, having asked, out
loud, although she did not answer, because he asks — and he stood there, and everybody
waited, and after about a minute he said, "I don't think there's anything for me to hold."

They tried twice more. Another woman. A man. Nothing.

The fourth was a boy of about fourteen and I want to record that Caeneus stayed with that one
a very long time, longer than was useful, long after Persephone had said his name twice, and
when he came away he did not speak to anybody for an hour.

---

So they did it the other way, which is the way anybody sensible does anything, and which had
not occurred to me: they went back and looked at the one that had worked.

Persephone chaired it. There was a table, and a pot of the tea that tastes of nothing, and
four dead people and two gods trying to work out what had been in a river three months
earlier that was not in a corridor on a Wednesday.

They got there in about forty minutes. It is not complicated once you stop looking for
something complicated. I had been looking for something complicated for four thousand years
and I would like that noted.

His mother wanted to come back.

That is the whole of it. Not *was willing*. Not *consented*, which is Hades's word and which
he used four times before Medusa told him to stop. She had shot herself and gone down and
stood at a port for ten years to be in that water on that day, and what was in her when he
put his hands on her was a thing pointing hard in one direction, and he did not make it. He
carried it.

And she wanted to come back **to him**. Specifically. A named person she was going towards.

The woman ninety years dead from the fishing town wanted nothing. She had had it taken out of
her. That is what the Frame does — not kill, exactly; it makes an unwanting thing, and you
cannot carry a thing that is not going anywhere, and there is nothing for a man to hold.

Hades said, "So it doesn't work on the Nothing." He said it in the flat voice of somebody
adding a column and getting a number he already knew.

And Medusa said: "It works on the ones somebody's still coming for."

---

Now, here is the part that took the rest of the week, and I was present for all of it and
contributed nothing.

Everyone in Asphodel is waiting for someone. Medusa said that to a boy on a dock ten years
ago as a piece of routine cruelty and it is simply true. It is the condition of the place.
Two hundred years is a sentence you serve by waiting, and the thing you are waiting for is
always a person, and a great many of those people have long since gone through the Frame and
are standing in a room under the world with their wanting taken out.

So the ones in the Nothing who can be carried are the ones with somebody in Asphodel still
coming for them.

Which means the work does not scale by the renewer. It scales by the grief.

And there was a second thing, which Persephone saw first and put in front of them without
softening it, and which is the reason this chapter is not a triumph.

If a soul goes back to the world, it goes to the world. It is renewed — properly, the thing
that was always advertised — and it goes into a life somewhere as somebody new, and it is
gone. It does not come back to Asphodel. It does not know you. The person who has spent
sixty years, ninety years, in a canteen with a games room and a basketball court waiting for
a particular face to come through the turnstiles gets to give that face away permanently, in
exchange for nothing, on the understanding that somewhere on Earth in twenty years there
will be a stranger who owes them nothing and will never know.

That is the offer. Medusa took it to them herself and did not dress it.

---

The room they used holds about sixty and about ninety came.

Prue organised it. She did the chairs and she did the list and she did a notice with the
times on it in a very good hand, and she was extremely happy for about two days in the way
that she is, and then somebody asked her who she was putting forward and she said, "Oh — no.
I'm not waiting for anyone," and went off to see about the urn.

I had heard her say that once before, ten years ago, on a dock, cheerfully, as a small
correction to a colleague. It had not occurred to me on either occasion that it might be the
worst sentence in the building.

Adrian volunteered on the first morning. His wife. Forty-one years.

He asked one question, which was whether she would be all right, and Hades — who is a great
many things and is not a liar — said that he did not know, that nobody had ever done this,
and that what they could tell him was that she would be alive and would not remember any of
it, including him. And Adrian said, "Right, well. That's the good bit, isn't it," and signed
Prue's list, and went to work, and was on shift at the turnstiles that afternoon as usual.

They took eleven in the end. Not because eleven was a target — they had thirty-one on the
list by the Thursday — but because eleven was what the day held before it became obvious
that the day had held enough.

---

I am going to describe the thing itself once, properly, and then I am not going to describe it
again in this account, because it is the same every time and because I have come to think
that people who describe it repeatedly are doing it for themselves.

They brought them out of the Nothing one at a time. That part is Hades, and it is manual, and
it is not pleasant to watch: he goes in, he finds them, he brings them out the way you bring
somebody out of a burning building, and by the fourth he had to sit down.

They put them in the shallow end of the Lethe, where the water is about knee-deep and moves
slowly, and where the divers stand when they are working.

Then the person from Asphodel goes and stands in front of them and says who they are.

That is it. That is the whole ceremony and nobody designed it; it happened at the first one
because Adrian did not know what else to do and it turned out to be necessary, and after that
they did it that way. He stood in front of a woman who had been unwanting for forty-one years
and he said his own name and then hers and then a thing about a house in a street, and she did
not respond, and he kept going, and somewhere in the third or fourth minute — and everybody
present felt it and nobody has been able to say what it was — she *turned*, not physically, not
anything you could point at, and there was suddenly a direction in her.

And Caeneus, who had been standing behind her in the water the whole time doing nothing at
all, put his hands on her shoulders and took hold of it and carried it.

She went in about four seconds. There was no light. She was there and then the water had
nobody in it and there was a sound like a room going quiet.

Adrian stood in the shallows for a bit. Then he said, "Thank you," to nobody in particular,
and got out, and put his shoes on.

They did ten more.

---

By the ninth, Caeneus was slow.

Nobody remarked on it. He was on his feet the whole day, he did every one, he was patient
with every person who came down to the water and several of them needed a great deal of
patience — one man could not get through his wife's name and had to go away and come back —
and at no point did he indicate that anything was wrong, and I have since established that
nothing was wrong in any sense that a physician or a god would recognise.

But he was slow, in the way that a man is slow at the end of a day of moving furniture, and
somewhere around the ninth Medusa stopped watching the water and started watching him.

He came out at about seven. Somebody had brought bread. He sat on the steps with the bread
and Prue sat with him and told him at length about the difficulty with the urn, and he
laughed in the right places.

Persephone said, quietly, to Hades, that they should do fewer next time.

Hades said, "Fewer than eleven?"

And she said, "Fewer than eleven."

---

I went up on the Friday to tell Riddy, because somebody had to and because I wanted to be
the one, which I did not examine at the time.

I had it arranged on the way. I had the number and I had Adrian and I had the thing about
there being no light, and I had a sentence about the fishing town that I was fairly pleased
with.

She let me get about half of it out.

Then she said: "How was he?"

I said that he had done all eleven, that he had been patient with a man who could not get
through his wife's name, that he had eaten some bread on the steps afterwards and laughed at
one of Prue's stories about an urn.

She said: "That's not what I asked."

So I said: tired. He was tired. He was slow by the ninth and Persephone had said they should
do fewer next time, and Medusa had stopped watching the water and started watching him.

And she went quiet for a while and then asked me when I was next going down.

I said Sunday.

She said, "Right," and did not tell me why she wanted to know, and I did not ask, and I went
back to Olympus that evening and thought about eleven people in a canteen and not once, in
the whole of the crossing, about the question she had actually asked me twice.

---

Eleven souls went back into the world that day.

They are out there now. One of them is a baby in a fishing town, and one of them is
something I have not been able to establish and have stopped trying to, and none of the
eleven knows any of this or ever will, and eleven people in a canteen under the world have a
gap where a face used to be and got nothing whatsoever in return.

That is the first mass renewal, which is the thing my four thousand years were for.

I did not design the mechanism. I did not find the flaw in the Frame, I did not chair the
table, I did not think of asking the ones who were still coming for somebody, and when the
work was being done I stood on a bank in a dry coat.

Here is what I did.

I put a woman in a road in Krete on a holy day, and I made sure a man took her coin, so that
she would arrive without one, so that she would be sent to the wrong queue, so that she would
meet a dog handler ten years dead who had also been sent to the wrong queue, and so that on
a night about a fortnight later they would compare two prophecies and find them identical.

I placed her there.

That is the strongest sentence I have, and I have been over it a number of times since, and I
notice that every verb in it is a verb about position.

# Chapter Eighteen

## A Wall With a Door In It

Ari put it to him nine days after they brought him up, in the room with the half-closed
shutters, sitting on the end of the bed because there was one chair and he was in it.

She said she wanted him to build something.

He said that he had one hand.

And she said that she had not asked him to build it, she had asked him to design it, and that
there were four hundred people in her city who could hold a chisel and not one of them could
tell them where to put it.

Riddy, who was in the room, says he then spent about five minutes explaining why he was the
wrong choice, and that all of it was true. He is sixty-something. He has not designed anything
that was not a cage since before Ari was born. He is, and he used the word, her father's
creature — the last surviving instrument of the administration she had killed her way out of,
and there were people on her staff who thought he should be tried.

Ari let him finish. Then she said that she had considered all of that, and that she had also
considered that he was the only person alive who had built something the gods could not
follow him into, and that she was in the market for exactly one of those.

---

I should set out the problem properly, because everything in this chapter is a consequence of
its shape, and because it is not the problem anybody thought they had.

They can do it. That was established in the autumn: eleven souls went back into the world in a
single day and are out there now. What they cannot do is do it *often*, and the reason is not
the one you would guess.

The reason is not Caeneus. He is the part everybody looks at and he is not the bottleneck; he
can carry as many as there are, and what it costs him is a separate horror and is not a rate
limit.

The bottleneck is retrieval.

Somebody has to go into the Nothing and bring them out one at a time, by hand, and the
somebody is Hades, and there is no second somebody. He comes out grey. By the fourth in a day
he has to sit down. The arithmetic that everyone at the table had been avoiding since the
autumn is that at the observed rate — call it eleven on a very good day, and there had not
been another very good day — the Nothing empties in a length of time that nobody would write
down, and the strain that Hades has been warning his brother about since before any of this
does not wait for them.

So the objective is a throughput problem, and it had been sitting in an office for four months
being discussed as a moral one.

---

Daedalus asked to be taken down.

They took him down in the third week, which was too early, and he was ill for two days
afterwards and did it again the following week. He went to the port, and the Lethe, and the
platform, and he stood at the doorway of the Nothing for about a quarter of an hour with a
lamp, not going in, and asked to be shown where the water came from and where it went
afterwards.

Then he asked Hades how the Frame was made.

I was there for this and I would like to give it to you at length, because it is the most
frightening conversation I have ever sat through and nothing in it is raised above a
conversational volume.

He asked what it was made of. Hades said he did not know.

He asked what he had started from — whether there had been a site, whether something had been
there before, whether it had been assembled somewhere and moved. Hades said that he did not
think it was like that.

He asked how long it took. Hades thought about it and said he could not say, and that it had
not felt like time.

He asked whether there were drawings, and Hades laughed, not unkindly, in the way you laugh at
a question from a child about your work; and then stopped laughing, because Daedalus was
writing the answers down with his left hand, slowly, in a column, and the column was all in
one direction.

He asked, last, how Hades had known when it was finished.

And Hades sat with that for a while and said: "It stopped needing anything from me."

---

That is the whole of it and it is why they need a man with one hand.

The gods did not build the machinery of death the way a thing is built. Zeus told his brother
to make it and it was made, and it works, and it has worked for four thousand years, and there
is no one alive — there is not, as far as I can establish, anyone who has ever existed — who
can tell you how it functions, including the person it came out of.

Which means it cannot be repaired. It cannot be modified, extended, throttled or reversed. It
cannot be *understood*, and every proposal anybody had made in four months for fixing the
Nothing had quietly assumed that somewhere there was a mechanism with a hand on it.

There isn't. There is a thing that stopped needing anything from him.

Daedalus said, in the corridor afterwards, that he had built one building in his life that he
could not fully account for, and that it was the labyrinth, and that he had been inside it in
the dark for four and a half months finding out exactly how much of it he did not know. He
said it as a professional observation. He was not making a point. It was, however, the point.

---

His design took him eleven days and it is not clever.

I want to say that with respect, because I had assumed — I had assumed a great many things
that week — that what would come back would be intricate. He built a maze once and it was the
most famous object in the world.

What he came back with was: *stop carrying them out. Take the water in.*

The Lethe runs past the Nothing at a level a little above it and about ninety feet away
through rock. If you cut a channel, and you cut it at the right fall, the water goes in on its
own and keeps going in, and the floor of the Nothing becomes the shallow end of the river.

Then nobody retrieves anybody. The souls are standing in it already. The grievers walk in
among them, and say who they are, one at a time or forty at a time depending on how many
people you can bring down that day, and the man who carries stands where the water is deepest
and does the thing he does, and the rate is no longer set by how many a god can lift before he
has to sit down. It is set by how many living people can be persuaded to come down and say a
name.

Which is a problem, but it is a problem of the ordinary kind.

He drew it on the back of four sheets of accounts paper and had to be asked twice to explain
the fall, because he assumed everybody could see it.

Caeneus asked the only question that mattered and nobody else in the room had got near it.

He had been quiet for the whole of the presentation, which is usual, and he waited until
Daedalus had finished and the others had started on the practicalities, and then he said:

"The people saying the names. They'll be standing in there, will they?"

Daedalus said yes. There is no arrangement in which they are not; the water is going into the
room and the room is where the dead are, and a griever has to be close enough to be heard.

And Caeneus said: "Then that's the number. Not the rock."

He said it without any edge on it and went back to listening, and it took the table about half
a minute to understand what he had said, and Medusa was the first one there and put her pen
down.

Because he is right and he is the only one in that room who has been inside it. Everybody had
been solving for how fast souls could be brought to grievers. Nobody had solved for how many
human beings can be asked to walk into the worst place that has ever existed, in the dark,
past several hundred thousand people standing up, in order to say a name at one of them — and
then walk back out and go to work in the morning.

Riddy asked him afterwards how many he thought would do it.

He said he had no idea, that he was probably the wrong person to ask as well, and that the
thing he did know was that the first forty would be the hardest and after that it would either
stop entirely or it would not stop at all.

---

Then he did the numbers, and this is the part Riddy has written down and reads back to people
when they are being optimistic.

Ninety feet of rock. No blasting anywhere near it, because nobody knows what the Nothing is
made of or what it does under shock. Hand tools, therefore, and men working short shifts
because of the air, and the shifts have to be dead men, because living men cannot be down
there long, and dead men tire in the way that the dead tire, which is slowly and then all at
once and without warning.

He said: two years, if nothing goes wrong.

Somebody asked what they had.

He said he had no idea what they had, that he was the wrong person to ask, and that from what
he had been told about the state of the reservoir and the temper of the man upstairs it was
very unlikely to be two years.

Nobody argued with him. Persephone asked whether there was a version that took eight months
and he said yes, there were several, and that all of them ended with ninety feet of rock
coming down on a working party or the river going somewhere it could not be got back from,
and that she was welcome to choose one.

They started on the Tuesday.

---

That is the sentence I would give you if you only took one out of this chapter, and I did not
understand it at the time and have understood it since, mostly because Riddy said it to me
plainly when I was being slow.

He told them it would take longer than they have. He was not being modest and he was not
angling for a bigger crew; he had been asked a technical question and he gave the technical
answer, which was that this cannot be finished in the time available.

And then he went out on the Tuesday morning with a lamp and a boy holding his instruments and
started it anyway.

Riddy says that is the only posture available to any of them. She said it flatly, as a fact
about the world rather than as a consolation, and went back to what she was doing.

---

I did nothing that week.

I want to be exact rather than self-pitying, because there is a version of this in which I am
being hard on myself and it would be false. I was present at the Frame conversation, which was
useful, since somebody had to bring the two of them into a room and I am one of about four
beings who can walk into both. I answered questions about the water. I carried two messages.

But when it came to the thing itself — ninety feet of rock, and a fall of about one in
forty, and whether the far end should be cut wide and stepped or narrow and dropped — there
was nothing in me that was any use whatsoever, and there was nothing available to any of us.

That is what I keep arriving at. Not that I was useless. That *we* were.

There is no god who can cut that channel. Zeus could collapse the roof of it in half a
second, and Poseidon could put a river anywhere he liked and could not tell you at what fall,
and Hades made the largest object in the underworld and cannot say what it is made of. Making
is not what any of them do. They command, and they transform, and they destroy, and the one
thing in the whole apparatus that was ever actually *built* was built by a mortal who charged
for it, and who is currently in a wet corridor two hundred feet under Asphodel with one hand,
telling a dead stonemason that his line is wandering.

I stood on the platform and watched them work for most of that first day.

It is not dramatic. Two men at the face and one behind with a basket, a change of shift every
forty minutes, and a long argument about the lamps that was still going when I left. By the
end of the day they had done perhaps four feet.

Four feet, of ninety, of a thing that will take longer than they have.

I would put it down as the last good day I had before the river and the desk and the man with
the pen, except that I did not know it was one at the time, and it does not become one until
later, which I am told is how they generally work.

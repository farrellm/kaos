# Chapter Thirty-Six

## Owed Nothing

I'll do this plainly and it isn't a triumph, and if it reads like one that's my fault and I'd
ask you to go back over it.

---

By the spring he was not frightening.

That's the part nobody who wasn't there believes, and I've stopped arguing about it. You have
in your head the man from the start of this — the garden, the rifle, the ball boys, the
kitten. That man ran out of the thing he was made of.

The Meander failed in the middle of a sentence in the winter, in front of five of his
children, and after that there was nothing to ration. He didn't shrink and he didn't wither
and there was no scene. He simply stopped being able to do anything. The weather went first,
then the travel, then whatever it is that had been keeping four thousand years off his face.
By the spring he was a very large old man in a house that had a lot of rooms in it, and the
thing you noticed was that he had started asking people what day it was.

Hera had Olympus by then and had discovered there was nothing in it, and had stayed anyway,
and would not go into the west wing. Poseidon was dead. Athena had taken over the running of
the household because somebody had to and nobody else would.

Nobody killed him. I want that stated, because it comes up.

---

Prometheus went up and asked him.

That was in the third week of the spring and it took him four days to get an answer, and I
wasn't there and neither was anyone else, and the account of it in the book is his and is
six lines long, which for him is nothing.

What he says is that he explained the arithmetic — what Zeus was made of, how much of it was
other people, what would happen to all of it if he simply stopped. And that Zeus listened to
all of it and asked one question, which was whether it would hurt, and he said he didn't know,
and Zeus said, "All right."

I've asked him twice whether that's really how it went, because it seems short. He says it is.
He says the argument he'd prepared was about four thousand years old and he never used any of
it, and that this is the third time that has happened to him and he has stopped taking it
personally.

---

There were two problems and neither of them was Zeus agreeing.

The first is that it doesn't work on the unwilling, and agreement is not the same as wanting.
You have to be going somewhere. A soul that has consented on paper and is not actually pointed
at anything is exactly the same as a soul out of the Nothing, which is to say there is nothing
in it for anyone to hold.

The second problem is that Zeus is not one soul.

I don't know how many. Nobody does. Kronos, and then everyone he took in the years he was
doing it by hand, and then four thousand years of drinking the rest. Hades had put it in the
hundreds of thousands, and then said the thing he always says, which is that he'd rather not
guess.

So the thing on the table was not: can a god be renewed. It was: what happens if you carry
something that size, and what comes out the other end, and does any of it go where it should.

Nobody could answer that. Daedalus was asked and said it wasn't an engineering question and
looked genuinely annoyed about it, which is the only time I've seen him decline to be useful.

---

He came down on the ferry like everybody else.

He walked to the port from the top on his own feet over about nine days, with Prometheus and
two of his own children who did not speak to him the entire way, and there is apparently a
version of this that people on Earth have already turned into something, and it wasn't
anything. It was a big old man walking slowly and stopping a lot.

Charon took him across.

I asked Charon afterwards what he'd said to him and he said, "Nothing. He's a fare."

Then he said that Zeus had tried to pay him and had nothing to pay with, and had gone through
his pockets twice in front of everybody, and that he'd told him to sit down, and that this had
taken about a minute and had been the worst minute of his four thousand years, and that he'd
be grateful if I didn't put that in.

I've put it in. He can take it out.

---

Right. The water.

It was in the drained end, where the channel comes in, where it's about knee-deep and moving
slowly, which is the same water they've been doing all of it in since the winter, because
there was never going to be a special place for this.

There were maybe sixty people there. It wasn't a ceremony and nobody organised it; it was the
shift that was on, plus whoever had heard.

Hera came.

Nobody expected her and nobody had asked her and she came down on the same ferry as the crew
change, which she must have arranged, because there is no other way to do it and not be
noticed. She stood at the back by the wall with her hands in front of her, and she did not
speak to anyone and nobody spoke to her, and when Medusa offered her a chair she said no thank
you.

She had had a hundred chances at him and taken none of them. She raised an army and took his
house, and by the spring she could have finished it any afternoon she liked with nobody to
stop her, and what she did instead was sit in Olympus for four months not going into the west
wing. I've never been able to make that fit and I've stopped trying to. I don't think it's
love. I don't think she'd say it was.

Prometheus went in with him.

And this is the part I'd been dreading writing, and I've decided the way to do it is to
just report what he said, because what he said was short.

He stood in front of him in the water and he said his own name. Then he said Zeus's. Then he
said that he had been born mortal in a place with a name that I'm not going to spell because
I have it three different ways in the book, and that his father was Kronos and had been a
psychopath, and that he had a brother who had been given death and a brother who had been
given the sea, and a wife who was also his sister and who was at that moment sitting in a room
in a house he built and would not go into the west wing.

And then he said: and when you were a boy your father ran over your dog with a cart, and you
loved her back to life, and she stayed with you the rest of her life, and that was the first
one, and you have never done it since.

That's it. That's what he said.

Prometheus told me later that he had not planned the last part and does not know where it came
from, and I believe him, because he plans everything and none of it ever works.

---

It took about four hours.

I'm not going to be able to give you what that looked like because it did not look like
anything at all for most of it. Two men standing in water. One of them very old and one of
them holding his shoulders from behind.

It came out slowly and it came out in the wrong order and it went into the river, and the
river took it out through the channel and into the room, which is where they all had to go
anyway, and there were people in there for the whole four hours doing the ordinary work with
the ordinary rota, saying names at whoever they'd come for, in the middle of it.

Some of what came back had been in there since before Troy.

I've been asked a lot whether it was beautiful. It was not beautiful. It was a very long
afternoon in a wet room and it smelled of the river, and about two hours in somebody's dog got
in and had to be got out, and everybody was extremely tired.

Hera left at about the third hour. She was the only one who did. She went up the steps at the
drained end without hurrying and without looking back, and I have not seen her since, and as
far as I know neither has anybody else in this account.

At the end there was one left.

They took him out and put him on the bank and he was breathing and he did not know anything at
all, and about four days later a woman on the north coast who is on Prue's list and has been
waiting since the summer had a child, and that is where it went, and nobody is ever going to
tell him, and nobody is going to check.

One life. Owed nothing to anybody, by anybody. That was the whole point and it took me a while
to see it: he doesn't get punished and he doesn't get forgiven, because there's nobody left in
him to do either to.

---

Caeneus came out of the water at about six.

He sat down on the edge of it, which he does now, and Prue brought him the tea he can't taste,
and I came over and sat next to him and asked him how he was.

And he was pleasant to me.

That's how you know. He's pleasant to strangers — properly, kindly, he's very good at it,
it's most of what the job ever was. He asked whether I'd come far. He said he was sorry, he
was a bit slow after, and had we met?

I said we had.

He said, "Sorry — you'll have to tell me again," and I said it was fine, and he said, "It
usually is," and drank the tea he can't taste, and asked me whether the dog belonged to
anybody.

---

Prometheus sat down in the water when it was finished and stayed there long enough that two
people went in to get him.

He hasn't said anything about it, to me or in the book, and I haven't asked. He was back on
the shift the next morning, standing in the river saying names at strangers with everybody
else, and has been most days since, and is not especially good at it.

---

I said I wasn't going to make a speech about it and I'm not.

I'll say two things that are facts.

The first is that I knew it was coming, from about the third week of the winter, and I had a
year to arrange myself and I did arrange myself, and it turns out that doesn't do anything. I
don't know what I expected it to do.

The second is that we finished the shift. There were four hundred and some still to bring
back from that afternoon alone and the rota doesn't care, so I got up and went and stood in
the water and said a name at somebody I'd never met, which is the job, and I did it badly for
about an hour and then I did it properly.

Prue walked back with me afterwards and told me at length about a problem with the lamps.

She knew exactly what she was doing. She'd have been doing it for sixty-one years.

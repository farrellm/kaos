# Chapter Twenty-Nine

## What We Did About It

It wasn't my idea and I want that clear before I start, because I've noticed that when you
tell a thing you end up in the middle of it whether you were or not, and I wasn't. I carried
messages. I was up top for most of it.

It was Prue's.

---

Here's the situation, quickly.

There's a steel door on the Nothing with Hermes sitting in front of it, and Caeneus can't get
near the river, and the channel Daedalus is cutting is at fifty-one feet of ninety and won't
be through for a year even if nothing falls in.

And underneath all that there's the thing nobody upstairs has ever once thought about, which
is that the underworld is a workplace and the workforce is us.

The dead run it. All of it. The handlers at the port are unresolved. The divers are
unresolved. The people on the turnstiles, the canteen, the dogs, the boats, the laundry, the
lot. There are eleven gods and about six hundred people who work directly for them and
somewhere north of two hundred thousand of us, and we have been doing every job in that
building for as long as there has been a building, in exchange for a two-hundred-year
sentence at the end of which nothing happens.

Nobody had ever added it up like that. Prue added it up.

---

She did it the way she does everything, which is with a list.

I'd told her about the Frame in the spring — she'd have got there anyway, everyone in Asphodel
had it by then, but I'm the one who told her, and she went quiet for a bit and then said,
"Right, well, that's the two hundred years gone, then," and I said yes, and she said, "So
what have they actually got?"

And I said I didn't understand the question.

And she said: what have they got. What is the threat. She'd been down there sixty-one years.
She couldn't be killed, she was already dead. She couldn't be denied Renewal, there wasn't
any. She couldn't be sacked, because where would she go. She said all this without any heat
at all, in the voice she uses about the urn.

Then she said the only thing they've got is the Frame, and that they'd have to be very stupid
to use it, and that she'd like to make a list.

The list took her nine days. Sector by sector, name by name, who'd stop and who wouldn't and
who'd say they would and then not. She's been down there sixty-one years and knows everybody
and everybody likes her, and it turns out that being the person who does the chairs for
sixty-one years is a qualification.

She had nine thousand names before she told anyone she was doing it.

---

They stopped on a Tuesday morning.

Not everyone. About forty per cent, which Prue had said was the number and which is why the
next part happened the way it did.

The port went first because the port is where the arithmetic is most obvious: a ferry comes
in every four hours whether anyone meets it or not. By the second day there were fourteen
thousand newly dead standing on a dock with nobody to tell them anything and no dogs and no
lines, and by the fourth day there were forty thousand and they were on the road up to the
turnstiles, and the ferry kept coming, because dying doesn't stop.

I want to be accurate about what a strike in the underworld looks like, because it isn't
barricades. It's a dock. It's a very large number of people standing on a dock being
frightened, and a smaller number of people standing off to one side deliberately not helping
them, and that is much harder to do than fighting and about four of the people I know best
found they couldn't do it and went back to work and were not judged for it by anyone except
themselves.

Adrian could do it. Adrian stood at the edge of the dock for six days and talked to anyone who
came up to him and told them the truth — all of it, the Frame, the Nothing, the lot — and told
them nobody was going to make them go anywhere, and that he was very sorry, and that they
should sit down because it was going to be a while.

Forty thousand people got the truth from a man who wasn't allowed to help them.

---

On the sixth day they started putting people through the Frame.

I'm going to do this bit plainly. Zeus had taken the City of the Dead at the curtain and it was
his, and he had somewhere between four and six hundred people down there who worked for him
directly, and he told them to clear the dock, and clearing the dock means one thing, and they
did it.

They didn't start with the newly dead. They started with the strikers, because it's a
demonstration and because a demonstration has to be aimed at somebody.

They took Adrian on the sixth day, in the afternoon, off the edge of the dock, in front of
about nine hundred people.

He'd have been forty-two years dead in the November.

---

Right, so — no. I said in the last chapter I wasn't going to do the thing he did, and I'm not
going to sit here and build him a paragraph. He'd have hated it. He was a fussy, kind,
extremely boring man who explained the salt to me on my third day dead and who gave away a
wife of forty-one years so she could be someone else's baby, and there isn't a sentence that
improves that.

What I'll say is what happened next, which is the only part that matters and is also, if you
want it, the eulogy.

Nine hundred people watched it. And then they did not disperse.

That's it. That's the whole turn. They didn't rush the guards and they didn't fight, because
you can't fight people who can put you in the Frame and there's no version of that anyone
wins. They stood there. And then the ones who'd gone back to work in the first week came out
and stood there too, and by the evening there were about nineteen thousand people on that
dock and the road above it, not doing anything, and the four to six hundred stopped, because
there is no procedure for it.

You cannot process nineteen thousand people who are standing still. You cannot even begin.
It takes about ninety seconds a soul and there is one Frame and it is at the other end of the
underworld, and the entire apparatus for moving people to it depends on those people walking
there themselves, which is why there are divers, which is a thing Medusa has been saying since
before I died and which nobody upstairs ever heard as anything except a staffing note.

Nobody can be forced. It's the oldest rule in the building. They built the whole system on
it and then forgot it was load-bearing.

---

People ask me about Hades and Persephone and I've stopped enjoying the question.

They did nothing. Publicly, for twenty-three days, the god of death and his wife did
absolutely nothing while their entire workforce stopped and their brother's men put a
forty-one-year man through the Frame off the edge of a dock.

And they were right to, and I've had a year to get comfortable with that and I'm not.

Because the moment Zeus knows the management is with us, they're gone — he takes the
underworld the way he took the cities, and he puts one of the ones who came back in that
chair, and then it isn't Persephone signing the diver rosters, it's somebody who wants to
please him. Everything we have runs through those two staying exactly where they are. She
said that to me herself, in about nine words, and then went and did her job for
twenty-three days in a building where nothing was working.

What they did do was not stop it. There's a version of this where the underworld's own
management breaks a strike in its own house and nobody upstairs even hears about it, and
that version was available every single day, and they didn't take it.

I know that's the same thing as doing nothing. I know. I've done the argument.

---

It took another nine days and I'm not going to give you all of them.

The short version: the guards held the door and everything else stopped. No port, no
turnstiles, no canteen, no boats. Hermes sat in his chair in front of the Nothing with his
list and had, by the end, nothing to do but be extremely polite to a corridor with four
thousand people in it.

They didn't do anything to him. That was Prue's instruction and she was rigid about it — no
one touches him, no one shouts at him, nobody so much as blocks his way to the lavatory,
because the moment somebody puts a hand on a god this becomes a story about that instead.

So they just filled the corridor. In shifts. With a rota.

I've thought about that a lot, the rota. Two hundred thousand people whose entire experience
of organisation was being allocated an appropriate sector, running the largest sit-down in the
history of anything, off a clipboard, in forty-minute reliefs, because the woman with the
clipboard has spent sixty-one years finding out who's reliable.

On the twelfth day Hermes stood up, folded his chair, said, "This is silly," to nobody in
particular, and walked out through them. They let him past. Somebody said good night to him
and he said good night back.

He hasn't come back and as far as anyone knows nobody has asked him to.

---

Caeneus walked down that corridor about an hour later.

That's what it was for. Not the door and not the garrison and not Olympus. Twenty-three days
of it, and Adrian, and four thousand people standing in a wet passage in reliefs, so that one
man could get to the water.

He didn't make a speech either. He asked Prue whether her feet hurt, and she said they did,
and he said his did too, which was a lie, because he can't feel his feet and hasn't been able
to since the spring, and I don't think Prue knew that.

Then he went in.

---

But the door wasn't what mattered and it took us weeks to work that out.

Here's what mattered. Adrian stood on the edge of that dock for six days and told forty
thousand newly dead people the truth, and then the numbers kept coming, and other people took
over doing it, and by the end of the Stoppage something like a hundred and ten thousand people
had come off those ferries and been told, plainly, on arrival, what the Frame is.

Almost none of them went in.

Not out of defiance — a lot of them were furious, but that's not it. They just wouldn't get in
the water. You can't be forced and there's nobody to persuade you and the one person who
walks up to you at the rail says: don't.

Which means that for twenty-three days, and for every day since, the thing at the other end of
the Frame got nothing.

We didn't plan that. I want to be honest about it because it's the single most important thing
that has happened since I died and it was a side effect of an old man being kind to strangers
on a dock. Everybody was watching the door. The supply had already stopped.

They noticed upstairs in the third week. I know exactly when, because Prometheus came down
looking like he'd been slapped and asked me who had authorised it, and I said authorised what,
and he said the interdiction, and I said the what.

There wasn't one. There was Adrian.

---

One more thing and then I'll get on.

A fortnight later somebody asked me what we were calling it, because it had got into the songs
by then and Orpheus needed a word for it, which tells you something about how information
moves now.

I said I didn't know. I asked Prue.

Prue said she hadn't thought about it and that she'd been calling it the stoppage, in her
head, because that's what it was, and then she asked whether that was all right or whether it
needed to be something better.

It's in the books now as the Stoppage. Two hundred thousand people, twenty-three days, one
man through a door, and it's named after the thing a woman with a clipboard called it when she
wasn't thinking about it.

I like that more than anything else that happened that year.

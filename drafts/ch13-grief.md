# Chapter Thirteen

## Grief

This chapter is not important and I am including it for completeness.

I want to be straightforward with you about that, because we are about to move into the
part of this account where a great many things happen at once and I would rather not have
you carrying anything unnecessary. What follows is an afternoon in which nothing was
achieved, nobody was moved, and no decision was altered. I went to see an old friend. It
did not go especially well. Then I went back to work, and the work is what matters, and the
work resumes in the next chapter.

I include it because I have promised you the whole of it, and because a man who only tells
you the successful afternoons is not telling you anything.

---

I wrote the speech on the ninth day.

That is not a figure of speech; I mean that I sat down on a rock that was no longer holding
me and composed it, out loud, the way you would compose anything you intended to survive.
Four thousand years is an unusual amount of preparation for six minutes of talking, and I
had used all of it. I had drafts. I had a version that opened with the arithmetic and a
version that opened with the apology, and I had gone with the arithmetic, because I have
always believed that a man who apologizes first is a man asking to be interrupted.

I will give you the shape of it, since you will want to be able to recognize it later.

I would say that I had loved him. I would say that I had known exactly what I was taking
and had taken it anyway, and what I had bought with it — the end of Zeus, the emptying of
the dark, every soul ever swallowed handed back — and then I would say the line I was
proudest of, the one I had been carrying up and down that plateau for longer than there
have been countries: *the arithmetic had been monstrous, but it had been arithmetic.*

And then I would say: I chose who paid, and I chose you.

You are permitted to find that vain. I have found it vain myself, on and off, for a very
long time. But I would ask you to notice that it is also true, every word, and that a man
can be vain about a true thing. Most of us are. It is nearly the only kind of vanity
available to the honest.

I went down on the eleventh day.

---

The port was busy. I had expected — I don't know what I had expected. Something quieter.
Something with a bit of ceremony in it. What there was instead was four hundred people in
five lines and a woman on a public-address system asking them not to stroke the dogs, and
a smell of wet rope, and a queue for the turnstiles that went back past the awning and out
of sight round the containers.

He was at the desk. He has always been at the desk. He was doing the thing with the
manifest where he makes a small mark for every fifty and a longer one for every hundred,
and I stood and watched him do it for a while before he saw me, and I want to record — I am
aware this is sentiment and I am putting it in anyway — that his hands have not changed.
Four thousand years at a desk in the dark and he still holds a pen like a man about to
sign for something.

There was a dog under the desk. Old, grey about the face, asleep on its side with its feet
out. I knew whose it was. I knew the whole of that story, the boy and the port and the
promise to visit, and I stood there and thought, *there is the dog*, and filed it, and I
will come back to the dog.

He looked up.

He looked at me for slightly longer than a man looks at a thing he was not expecting, and
then he looked back down at the manifest and made a mark.

I said his name.

He made another mark.

---

Now. I have told you a great many difficult things in this account and I have generally
told them at speed, so let me be equally quick here.

He did not speak to me. Not for the whole of it. I stood at that desk for the better part
of an hour, in the noise, with the lines going through and the dogs working and a man
somewhere behind me explaining to a woman that there had been no mistake, and I said —
some of it. Not the speech. You cannot deliver a speech to a man who is doing paperwork;
the speech requires that the other party stop, and he did not stop, and after about ten
minutes I understood that he was not going to and that the speech was going to have to wait
for a better afternoon.

So I said the small things instead. I asked about the knee. I said that I had seen the dog
and had wondered. I said that I was free, which he could see, and that it had been eleven
days, which he had not asked. I said that the arrangement I had spent my imprisonment
constructing was now moving on its own and that a number of things he would want to know
about were about to happen underneath him, and I offered — I did offer — to tell him what
they were.

He made a mark for every fifty and a longer mark for every hundred.

At one point a handler came up with a query about a shipment of coinless out of a city I
did not catch the name of, and he answered her, at length, pleasantly, with a joke in it,
and she laughed and went away, and then he went back to the manifest and back to not
speaking to me, and I understood that the silence was not a general condition of the
afternoon. It had been prepared. It was for me. It had my name on it the way a chair at a
table has a name on it.

That was the worst part and I am putting it in the middle, where I put the bad ones, because
I have learned that a reader takes them better there.

---

He said one thing.

I had given up by then. I had got to the stage of leaving, which with me takes some time,
and I had said something magnanimous about coming back when he was less busy — I would like
to strike that from the record and cannot — and I had my hand on the edge of the desk.

He put the pen down.

And he said: "You haven't asked me anything."

That was all of it. He picked the pen back up. Somewhere out on the water the four o'clock
came in with its lamp lit and the lines began to move and I went home.

---

Right.

Let me tell you what that was, because you have been sitting with it for a page and a half
and I do not intend to leave you speculating when I have the answer.

It was grief.

He has had four thousand years alone with what I did to him, and no way to put it down,
and no one to put it down in front of. Grief that old stops being a feeling and becomes a
kind of furniture — it is simply in the room, you walk round it, you stop mentioning it to
guests. And then the man who made it walks up to your desk on an ordinary Tuesday and
starts talking about arithmetic.

Of course he would not look at me. Of course he had a sentence ready, and of course it was
an accusation, and of course it was about form — you didn't ask, you never ask, you arrive
and you announce. That is what the injured say to the people who injured them. I have heard
humans say a version of it to each other on riverbanks for as long as there have been
riverbanks, and it means: *you are still doing the thing you did.*

He was not telling me there was something to ask.

He was telling me I am the sort of man who does not ask.

And he was right, and I sat with that on the way back up, and I want you to know that I did
sit with it, because I am aware of how I come across and I would not want you thinking I
went home pleased with myself. I did not. I went home and I thought about a man's hands and
a sleeping dog and I felt, for about an hour, entirely without excuse.

Then I got up, and I went back to work, because I had eleven days of freedom behind me and
a very great deal to arrange, and because — and this is the sentence I would ask you to
keep, since it is the truest one in the chapter — grief is not information. It is weather.
You wait for it and it passes and the thing underneath it is unchanged.

You may put the afternoon out of your mind. I did.

---

So.

Persephone is about to stand up in a room in the underworld and say out loud what the rest
of them have only been circling, and once a thing like that is said out loud it is real and
it has a name and it can be planned against and defended and lost. That is where we are
going. That is the next chapter and it is the beginning of the second half of this.

I knew it was coming. I had known since the rock, and in a longer sense for rather longer
than that, and I had positioned three human beings and a ferryman and a queen of the dead
so that when it was said there would be someone in the room to say it.

The speech keeps, by the way.

I did not change a word of it, in all the time that followed. Not one. I want that noted
here, at the bottom of an unimportant chapter, where you will not think much of it.

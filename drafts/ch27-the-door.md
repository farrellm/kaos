# Chapter Twenty-Seven

## The Door

There is a door on the Nothing now.

There was never a door. That is the thing I keep going back to and I cannot make it sit
still. For four thousand years the worst place that has ever existed was a doorway in a wall
in a corridor, and anyone who wandered down there could walk into it, and two dead people
did, on an ordinary morning, following the sound of a boy shouting. It was not guarded
because it was not thought of. It was the back of the building.

It has a door now. Steel, industrial, on a runner, with a wheel on it. It took nine days to
put in and the noise of the fitting carried the whole length of the platform at the Lethe,
so the queues heard it going in, day after day, and nobody told them what it was.

Hermes is on it.

---

He has a chair and a small table and a list, and he sits with his feet up on the wheel and
checks names against the list, and there is no reason for the list, because nobody is
permitted through it at all. He has said so himself, pleasantly, several times a day, to
people who are not asking to go through it. The list is so that he has something in his
hands.

He is the god of crossings. Roads, borders, thresholds, the conducting of the dead from one
condition into another. Four thousand years of that, and a great deal of it done well by
every account including his own, and he did not answer the telephone for the last stretch of
it, and now he is here, in a chair, with his feet on a wheel, because his father called and
this time he came.

He is perfectly friendly. That has been the surprise. He asks after people. He remembered
that one of the handlers had a thing with her shoulder and asked about it, twice, and meant
it both times.

---

Caeneus came down on the second day, before the fitting was finished, when it was still just
a frame and a tarpaulin and six of them working.

I was on the platform. I am on the platform a great deal now; there is nowhere else that I
particularly need to be, and the trains are warm.

He came down with Medusa, and Medusa did the talking, because he had been told to let her,
and she was very good — she was reasonable, she was brisk, she quoted the establishment's own
regulations back at the foreman, she got as far as the phrase *operational necessity* — and
about four minutes into it Hermes came out from behind the tarpaulin with the list under his
arm and said, "Oh, hello. You're the one."

And Caeneus said yes.

And Hermes said, "Right, no. Sorry," and went back behind the tarpaulin.

That is the whole of it. That is the entire engagement. A dead man walked the length of the
underworld to get to the only place where the thing he can do is worth doing, and the answer
took four words and one of them was an apology, and it was not even unkind. Nobody raised
anything. Nobody was struck. Hermes came back out about a minute later with a folding chair,
which he offered to Medusa, who did not take it.

Eleven days now, since he last got near the water.

---

I have watched him in those eleven days and I am not going to be clever about what I have
seen.

He works. There is nothing at the river to work at, so he has found other work — he is at the
port most mornings, doing what he did for ten years, which he is still extremely good at, and
in the afternoons he sits with the ones Medusa has brought in and he talks to them, which is
the actual job and always was. He is patient. He is funny in the way that makes people who
have just died feel less like a case.

And he is losing it in a straight line, and it is not slowing down.

The dog went last week. Not the animal — the animal is asleep under a desk at the port with
its feet out, and Caeneus walks past it every morning, and last Tuesday he asked whose it
was.

He asked me. I told him.

I want to put down what that is like, and I find that I can't, and I am going to leave the
space where the description should be rather than fill it with something that sounds well.

---

Zeus is very well.

He is up early. He is across his own correspondence for the first time in centuries. He has
a manner now that I have not seen on him in four thousand years and which I have been trying
to find the word for since Thursday, and the word is *unbothered*. He does not check his
forehead. He does not ask about the Meander because he no longer has to; the supply is a
solved problem and he solved it, and there is a mechanism in place, and there was a family
occasion this month at which he carved.

His brother's wife is not speaking to him. His wife is in the north with an army that has now
tried once and failed. There is one fewer of his children than there was three weeks ago and
he has not mentioned it, and neither has anyone else, and the ones who came back have stayed
back, and one of them is sitting on a chair in a corridor with a list of names of people who
cannot go anywhere.

Everything I ever said about him is still true. He is a frightened man. He is his father. He
is a bully in a garden with a peach.

And he has won the month.

---

Right.

This is the point in the account where I tell what happens next.

That is the arrangement. It has been the arrangement since the first page — I go slightly
ahead, and come back, and lay it down in order so that it can be borne. I have done it about
a boy at a river and a girl in a road and a man with a gun on a beach, and I did it well, and
I did it in the past tense because the past tense is what a man speaks in when he already
knows how it comes out.

So.

The door is on the Nothing and Hermes is on the door. The reservoir is a garrison. The one
capacity in existence that can empty it is eleven days from the water and forgetting itself in
order, backwards, and the machine that made all this is being run competently for the first
time since it was built, by a man who is sleeping well.

And what happens next is —

I don't know.

I have sat with that for some time before writing it down. It is not modesty and it is not a
device; I have used the device, I know exactly what the device is worth, and this is not it.
I do not know what happens next. I do not know whether the door comes off. I do not know
whether he gets back to the river, or whether there is anything left of him by the time he
does, or whether the thing he can do turns out to matter at all against the size of what is
in there. I have no information. I have no instruction. There is nobody I can ask who has not
already told me everything they were ever going to tell me.

Somebody is going to have to say what happens next.

It is not going to be me.

# Chapter Nineteen

## Distribution

Early in this account I said three sentences about Orpheus.

I said that he was useful. I said that an ego like that has no trouble accepting the notion of
a cosmic purpose. And I said that it has no suspicion that the purpose might be co-opted.

I was pleased with those. I remember being pleased with them, in the moment, on a rock, while
a man I had never met was being driven towards a bin by a god who had adopted him as a hobby.
They are efficient, they are true of a great many people, and they have the particular
quality I have always prized in my own sentences, which is that they cannot be argued with by
the person they are about.

I am going to leave them exactly where they are, unrevised, because the revising is the
chapter.

---

He came out of the ground in the summer with nothing, and he had a car with no fuel in it and
a wife who had thanked him and got out, and for about three months he did nothing whatsoever
that I could see.

I checked, is the thing. Not often. He was not on my list of live concerns — he had done his
part, he had been the door, and the door had opened, and I had assumed him spent in the way
you assume a spent thing. But I went past occasionally, the way you do, and what was there was
a man in his mother's house in Krete who had stopped playing.

Calli — who books him, and has booked him since he was nineteen, and whose relation to him I
have never established and have decided at this late stage not to ask about — told Riddy that
he did not touch it for three months. Not once, not to tune it. That this had never happened in
his life, not when his father died.

Then in the autumn he went to a room above a bar in a town on the north coast that holds
about sixty people and does not have a name worth putting down, and he played for two hours,
and what he played was what had happened.

---

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this sound like a decision and it
was not one, and everybody involved is clear that it was not one.

He did not write a protest. He is not capable of a protest; he has never in his life had an
opinion that lasted a week. What he had was the thing he has always had, which is that he
cannot hold anything privately, and he had spent three months holding the largest thing anybody
has ever been given to hold.

So it came out the way everything comes out of him, which is arranged, in a key, with the
awful accuracy he brings to a feeling.

The coin. The water that would not rise for her because he had made sure it wouldn't. The two
hundred years, and what a canteen looks like, and what the queue looks like from the wrong
side of it. The place under the world with the people standing in it. What Hades did with his
hands.

He named it. That is the part nobody expected and the part that could not be undone
afterwards. He did not sing about a dark place under the world; he sang the word Nothing, and
he sang it in a way that made it a location rather than an absence, and after that it had a
name that sixty people in a room above a bar could take home with them.

I have thought a great deal about whether he understood what he was doing. My conclusion is
that he understood it about as well as a river understands a valley.

---

The room above the bar held sixty. Nine days later he did a courtyard in Kydonia that held
four hundred and there were about a thousand, and the ones outside made the ones inside pass
it back.

Sparta arrested him in the first week of winter.

The charge was blasphemy, which has been on the books everywhere for as long as there have
been books, and which is ordinarily used about twice a year on somebody drunk. They held him
for nine days. Riddy's book has the detail and I have checked it independently because it
strains belief: on the fourth day the guards asked him to sing it. In the cells. And he did,
and they let him, and one of them wept, and then that guard went home and put a coin in a
drawer for his mother, which I am putting in here because it is going to matter later and I
did not think anything of it at the time.

They released him because the town outside the barracks had grown to about six thousand and
was singing the thing at the walls, and had learned it from people who had never been to the
courtyard in Kydonia, which meant it was already out and the only remaining question was
whether Sparta wanted to be the city that had a martyr.

Athens arrested him at the end of winter.

I want that recorded plainly, because Ari has spent this entire story citing Athens as the
place where they vote and it works, and the Athenians voted, and what they voted was to hold
him. It was not close. The blasphemy statutes there are older than the assembly and the
assembly declined to touch them, and a democracy that has been buying its own myths for six
hundred years is not more resistant to this than a client kingdom; it is differently
resistant, which is to say it takes longer and is harder to reverse.

They held him nine days and released him for the same reason Sparta did.

Thebes was the third and Thebes did not bother with a charge. They picked him up at a
crossroads, drove him sixty miles, and put him out on the road facing away from the city,
which is a thing they had apparently done before to somebody and is, as an administrative
act, almost admirable.

He walked back. Obviously he walked back. He played in Thebes eight days later to what may
have been fifteen thousand people, and Thebes did nothing at all, having worked out what the
other two worked out, which is that there is no version of arresting this man that does not
end with more people knowing.

---

And that is the distribution network.

Not a metaphor. It is the resistance's only means of moving the truth at scale and it consists
of one man with a guitar and no security, moving between towns on a schedule he does not
publish, in front of audiences that reconstruct the material for him in the next town before
he gets there.

Nobody built it. Nobody funds it. Nobody can direct it, and several people have tried; I
know of at least two occasions on which somebody sensible went to him with a suggestion about
emphasis, and he was pleasant about it and did not do it, and could not have done it, because
he does not work that way and never has.

---

It is her life.

I did not think of that for a month and neither, as far as I can tell, did anybody else
except the person it belongs to.

Everything he sings is hers. The coin is hers. The water that would not rise is hers, and the
two hundred years, and the queue, and what she saw in the Nothing standing next to a man she
had known for a fortnight. He was not in the Nothing. He was never in Asphodel. Nearly all of
the material he is moving across three countries at the rate of a city a week is testimony
from a woman who told it to him once, in a room, in the underworld, in the worst hour of her
death, while she was explaining why she was not coming home with him.

He did not ask her. It has never occurred to him to ask her, and I want to be accurate: it is
not that he considered it and decided against. There is no moment at which the question
formed.

She found out from a member of Ari's staff who was being kind, and who assumed she would be
pleased.

What she did about it was nothing, and she did the nothing on purpose, and I have had the
reasoning from her once, flatly, and she has not raised it since. It is moving. Nothing else
is moving. She has stood on a crate in a square in Heraklion and been agreed with by eleven
people, and he can put the same material into fifteen thousand in a night and have them sing
it back at him, and the arithmetic is not close and she is not sentimental.

But there is a cost and she has named it precisely, so I will use her words rather than mine.
She said: "It's his now. In any town where he's played it, I can't be the one who says it.
They've already heard it, and they heard it better."

The one thing she has instead of a life is that she is the person who tells this, and he did
not take it from her deliberately, and it is gone.

I file this here, in a chapter about how well the plan is going, because it belongs in it.

I had assumed, for the whole of the previous year, that the useful part of Orpheus was that
he had been a door. A one-time mechanism. I had assumed that his vanity would make him
biddable and that his grief would make him tired, and that between the two of them he would go
home to Krete and be a famous man who had been through something.

Both halves of that were wrong, and they were wrong in a way I would like to be exact about,
because it is not that I was insufficiently generous towards him.

---

*An ego like that has no trouble accepting the notion of a cosmic purpose.*

He never accepted one. Nobody offered him one. That is the entire problem with the sentence,
and I did not see it for a year because I was the person in the story who hands out cosmic
purposes and I could not conceive of the work being done by somebody who had not been issued
with one.

He was not recruited. He is not in anybody's plan, including mine, and there is no version
of the last eight months in which he was told what to do and did it. He went to a room above a
bar in a town with no name because he could not hold it any more, and everything after that is
consequence.

*And no suspicion that the purpose might be co-opted.*

There is nothing to co-opt. You cannot co-opt a man who is not carrying an instruction. I had
built that sentence as a compliment to myself — it is a sentence about *my* competence, about
how neatly a vain man can be steered — and the thing it describes never happened, because the
one human being in this story who has moved the truth further than anyone else did it under
his own power, out of guilt, for a woman who has already left him and is not coming back.

I called it an ego.

I have gone looking for a better word for about a month now and I have not found one, and I
notice that what I am actually looking for is a word that lets me have been right.

---

I went to one.

Late winter, between Sparta and Athens, in a town whose square holds perhaps two thousand and
had rather more. I stood at the back, which is the only place I ever stand now, and I watched
a man do the thing he does.

It is very good. I want that on the record from someone with no reason to flatter him: it is
extraordinarily good, and it is not good in the way a protest is good, it is good in the way
weather is good, and there is no defence against it because it does not argue with anything.

Two thousand people learned the shape of the largest crime in the world in ninety minutes and
wept and knew every word by the third repetition.

And then it finished, and they went home.

I watched them go. I stood in a doorway and watched a square empty for about twenty minutes,
and they went home in the ordinary way, talking, some of them still singing it, and I do not
know what I had expected. Torches, possibly. Something with a direction.

I decided at the time that it was early days.

I did not go up to him afterwards. He was standing by a wall with about forty people around
him and I told myself that it was not the moment and that I had nothing to bring him, which
was true and was not the reason.

He was signing somebody's arm when I left. He was doing it badly and at length, the way he
does everything, and the woman whose arm it was had her eyes shut.

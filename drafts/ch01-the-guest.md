# Chapter One

## The Guest

I want to begin with a correction, because if I do not make it now I will have to make it
later, and later it will look like an excuse.

You have been told — by me, at some length, in a tone I stand by — that I was a prisoner.
That the whole of my situation was a rock, a bird, and an arrangement of chains that had
been personally supervised. You were told this because it was true and because it was
useful, and I would like you to notice that those are two different reasons and that I gave
you only the first one.

Here is what happened when the chains came off.

Nothing.

Not *nothing yet*. Not nothing-for-now, in the way a story tells you nothing when it means
wait. I mean that I was free for the first time since your species learned to bury its dead,
and the world took no notice whatsoever, and by the end of the afternoon I was sitting down
to a drink with the man I had spent four thousand years arranging to destroy, and he was
pleased to see me.

Let me take it in order. I am good at order. It is nearly all I have.

---

The Fates went into the fire and came out of it. You saw that. Lachesis told me to enjoy my
freedom and said that what I did with it was up to me, which I heard at the time as a
blessing, in the way that a man who has been holding his breath hears anything at all as
permission to stop.

Then they were gone and I was standing on the plateau with my arms down.

I want to tell you about my arms, and then I will not mention my body again, because there
is nothing more tedious than a man discovering he has one. For four thousand years my arms
were up. That was simply the fact of them; that was their profession. When the weight went,
they did not drop. They stayed where they were, out to the sides, like a man conducting
something, and I stood on that rock in the wind holding a shape that no longer had anything
to hold it up, and it took a measurable amount of time before it occurred to me to lower
them.

That is the first thing freedom did. It made me look like an idiot on a hill.

The eagle came at the usual hour. I want to record this because I have not told anyone and I
find I would like to have told someone. It came in low from the east the way it always did,
and it saw that the chains were gone and I was standing loose, and it made one pass, quite
close, close enough that I felt the air off it — and then it went away.

It did not attack. It did not scream. It rearranged its business and left, the way a postman
leaves a house where nobody lives now.

I had assumed, for four thousand years, that it hated me.

It had been coming to work.

---

The house was open. It is always open; there is nothing in the world that can get into it
except the things that live there, and one of those had just done considerably more damage
to it than an army would have.

I want to give you the room as I found it, because I have thought about that room a great
deal since and I have never once thought about it without noticing something new.

The bees were still burning. Not the bees — they were gone, they had gone in a moment, they
went the way things go when Zeus decides. What was still burning was the ground where they
had been kept, a low black shape past the terrace with the smoke coming off it flat in the
wind, and the smell of it was in the house and would be in the house for a week. There was a
peacock walking about in that smoke as though nothing had occurred, which is the most
accurate thing I can tell you about Olympus.

Inside there were two ball boys with a dustpan and a bucket, and a smear on the marble that
they were working on with the concentration of men doing surgery, and one of them was
crying, which the other one kept quietly telling him to stop, because there is no crying
about a cat where he might see you.

The bottles were on the table in a row. Six of them. One was half.

And in the middle of it, calling out for his family in an empty building, was the king of
the gods.

*Dionysus. Hera. Where'd everybody go?*

I said: in here.

---

He came round the corner and he saw me — off the rock, out of the chains, standing in his
house without permission for the first time in the history of the world — and I watched his
face do the whole thing, and I am going to tell you honestly what was on it, because I have
had every subsequent day of my life to wish it had been something else.

He was startled for about a second and a half.

Then he was delighted.

*What are you doing here?* he said. *I did not summon you.*

And I said the line I had been building for four thousand years, the one I had carried up
through all of it, the one I had said to the inside of my own skull on the days when the
bird was slow — I told him that he had been right. That it had not been a dream. That it was
a vision.

I let it land.

I want you to understand the craft of it. I had chosen the words for weight and I had chosen
the moment for maximum injury and I had come down off that rock with one purpose, which was
to stand in front of him unchained and tell him that the thing he feared most was true and
was already in the building. It was, I still maintain, an extremely good line.

He looked at me for a moment.

Then he said, *Well — have a drink.*

---

He got the bendy straw out of the drawer himself.

You remember the straw. Gold, articulated, faintly ridiculous; he had produced it for me
years ago with enormous pride because it matched the pattern on my — he called it my Grecian
key, he meant a bracelet that was part of the restraint, and he had never once in four
thousand years understood why I did not find the coordination charming.

He put it in the glass. He put the glass in front of me. He pulled out a chair and he
patted it, twice, the way you do for a dog you are fond of.

And he said, *Sit down. Sit. You look terrible. Sit.*

So I sat down.

I would like to skip forward now. I am aware that a better arranged account would skip
forward, and I am, as you may have gathered, extremely well arranged. But I have decided to
tell you about the next hour because everything that happens in this book happens because of
it, and if I hide it here I will only have to produce it later at a worse moment.

He talked. He talked for an hour.

He told me he had solved it. He said the word *solved* four times. He had located the
problem — and the problem, it turned out, had been fate itself, the whole apparatus, three
women in a poolroom holding the world hostage with paperwork — and he had gone down there
and he had settled it personally, and now there were no more prophecies, and therefore no
more prophecy, and therefore no more line, no more waning, no more falling, no more of the
other thing.

He asked me whether I had heard about it.

I said I had heard something.

He asked me whether I had *seen* it.

And here is the first lie of the new era, and I would like it noted that it took me less
than an hour of liberty to tell it. I said no.

I had seen it. I had seen all three of them standing in the ash afterwards, perfectly well,
mildly entertained, and I had been freed by one of them with a joke about waiting. I knew
that the single fact upon which he had rebuilt his entire understanding of his own safety
was false, and I sat at his table and drank his stolen souls through a novelty straw and I
told him no.

Why?

I have given several answers to this over the years. The one I gave myself at the time was
strategy — that a man who believes he is safe stops looking, and I needed him to stop
looking. That is a good answer. It is even mostly true, and I invite you to keep it.

I will give you the other one when I can.

---

Then he asked me to look at his forehead.

He came round the table and crouched down in front of my chair, this enormous ruinous
creature folding himself up so that a chained man would not have to move, and he put his
face into the light and he said: *There. Is there another one? Tell me there isn't another
one.*

There was another one.

It was above the first and slightly across, finer, the way the second crack in a bowl is
always finer than the first and always means the bowl is finished. I looked at it for
somewhat longer than I needed to.

I told him it was the light.

He stood up and laughed and hit me on the shoulder hard enough to move the chair, and said
that this was why he kept me, and went off to shout at the ball boys about the smell.

And I sat there with my hand around a glass, and I understood the thing I have come to
regard as the true beginning of all this, and I am going to state it plainly and then I am
going to move on quickly, in the way one does:

He was not afraid of me.

Not because he had considered the danger and dismissed it. Because it had never assembled.
In four thousand years of speaking to me every week — of confessing to me, weeping at me,
asking me whether he was likeable — he had never once constructed the sentence *this man is
my enemy*, and the chains had not been what prevented him from constructing it, and
therefore the chains coming off did not cause him to construct it.

I had spent four thousand years assuming I was contained.

I was accommodated.

---

Now.

I will not have you leaving this chapter with that. It is accurate and it is not the whole
picture, and I have never yet let you go away with a piece of something.

Because while I sat in that chair being fond of, three things were happening that he did not
know about and could not have found if he had looked, and I want them in front of you before
we go any further, since everything from here is these three things arriving at each other.

**On a road outside a welded bin**, a young woman who had been dead a fortnight was standing
in the dark with a prophet, being handed the only job in the world that nobody has ever
completed.

**On a riverbank under the world**, a dead man was holding his mother, who was breathing, and
the god of death was on his knees in the mud in front of them asking a question that he — the
god, the professional, the one who built the machine — did not have the answer to.

**And in a walled compound in the north**, a woman with two bags and no retinue was walking
up to a door she had not knocked on in three hundred years, behind which was a son, and
behind him, standing in ranks in the cold and waiting to be asked, was every soldier her
husband had ever failed to visit.

That was the afternoon. That was all of it, running at once, and none of them knew about the
others, and I knew about all three, and I want to be very clear with you that at the time I
considered this to be the same thing as being in charge of them.

I got up. I put the glass down. I had a great deal to do and eleven days in which to do it,
and I was free, and the door was open, and there was no bird.

At the door he called after me.

He said: *Same time tomorrow?*

And I said yes.

I said it without stopping and without turning round and without thinking about it, the way
you answer a question you have been answering all your life, and I was down the steps and
out into the smoke before it occurred to me that I had been asked, that being asked was new,
that the entire structure of my existence had changed that morning and the first use I had
made of it was to agree to come back.

I did come back. The next day and the day after and a great many days after that, and I had
reasons, and they were excellent, and I will give them to you as we go.

You may weigh them as they arrive.

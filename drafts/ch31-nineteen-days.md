# Chapter Thirty-One

## Nineteen Days

He worked nineteen days.

Not nineteen shifts. Nineteen days, from the Tuesday the four hundred came to the Saturday
three weeks later when Medusa and I had the conversation I'm going to put at the end of this,
and in between he was in that water and there is no useful sense in which he stopped.

The dead don't sleep. I want to explain that properly because people hear it as a convenience
and it isn't one. You lie down because lying down is what you did when you were alive and the
routine is most of what keeps you a person; you get up because there's a shift. Nothing in you
insists. There is no mechanism anywhere in the underworld that makes anybody stop doing
anything, which is the same fact as *nobody can be forced*, seen from the other side, and which
is why it took them four hundred years to notice they had a workforce.

So there was nothing to make him stop except a person telling him to.

---

Here's the work, and I'll be quick.

The grievers go in forty at a time. They walk in past the ones who aren't theirs, in water that
is about knee-deep and moving slowly, and they find the one they came for, and they stand in
front of them and say their own name and then the dead one's and then something particular.
Somewhere in the third or fourth minute a direction appears. Nobody can tell you what that is.
Everybody in the room feels it and not one of them can put it in a sentence, and I have asked
about forty people and got forty versions and the best of them was a man who said it's like
when somebody in a crowd starts walking towards you.

And Caeneus stands where the water is deepest, which is about the middle, and takes hold of it
and carries it.

He does not generate anything. I want that stated because there is a version of this going
round already in which he is a kind of engine, and he isn't, and the version is going round
because it is easier to sing. He is labour. He takes hold of a thing that is already pointing
and he moves it, and if nobody is pointing it he can stand in that water for a week and nothing
happens, which is what the first afternoon in the corridor with the woman from the hill town
proved a year earlier.

Four hundred the first day. Then five, then six hundred, because Prue got the reliefs down to
forty minutes and put a second queue on the north side.

Over the nineteen days it was something over eight thousand.

---

Eight thousand.

You cannot see eight thousand.

That's the thing I have never worked out how to make anybody understand, up here or down there.
Eight thousand people went back into the world in nineteen days — eight thousand babies over
the following two years, on Prue's list, on three continents, none of whom will ever know
anything about any of this — and you go and stand at the door of that room afterwards and it
looks exactly the same as it did. The floor is water now. The far end still isn't there. And
there are still several hundred thousand people standing up in it and no god will give you a
number.

Hades won't guess. He has never once guessed and I've stopped asking.

So the arithmetic is: nineteen days, eight thousand, and it doesn't show.

Somebody in that corridor worked out how long the whole of it would take at the rate of those
nineteen days, and wrote the number on the back of a relief sheet, and I have seen the sheet
and it is a number about a man rather than about a room: it assumes he would still be doing it,
and by the fourth day everybody standing in that water knew he would not be.

The real rate is whatever it is once you take him out of it and put a rota in, and it is a
fraction, and it has been running for two years now and will go on running after everybody in
this account is finished. That is not a tragedy. That is what work is.

It is only not fine for one person, and it is not fine for him at a rate of about a year of
himself a day.

---

Now the book.

I said earlier that it goes backwards and in order — Leos first, then the compound, then the
dog, then the crossing, then his mother. That's what I had at the time and it's the tidy
version. It stays where it is, because the way it actually goes is the thing I want to tell
you.

It isn't clean. A thing goes and then some of it stays. The story goes and the feeling stays
for a month. Or the feeling goes and the shape of it is still in there, which is the worst one,
because he laughs in the right place at a story about a man he can't identify.

And the last piece of a thing can sit there for a very long time after the thing itself has
gone.

He lost his mother in the summer, in the sense that everything that had happened between them
went. But he still had bits of her all autumn. He had the fact that she'd waited. He had the
port, and the ten years of standing at it in the morning, which is a thing about her even
though it doesn't sound like one.

And he had her name.

I read to him twice a week. It's one of Charon's ledgers and I do a page and a half, good one
first, bad ones in the middle, and I go down on the Tuesdays and Fridays with Persephone, who
crosses the way other people take a train and who waits and reads a book while I do it and has
never once asked me about any of it.

In the nineteen days I stopped being able to keep up.

That's the part I'd give you if you only took one thing out of this chapter. It stopped being a
page and a half. He was losing faster than I could write, and I was writing in the margins and
on the backs of the previous pages and eventually on a second ledger which is not in order and
which I have never managed to reconcile, and there are three or four days in that stretch where
I simply did not get there in time and whatever it was is not written down anywhere and is
gone.

I don't know what they were. That's the point. I can't know, because the only record of them
was him.

---

What went in the nineteen days was the ten years.

The whole decade. The port, the turnstiles, the five lines, the dogs — he'd already lost Fotis
in the spring, which we knew about, but this was the job itself. How to do it. Which of the
three jokes to use on the ones who are angry. Standing slightly to the left of people. The
smell of wet rope.

And Prue.

He is perfectly nice to Prue now. She has said, once, that it is fine and that she's had
sixty-one years of being the person people are perfectly nice to and that she'd rather have him
here being nice than not have him, and then she went and did the chairs. That's the whole of
what she has ever said about it. If anybody ever writes this up and gives Prue a speech I will
find them.

The cruelty of it is specific and I want to name it. Those ten years were the only part of his
life he chose. He was murdered out of the first part by his own people and he is going to be
carried out of the last part by this, and the middle — a job at a port, in the dark, that he
was very good at and that nobody had made him take — is the only stretch of it that was his,
and it is the stretch that paid.

---

On the fourteenth day Medusa came and found me.

She has been watching him since the first eleven, which I didn't know at the time and which she
told me that afternoon. She said she stopped watching the water in the autumn a year ago and
started watching him, and that she had not said anything for a year because she is not his
mother and because everything he was doing was working.

Then she said: he'll do it until there's nothing left, and he will not stop, and you're the
only person he'd stop for. So tell him.

I said what the numbers were. I said six thousand in fourteen days and I said what was in that
room and I said that every day he wasn't in the water was — and she let me get all the way to
the end of it.

Then she said, "I know what the numbers are. I'm asking you to tell him anyway."

And I didn't.

---

I've had two years to build something out of that and I haven't got anything.

I could tell you I did the arithmetic and the arithmetic is not close, and that is true. Eight
thousand people against one man is not a hard sum and anybody who says it is has never had to
do it. I could tell you he'd have refused, which is also true and which I know because I did
eventually ask him, months later, sideways, and he was surprised by the question in the way he
is surprised by most questions now.

But the honest answer is that I did not want to be the person who stopped it.

Not because of the eight thousand. Because I have spent my entire death being the one who says
the true thing that nobody acts on, and here was the one thing in the world that was actually
moving, and I was inside it, and I was not going to be the one who put a hand up.

Medusa didn't argue with me. She said, "All right," and went back to the platform.

She has never mentioned it again and she has never once looked at me in a way that means
anything, and I'd honestly rather she did.

---

On the Saturday I got to the mother's page.

It's in the middle, where the bad ones go. I do her about every third session and I have never
got it right. She shot herself the night they buried him without a coin, and she waited ten
years at a port in the dark because it had to be the right day, and I read that out and he
listened the way he listens.

And then, because it was the end of the page and because I have done it a hundred times, I said
her name.

And he said: "Who's that?"

Not upset. Interested. He thought I'd introduced somebody new.

I did the thing you do, which is to go back and say it again with the sentence around it — your
mother, the one who waited, this is her name — and he said, "Oh, right," and then, after a bit,
very gently, because he is kind about this and it is the worst part of it: "Sorry — is it
important?"

And I sat there and I realised I had never written it down.

Not once. Not in the ledger and not in the margins and not in the second one that isn't in
order. I had written down that she waited and what she did and what it cost him, and I had
never put her name on a page in two years, and I have thought about why and the answer is not
complicated. Writing a thing in that book means it's gone. It's a book of things that are gone.
And I had a superstition about it, which I would have denied if you had asked me at the time,
and which cost the one thing I might actually have been able to keep.

---

So on the Sunday I went up.

Persephone took me up on the crossing and I got the bus and I went to the room above the
fishmonger's on the harbour road and I asked Ekhi to get the entry out.

It's in the cupboard, in the shelf of books that isn't one book, and it is not long. It gives
the prophecy she carried for her child, in full, in her own hand. It gives the date she was
told he would have to die, and by whom, and it gives, in a different hand, added later, the
date she was told to wait, and the length of the wait, and one line confirming she had accepted
it.

And at the top of it, in her own writing, it gives her name.

Ekhi put the kettle on and left me with it and did not ask what it was for, which is the single
best thing anybody did for me that year.

I copied it onto the flyleaf of the ledger, at the front, under the one thing he wrote in there
himself.

---

I'm not going to put it here.

I said at the start of all this that I had nothing to arrange and nothing in it for me and that
you could have the lot, and you have had the lot — every bit of it, including the parts that
make me look bad, including the fourteenth day.

This is the one thing you don't get.

It isn't mine. It's his, and he hasn't got it, and I'm keeping it in a book above a
fishmonger's and on a flyleaf and in my head, and those are the three places it exists in the
world, and that's enough places.

You've been patient with me and I'm sorry. It's four syllables. It isn't anything.

It's just that I've got very little of him left that hasn't been sung at four thousand people,
and I find I'm holding on to this.

# Chapter Twenty-One

## Secondhand

I was not there.

I am going to say that once, properly, at the top, and then I am going to tell it anyway,
because it happened and it is the most important thing that happened on Earth in the whole of
this and leaving it out would be a dishonesty of exactly the kind I have been accusing other
people of.

But you should know what you are getting. Everything in this chapter is somebody's account.
I have two: Riddy's, which is close and unsentimental and was written down that night in the
back of a book; and Dionysus's, which is warm and unreliable and arrived four days later in
the form of a man who wanted to talk about something else. Where they agree I have simply
told it. Where they do not I have said so, once, and it matters.

I heard about it two days after the fact, from a woman who assumed I already knew.

---

Theseus came back on a Thursday morning through the tradesmen's gate at the palace in
Heraklion, unarmed, in daylight, and asked at the desk for an appointment.

He had been gone eleven months. Riddy's account is that nobody in the building recognised
him for a full minute and then everybody did at once.

Where he had been is not a mystery and was never a mystery: he had been with the Trojans, and
had been with the Trojans the entire time he was standing behind Ari's shoulder holding a
door for her. That is what he was. He had lied to her once, to get her to save six people,
and it had worked, and then her father had killed them anyway that same midnight, and Theseus
had walked out of the palace and not been seen since.

I had been told, by people whose business it is to know, that he was dead. That was the
settled view. There is a version of this in which he is dead and a version in which he is a
coward, and both versions were in circulation and neither was true, and I had accepted the
one that suited the shape of my account, which was the first.

He had spent eleven months moving what was left of his people out of the compounds in the
south and into places where they could not be counted.

---

Ari saw him at ten o'clock.

Riddy was in the room. This is the part I have from her directly and I have not altered a
word of the substance.

He did not apologise. That is the first thing she wanted recorded. He came in and stood and
gave a report — numbers, locations, how many were sick, what the compounds were like now that
the administration that ran them had lost its president — and he delivered it flatly, as a
man delivering a report, for about six minutes.

And when he had finished, Ari said: "Six people died because I trusted you."

And he said: "Seven."

And she said, "Say the rest of it."

And he said that the seventh was Nax, and that Nax had been his friend since they were
fifteen, and that he had been the one who locked him up to stop him surrendering, and that he
had spent that time deciding whether the six would have died anyway and had concluded
that they would, and that this had turned out not to help.

Riddy's note on this exchange, in the book, is four words long: *neither of them cried.*

---

Hecuba and Andromache came the following week, and they did not come humbly.

This is where the two accounts start pulling in different directions, and I will get to that,
but they agree on the shape. Two women who had been made to stand in front of Minos in that
same building and account for a piece of graffiti walked back into it to negotiate with his
daughter, and the daughter is the one who had spent a birthday wish on their pardon and got
seven of them killed for it.

Nobody in that room was in a position to be gracious. Riddy says the first twenty minutes
were the worst thing she has ever sat through and she has been dead.

Andromache did the numbers. Fifteen thousand people, minus what the last year has taken, in
compounds on land nobody else wants, with no schools worth the name and a registration system
that follows them for life. Hecuba did the other thing. Hecuba asked Ari, in front of
everybody, whether she was proposing an alliance or a favour, because they had had a favour
from that family before and the favour had a body count.

Ari, by both accounts, said: "A favour, so far. Tell me what makes it an alliance."

Before that, though, there were the twenty minutes, and Riddy's account of them is the
fullest thing in her book that month, so I am going to give a piece of it.

Hecuba did not raise her voice at any point. That is in both versions. What she did was ask
questions she already had the answers to, and wait, and let Ari produce the answers herself
in front of her own staff.

She asked how many Trojans were currently registered in Heraklion. Ari's chamberlain gave a
figure. She asked how many of those were registered at the address they actually lived at,
and the chamberlain did not have that figure, and Hecuba supplied it, and it was under a
third. She asked what the penalty was for a registration discrepancy. She asked whether Ari
had ever met anyone serving it. She asked what the labyrinth cells had been used for in the
year since the president died, and was told: holding. And she asked, in the same
tone, holding whom.

Then she said the only sentence of hers that both my witnesses quote identically, and it was
not about any of that. She said:

"Your father sat where you are sitting and told me that Troy was destroyed because we
overreached. I have wanted twelve years to ask somebody in this room whether they believed
that or whether it was simply what they had been given to say. You do not have to answer.
I would like you to know that it is the question."

Riddy says Ari took a long time and then said that she had believed it until she was thirty,
and that she had believed it the way you believe the furniture.

Hecuba said that was an honest answer and that she had not expected one, and sat back, and
after that the room was survivable.

---

Here is the disagreement, and it is not a small one.

**Riddy's version.** Hecuba names the price. She says the word *vote* — that the Trojan
remnant will stand with Krete against Olympus in exchange for the franchise, full and
unqualified, every adult, counted the same as anybody born in Heraklion. And Ari does not
consult anyone, does not take an hour, and says yes across the table in the time it takes to
say it.

**Dionysus's version.** Ari offers it. Unprompted, before Hecuba can ask, as the opening term,
because she had worked out on her own that she could not ask people to die for a city that
would not let them speak in it.

I have gone back to both of them about this. Riddy is certain. Dionysus is certain. Neither
has any reason to lie and both of them like Ari, and the difference between the two versions
is the difference between a ruler who concedes something under pressure and a ruler who
arrives having already decided to give it away, and I cannot tell you which of those Ari is.

I would once have been able to. That is not modesty; it is a fact about the last year. There
was a period of about three thousand years in which I could have told you what was in a
human's head in a room I was not in, because I had been in ten thousand rooms like it and
people are not as various as they believe. I have lost that, somewhere, recently, and I did
not notice it going.

What both accounts agree on is that it took eleven minutes.

---

Dionysus was in the room for the whole of it.

He had come with them from the south — he has been with them since the week the Trojans got
him, and by every account including his own he has made himself extremely useful in the way
that a god can: there has not been a bad harvest in the places where his people are, and there
was a week in the spring when it rained on one side of a valley.

He sat at the end of the table with his hands in his lap.

He told me about it himself, four days later, sitting on the edge of a fountain in a town
whose name I have decided not to put down. He was not complaining. That is the thing I keep
turning over. He described the whole of it with real pleasure, the way you describe a
match you watched, and then he said the thing that I think he had come to say, which was:
"They didn't need me for any of it."

I said something about the harvests.

And he said, "No, I know. I mean the *deal*, man. Nobody looked at me once."

Then he asked whether I thought Ari had noticed he was there, and I said of course she had,
and he said, "Yeah," and did not believe me, and was right not to.

---

So that is the alliance.

Krete and the Trojan remnant, made in a room in Heraklion in eleven minutes, by four humans
and a bodyguard, in exchange for a franchise that half of Ari's own administration thinks
will end her, and which she gave away either under pressure or on purpose depending on which
of my two witnesses you believe.

No god brokered it. A god was present, and had brought the weather with him, and had been
useful for a season, and sat at the end of the table with his hands in his lap while the only
alliance in this entire story that was made rather than ordered got made without him.

I have been telling you for a very long time that the plan required me.

Not in those words. I have been careful never to use those words. But go back through it — go
back to the beginning, to the plateau and the eagle and the man telling you about three humans
and a prophecy — and count how many times the arrangement is described as a thing that had to
be arranged, and by whom, and count how many of those sentences would survive somebody
pointing out that on a Thursday in Heraklion four people who had every reason to hate each
other sat down and did the necessary thing without a single one of us in
the building.

---

As far as anybody has been able to establish, Olympus did not notice for six weeks.

I have checked this more carefully than I have checked anything else in this chapter, because
I did not believe it. An alliance is made against you by the two populations you have spent a
generation keeping apart, in a building you installed a president in, and nothing happens. No
weather. No summons. Nobody is struck by anything.

The explanation is not subtle and it is not comforting. Krete was Poseidon's, and Poseidon
does not have Krete any more, and the god who took every city from him took them as a
possession rather than as a job. There is nobody reading the reports because there are no
reports, because reports were Poseidon's, and he is in the north being fed by his wife's
lover's mother's household and is not sending any.

When Olympus did notice, it noticed wrong. What came back up, through the routes such things
come up by, was that the Trojans in Krete had been given a vote — and it was received as a
piece of gossip about a weak ruler making concessions, which is exactly what it looks like if
you have spent four thousand years believing that humans do not do anything except in
response to something you did.

I include that because I have believed the same thing, professionally, for the same period.

---

There is a postscript and I am putting it in because Riddy put it in her book and because I
have thought about it more than the deal.

When it was done, and the terms were being written up, Ari asked Andromache whether she would
like to see the labyrinth.

Not the cells. The labyrinth — the thing itself, the machine, the place where her son was
killed. She offered to take her down.

Andromache said no. And then she said that she would like it filled in, all of it, concrete,
and the button destroyed and the room the button is in destroyed, and Ari said that it was
being used to house four hundred people who had nowhere else and that she would fill it in
when they had somewhere, and Andromache said, "Then that is the first thing on the list," and
somebody wrote it down.

Riddy's note: *this is what it looks like when nobody is helping them.*

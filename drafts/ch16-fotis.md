# Chapter Sixteen

## Fotis

I want to say something first about how well I know these people, because it is going to
matter in about four pages and I would rather have it on the table than produce it later
like a man defending himself.

I have watched Caeneus of the Amazons for ten years.

Not casually. Not the way you keep half an eye on a thing you have arranged. I had him from
the morning he arrived at the port with his coin missing and his mother not on the manifest,
and I watched him take the handler's job, and learn the dogs, and be good at it, and go on
being good at it for a decade in a place that does not reward being good at anything. I know
what he does when a new arrival will not get out of the queue. I know the three jokes he
uses and which one he keeps for the ones who are angry. I know that he stands slightly to
the left of people when he talks to them, which is a thing men do when they have been hit
in the past, and I know when that started and who did it.

I could have told you any of that on any day of the last ten years.

Hold that. I am going to come back to it.

---

The eleven went back on a Tuesday and by Thursday it was clear that the eleven had cost
something.

Not to the eleven. They were fine — they are fine, they are out there now, and one of them
is a baby in a fishing town and I find that I think about that more than is useful. It cost
somewhere else, and the accounting for it was not immediately legible, and for two days the
only thing anybody could say with confidence was that Caeneus was tired in a way that dead
men are not supposed to get.

He worked through it. That is the first thing to say about him and it will be the last thing
to say about him. He was at the river at six every morning and he did the job — he did the
actual job, the talking, the getting people to a decision they can bear — and in the
afternoons he sat with Medusa and answered questions about what it had felt like, which he
could not do, because it had not felt like anything. He said it was like being asked what
your hand does.

Persephone had a theory. Hades had four, and wrote them down, and crossed out three.

I had one too and I am not going to pretend otherwise: I thought it was the strain of it,
which is what you think when a thing is new and hard and the person doing it looks grey. I
thought he needed a fortnight. I said so, to two people, with some authority.

---

I went up to Krete on the Friday because there was a message from her and she does not send
messages.

She was in a room off the west courtyard that Ari's people had found for her, which had a
table in it and nothing else, and she had been on Earth long enough by then to have stopped
expecting the second conversation to go differently from the first. She looked worse
than he did. She had a burn on her hand from something and did not mention it.

She did not ask how the plan was going. She has never once asked how the plan is going,
which I had put down at the time to a sort of protective incuriosity and now understand
somewhat differently.

She asked whether I was going back down.

I said I was.

And she said: "Ask him why the dog's called Fotis."

I said that I knew why the dog was called Fotis. It means light; he says so to anyone who
will stand still for it; he said it to Medusa on the first morning and Medusa told him she
didn't care.

"I know what it means," she said. "Ask him why he picked it."

---

Now.

I would like to be fair to myself here, and I have discovered that being fair to myself is a
much narrower activity than I used to think, so let me simply put down what I did.

I went down on the Saturday. I found him at the port, off shift, sitting on the crate by the
awning with the old dog's head on his foot, and we talked for about twenty minutes about
nothing — about the four o'clock, about a handler who had been promoted, about a woman he
had spent an hour with the previous day who would not go into the water until she had been
told, properly, that her sister would not be waiting on the other side. He is very good and
he does not know it. He asked after Riddy twice.

Then I asked him why the dog was called Fotis.

And he said: "It means light."

And I said yes, I know, but why that.

And he thought about it.

I want to give this its proper duration. He did not flinch and he did not go pale and there
was no moment in which anything visibly went wrong, because nothing was happening to him —
that is the part I got wrong for two days and I am putting it down here so that the record
shows where I was. He simply thought about it, the way you think about why you took a
particular road, and then he moved his foot slightly under the dog and said:

"I suppose I liked it."

He said it easily. He was not troubled. He looked out at the water.

And then, because he is who he is, he asked whether Riddy had wanted to know, and I said she
had, and he said, "Tell her I don't remember," and laughed, and said that was going to
happen more, wasn't it, and went and got two cups of the tea that tastes of nothing.

---

Here is what I did not know, and what she knew, and how she knew it.

He told her the whole of it twice. Once on some steps outside a party in the first week she
was dead, badly and sideways, in the way you tell a thing you have decided to be light about
— and then again, properly, in his room, on the night before everything went wrong in the
Nothing. Two tellings, eight days apart, to the same person, which as far as I can establish
is the only time in ten years he told it to anybody at all.

There was a boy called Leos who was sent away when he came of age, as they all are, and who
came back, as they are not allowed to, because he could not stand it. And Caenis, who had
been keeping something that Leos was the only person alive to know, told him to go — and
before he went, gave him the only useful thing in reach, and said: *take this, it's bright.*

They caught him about a hundred yards out.

Ten years later a man at a port who had been given a working dog and told to name it named
it *light*.

He never said the two things in the same breath. Not to her, not in either telling; she put
them together herself, on the second night, lying awake, and she has been carrying it since
as the thing she knows about him that nobody else does.

That is what she sent me down to check.

---

I got back up to Krete on the Sunday and told her, and I had spent the crossing arranging
how to tell her.

I did not get to use any of it. She was standing when I came in, and she looked at my face,
and she said, "Right," and sat down and took the flyleaf out of the back of a book on the
table and started writing.

She wrote: *the dog is called Fotis, which means light, because of the torch you gave Leos
the night before they killed him.* Then underneath, on the next line, she wrote *Leos* and
sat with the pen for a moment and then began writing everything she had about him, which
took her about forty minutes, and I stood by the window for the whole of it because it did
not seem to be a thing to sit down in the middle of.

When she finished she read it back and made two corrections and then said, without looking
up, that it would be the compound next, and then the dog, and then probably the crossing,
and then his mother, and that she would need a bigger book.

I asked her how she could possibly know the order.

She said: "Because he told it to me in that order, and it's going out backwards."

---

I have had four thousand years and a plateau and a bird and the entire uninterrupted
attention of a mind that does not sleep, and I had a catalogue of that man that ran to ten
years and included which side of a person he stands on.

She had two conversations.

I am putting that down and I am not going to build anything on top of it.

---

The tea tastes of nothing, by the way. They all say it and it is true and I had never once
had a cup of it until that Saturday, in ten years of watching him have it every day of his
death.

# Chapter Thirty-Seven

## The Tenth

Something arrived a month and six days after the water, in a village up the coast, to a woman
who had never heard of any of us.

She did what people do, which is nothing for six days and then mention it to somebody, and it
came down the routes the way they always have, and it was in the cupboard by the end of the
week.

Ekhi took the pencil out from behind her ear and made a mark on the inside of the door.

That's the chapter. I could stop there and I'm going to keep going for about nine pages, but I
want it on the record that the whole of what happened is a woman making a pencil mark on a door
in a room over a fish shop, and that everybody in that room understood exactly what it meant
and nobody said anything.

---

I went to see them about it because somebody had to and because I'd stopped being frightened of
them somewhere around the winter.

They're in the municipal baths on the north side. Drained since before the war, full of
pigeons, card table set up in the deep end. That's where they were when Prometheus went and
asked for a correction and didn't get one, and it's where they've been the whole time, and I
would like to say for the record that in the entire year and a half when there was no fate
being written anywhere in the world, the three of them were about nine feet from a public road
playing a game with the scoring on the back of a tide timetable.

They were working when I got there. That's the first thing. The table was covered.

Lachesis said, "Oh good, it's the one who asks."

---

I'll give you the exchange because I said I'd give you everything, and because I've been told
since that I should have written it up properly, and it is better like this.

I asked whether they'd stopped on purpose.

Clotho said no.

I asked whether Zeus burning them had done it.

Atropos said no, and then said, "That was a Tuesday," which I've thought about a lot and have
decided is not an answer to anything.

I asked what had stopped it, then. And there was a bit of business — they do that, they enjoy
themselves, and I've learned to stand still and let it go past — and then Lachesis put her
pencil down and said:

"You can't write a thread out of something somebody else is holding."

---

Right. I'm going to do this once, flatly, and then we'll get on.

A prophecy is written about a life. Not predicted — **written**, which is what they've been
telling anybody who asked for four thousand years and which everybody has heard as poetry.

And what a life is made of, as far as I can follow it, is what's available to it. The shapes it
could take. That's the material. That's what's on their table.

Zeus had it.

Not metaphorically. He had hundreds of thousands of them in a room under the world with the
wanting taken out, and he had been taking them for four thousand years, and it turns out that
past some point you cannot keep drawing that much of it out of the world and have there
continue to be enough of it left to write with.

So they stopped. Not in protest. Not as a strike, which is the version that got about and which
I've had to correct nine times. They stopped the way a mill stops, and they went and sat in a
drained swimming pool for a year and a half because there was nothing to do, and they let Zeus
think he'd done it, because it cost them nothing and because they've never in their existence
corrected anybody about anything.

I asked how they get out.

That's the bit nobody in the network has ever known and it's the bit I actually wanted. Four
hundred years of holding prophecies and the whole of Ekhi's theory of where they come from is
*they arrive*. So I asked. It's written on a table in a drained swimming pool by three women
with a pencil, and then somehow it is in a woman in a village who has never heard of any of us,
and I would like to know what happens in between.

Clotho said, "Yes."

I said that wasn't an answer and Lachesis said no, it wasn't, and Atropos offered me a biscuit.

So I still don't know, and neither does anybody, and I've come round to thinking that's
probably how it stays. I've noticed that the things I've been told in the last two years are
mostly things I worked out and then had confirmed by somebody who was never going to volunteer
them. Nobody in this story has ever explained anything to anybody. They answer what you ask,
and only that, and if you ask the wrong question you get a true sentence that leaves you
exactly where you were.

I asked Lachesis, straight out, whether they'd wanted him dead.

She looked genuinely surprised. She said, "Why would we want that?"

And I said because he was the reason, and she said yes, and I said so surely, and she said —
and this is the closest I've had to a statement of purpose out of any of them —

"We wanted to be able to do the work."

---

That's it. That's what all of it was for.

Four thousand years, and Prometheus on a rock, and Charon in the river, and my husband taking
my coin, and a boy in a compound with a light in his hand, and Adrian on a dock, and a very
large old man walking down to a port on his own feet.

Working conditions.

I sat with that on the way back and I've sat with it since and I want to be careful not to make
it bigger than it is, because the temptation is to be bitter about it and I don't think bitter
is right. They're not cruel. They're not kind either. They're the people whose job it is to
write down what's true, and for a year and a half they couldn't, and now they can, and they're
pleased, in the way you're pleased when the thing you're good at is available again.

Nobody was ever going to be on our side. That's the actual finding. Not against us — that would
be something, you can work with against. There was simply never anybody up there whose business
was us.

Caeneus said that in a room under the world before I'd properly met him, in different words,
and I didn't take it in at the time because I was busy being dead.

---

Now the part I've been avoiding.

I'm not sure it's good.

Prophecy is back and everyone in that room over the fish shop is delighted, and I sat through
the meeting where they discussed the second procedure and how the intake would be handled and
whether the routes in the south still worked, and I said the right things and went home and sat
on the balcony.

Because look at what they do to people.

Minos got a true one and murdered a child over it and spent thirty years telling his daughter
she'd done it. Ari believed hers before she was two and carried it until she was thirty-one.
Caeneus was given one and his own mother handed him to a compound to make it come true, and
waited ten years at a port to finish the job, and it is the single worst thing anybody in this
account has done to anybody and she did it *correctly*, by the terms of the thing.

Mine says a line appears, the order wanes, the family falls, and kaos reigns. All of that
happened. Not one word of it helped me. I understood what it meant about four days before it
finished.

I put this to Ekhi and she did not defend it. She said the words are true and that's the whole
of what they are, and what people do with a true thing is not the fault of the true thing, and
then she said the sentence I've decided to keep, which is: "It's not for their good. It's just
what's so."

I asked whether she'd rather have the year and a half back, when nothing was written and nobody
was told anything.

She said no, immediately, and then thought about it for a long time, and then said no again,
slightly differently.

---

I told Prometheus.

He was on the shift, at the shallow end, between people. I gave it to him in about four
sentences — that it had started again, that the first one had come in a month after the water,
that the reason it stopped was the hoarding and not the fire, and that they'd said outright
they had never wanted Zeus dead and had only ever wanted to be able to do the work.

He said, "Ah."

That's it. That's what I got. He stood there for a bit and then he said that it made sense of
the timing, in a voice like a man agreeing with a bus timetable, and then somebody came down
the steps who needed talking to and he went and did that instead, badly, the way he does.

I'd gone down there specifically to tell him. I'd had it arranged on the crossing — the order,
the bit to leave till last, all of it. I'd been rather looking forward to it.

I don't think he was pretending. I think he'd finished with it. Four thousand years of wanting
an answer out of those three, and when one finally came out of that room it came out to
somebody else, about something that was never his question, and by then he had a woman in the
water in front of him who could not swim.

---

The boy got his tenth in the spring.

He'd had nine by heart since before I met him and had been waiting for the tenth for the better
part of two years and had spent most of it quietly certain he'd done something wrong. He's
twenty-one now. He's got a coat that fits and he's stopped calling Ekhi ma'am.

They gave it to him on a Thursday, which is when he's tested, and he took it away and had it by
the evening the way he always does.

It's four lines about a woman in a village up the coast who has no idea, and who will not be
told for years, and might never be, depending on how it reads when the time comes. He said it
to us in the flat voice they use, with the music stripped out of it, twice, and Ekhi said
"good" and put the kettle on, and the man with the bad chest asked if there was any of the
lemon thing left.

And that's how fate started again in the world: a twenty-one-year-old in a borrowed room
reciting four lines badly while somebody looked for a biscuit.

I stayed for the tea. I'm glad I did.

I still don't think it's good. I think it's true, and I think those are different, and I think
learning the difference is most of what I've got out of two years of this.

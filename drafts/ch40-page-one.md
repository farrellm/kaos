# Chapter Forty

## Page One

This is the last of it, and it's small, and I'd rather you knew that going in than got to the
end and felt short-changed.

Nothing happens in this chapter. That's not modesty. I checked.

---

I saw Prometheus in the autumn, on the long beach past the harbour, on a Tuesday, from about
two hundred yards.

He was walking. That's all. He had his shoes in one hand and his coat over his arm and he was
going along the tideline at the pace of a man who has not been told to be anywhere, stopping
every so often and looking at the water and then going on.

I watched him for a while, which I'm aware is a thing he'd have made four pages out of.

He's been on the shift most days since the spring and he's not good at it, which everyone
knows and nobody says to him. He can't get the names out without arranging them. He'll stand
in the water in front of somebody's dead husband and you can hear him building the sentence,
and by the time it's ready the moment's gone past and the person he's brought down is crying
for the wrong reason. Prue has him on the rota anyway. She says he's improving. He isn't, but
she's right to have him on it.

I didn't go over.

He looked like a man with nothing whatsoever to do, and I worked out on the way home that it
was probably the first time that has been true of him in four thousand years, and I still
don't know whether it's a good thing that happened to him or the last one.

I don't think he does either. He hasn't put it in the book and he's put everything else in
the book.

---

The flat's above a chandler's and the balcony is about the size of a door.

I go down on the Tuesdays and Fridays with Persephone, which is not a sentence anybody would
have believed a year ago, and she waits and reads while I do the session, and we come back up
and she asks after the lemon tree, which is dying and which she has strong views about.

The book is one of Charon's ledgers. He gave me four and said he had crates of them, which
was true, and said nothing else about it.

I do a page and a half. More than that and he gets tired, and when he's tired he's polite,
and the polite is worse than the tired.

---

Last Friday I did the dog.

It's near the front because it's a good one and you start with a good one. His dog was called
Fotis, which means light, and he called it that because of the night his friend Leos came back
to the compound when he wasn't allowed to, and Caenis gave him the only useful thing in reach
and told him it was bright and told him to go.

They caught Leos about a hundred yards out.

He listened to all of that the way you'd listen to a story about a cousin — properly,
politely, with his head slightly on one side. And when I'd finished he said, "That's very
sad," and I said yes, and he said, "Did he keep the dog long?"

Fourteen years. I told him fourteen years.

He said that was a good run for a dog, and looked pleased, and that's the sort of thing that
happens now. He gets the shape and not the weight. Sometimes that seems like a mercy and I've
learned not to say so out loud, because Prue heard me say it once and did not speak to me for
a day and a half.

---

Then the middle, which is where the bad ones go.

I did his mother. I do her about every third session and I've never got it right and I don't
think there's a right.

She was in the network. She wrote his prophecy down and kept it, and she gave him up to the
Amazons because it had been decided that he had to fulfil it, and she went home and shot
herself the night they buried him without a coin, and then she waited ten years at a port in
the dark because it had to be the right day.

He asked whether she'd been on her own for the ten years, and I said mostly, and he thought
about it and said, "That's a long time to be sure about something."

Which is the best thing anybody has ever said about her, and he will not remember saying it,
and I wrote it in the front where he wrote the other one.

---

Then the balcony, because the balcony goes at the end. It's page one and I read it last, which
makes no sense and is the order that works.

There was a man who spent his childhood in a compound where nothing grew, because nothing did
— dust, and a wall, and six hundred women who had a great many virtues and no interest
whatsoever in flowers. And then he had a flat in Krete with a balcony the size of a door, and
he bought pots, and he grew herbs on it, and the thing he missed most about being alive was
the smell of them when you water them in the evening while it's still warm.

He always asks me to do that part again.

So I do it again, and I tell him what it smelled like on the Tuesday, because I go out and
water them before I come down specifically so that I'll have it, and I have never told him
that and I'm not going to.

---

And then on Friday he asked my name.

He does ask. He's asked me a lot, over the months, and he's always apologetic about it and I've
always said it and it's always gone.

I said: Riddy.

And he said, "Riddy," the way you say a word you're checking, and then he said, "Is that
short?"

And I said yes, Eurydice, but nobody's called me that since my mother, and he said Riddy was
better, and I said I thought so too.

Then he asked whether I'd come far and I said not really, and we sat there, and Persephone
came down the steps and said we had about ten minutes.

---

I went down again on the Tuesday.

He was on the wall outside the port office with the tea he can't taste and he saw me coming
along the road from about forty yards, and he put the cup down and stood up, and he said:

"Riddy."

Before I said anything. Before I got to him.

And then he said, "Sorry — I don't know how I knew that," and sat back down, and asked whether
I'd come far.

---

One more thing, and it's mine, and then I'll stop.

I never convinced anybody.

I want to put that down flatly because it has taken me two years to be able to and because
somebody at some point is going to write this up as though I did. I said the true thing in
temples and squares and kitchens for a year and a half. I said it to priests and to my own
aunt and to eleven people in Heraklion, four of whom were waiting for the crate.

Not one of them changed anything because of me. Not one.

What changed things was Adrian standing on a dock being kind to strangers, and Prue with a
clipboard, and a man with one hand and a piece of accounts paper, and Orpheus singing my life
back at four thousand people a night in a key I would not have chosen, which I have decided to
be finished being angry about and am not.

That's the job, as far as I can tell. You say the true thing and it doesn't go in, and you say
it again, and one day something happens for entirely unrelated reasons and everybody says the
thing you have been saying as though they'd thought of it. Cassandra told me it means four
different things and she was being kind. It means one thing. It means you keep talking.

She's dead now, by the way. Last winter, quietly, in the room above the fishmonger's, with
Ekhi there. I've taken her seat and I do not have her patience and I am told I will.

So — you.

I've been aware of you the whole way, the way you're aware of a draught, and I said at the
start that I had nothing to arrange and nothing in it for me and that you could have the lot.
You've had the lot. Every bit of it, in order, including the parts that make me look bad and
the part where I let a man sing my death because the arithmetic was better his way.

I'm not going to ask you for anything. I've never once asked and it's never once worked.

But you sat here for the whole of it, which nobody has ever done, and if you got to the end of
it thinking *yes, all right, that's what happened* — then that's it. That's the entire thing.
That's what a prophet is for and I've had one, and it took two hundred thousand dead people, a
strike, and a dog to get here, and I'll take it.

---

I'm not going to tell you what that means, because I don't know, and because everyone I've
told has immediately told me what it means and all of them are guessing.

It might be the first thing that's gone in since the spring. It might be one thing. It might
be that he heard somebody say it that morning and it was lying about in him when I came round
the corner.

Nobody's coming to explain it. That's the other thing I've had to get used to — there's no one
left whose job it is to know. The Fates are back at work and they don't do questions. Hades
runs a port. Prometheus walks on a beach.

You get what you get, and you do what you're told, and it turns out that when nobody's telling
you anything you have to decide for yourself what to do with a man saying your name on a wall.

What I decided is that I'd go home and water the plants.

It's still warm in the evenings. It'll be warm for another few weeks and then it won't be, and
he'll still want that part, and I'll do it from memory and he won't know the difference and
I will.

That's all of it. That's the whole thing. I said I'd tell it to you in the order it happened
and I have, and you'll believe it or you won't.

You'd be the first.

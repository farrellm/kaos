# Chapter Twenty-Four

## The Ferryman

There is a version of this chapter in which I do not go back to the river.

I have run it many times since. In that version I stay on the plateau with my liver and my
opinions, and the boy in the water goes on forgetting his mother a piece at a time, and the
reservoir drains or it doesn't, and the story arrives more or less where it arrives without
costing me anything further. I would like you to hold that version somewhere while you read
this one. It has made the reading easier for several people I have told it to.

It has never once made it easier for me.

---

The port was busy. It is always busy. The dead do not observe holidays and they arrive in
the numbers they arrive in, and that week they were arriving in numbers because Zeus had
gone back to the old method and was working his way through the minor gods and their
households, and a god's household is a great many people who did nothing except answer a
door.

Charon was at the desk under the awning with a pen in his mouth, doing the thing he does
with the manifest where he makes a mark for every fifty and a longer mark for every
hundred, because the machine that was supposed to do it has never once worked and he will
not learn it. Behind him a dog I did not know was asleep with its head in a filing tray.
Fotis was old by then and did the port in the mornings only.

"You're back," he said, around the pen.

"I'm back."

"It's my day off."

"You're working."

"It's my day off," he said, "and I'm working, and now you're here, so you can see how the
day is going."

I want to record that he was funny. People forget that about him, or rather people never
knew it, because the only ones who meet him are frightened and newly dead and in no
condition to appreciate a dry man. He was funny for four thousand years at a desk. I have
known gods with cities and choirs who could not manage it for an afternoon.

I said what I had come to say. I had prepared it, which I mention because you should know
that everything that follows happened to a man who had prepared.

I said that I had been free for eleven days and had spent nine of them working out what to
do with the freedom, and that I had concluded there was nothing to do with it that was
worth anything unless I first came here and said the thing I had not been able to say when
I did it. I said that I had loved him. I said that I had known exactly what I was taking
and had taken it anyway, on the understanding that what I was buying with it was the end of
Zeus and the return of every soul that had ever gone into the dark, and that the arithmetic
had been monstrous but it had been arithmetic, and I had done it.

I said: the greater good is never free. Someone pays. I chose who paid, and I chose you,
and I have carried that for longer than your species has had cities.

It is a good speech. I had four thousand years to write it. You can hear the places where I
polished it — *the arithmetic had been monstrous but it had been arithmetic* — and I want
you to notice that I am still, even here, even now, pleased with that line.

He took the pen out of his mouth and put it down and looked at me for a while.

"Who told you it was your plan?" he said.

---

Now. I have been doing this a long time and I know how to keep hold of a scene. You place
the question, you let it sit, you go somewhere else for a paragraph so the reader arrives
at the answer at the same moment the fool in the story does. It is a cheap effect and I
have used it on you perhaps a dozen times since we met and you did not notice once.

I am going to do it here, and I want you to watch me do it, because it is the last time it
works.

I said: Lachesis.

I said: she came to my door before the rock. She said it was rude not to answer. She told
me it was not my fate to kill Zeus. She told me what I had to do to Charon and she told me
to submit to my punishment and take it and not break, and she said —

She said there is a greater plan.

"Say it again," he said.

"There is a greater plan."

"That's what she said."

"That is what she said."

"Right," he said. "And where in that is you?"

---

I have thought since about the fact that he did not raise his voice. He had four thousand
years to raise it and he did not spend one of them.

He told it plainly. She took a crossing once — they do that, the Fates, they come down for
business and they will not walk when there is a boat — and it was the middle of the night
by the clock nobody down there uses, and he had her to himself in the dark for forty
minutes, and he asked her. Not out of grief. He had done his grief by then, he said, most
of it standing up. He asked because he had been killed by a man who told him not to hate
him and had not told him why, and after a few hundred years a thing like that stops being a
wound and becomes a question, and he wanted the question closed before he stopped caring
about it.

He asked her why I had done it.

And she told him. Truthfully. She has never done anything else.

She said: because it had to be someone he loved.

He said he waited for the rest of it. He is good at waiting; it is most of his job. And she
sat there with her hands in her lap and let the water go by and did not give him any more,
because there wasn't any more, because that was the whole of it.

The oath did not need a dead man. That is the part. I want to be exact about this because I
have been inexact about it to myself for a very long time and the inexactness is the only
place I have been able to live. **A living man could have sworn it.** A living man could
have stood on that shore and watched for the mark and kept the promise for as long as a
mortal keeps anything, and then handed it to his son, and it would have been kept, and it
was not necessary, it was never necessary, that he be at the bottom of the river to do it.

She did not need Charon dead.

She needed me to have killed him.

Because a man who has done that will endure a rock and a bird and four thousand years and
will never once break and never once tell, on the grounds that if he breaks then he did it
for nothing — and there is no torture in the world, there is nothing Zeus ever thought of
with all his terrible imagination, that holds a man as reliably as the fear that he has
already paid too much to stop.

I was not the author of the plan.

I was its first operation.

---

Well.

Let me — give me a moment. I am going to take us back to the desk, and the dog, and the
manifest with its little marks, because those were there and they were real and I find I
would like to be somewhere that has furniture in it.

I said several things. I said them quickly and they were not good. I said that he was
needed at the river and he said there were ferrymen before him and there'll be ferrymen
after and the river has never once cared which. I said that she could have been lying and
he did not bother to answer that, because you know and I know and every reader I have ever
had knows that they do not lie, they simply stand there while you do it to yourself. I said
— and this is the one I would take back — I said that if it was all for nothing then I had
better reasons than she gave me, and he said, what reasons, and I could not produce one. I
had four thousand years to write the speech about paying and I had never once written the
speech about why.

And then I did the thing I have done in every difficult passage of this account, which is
that I turned to you.

I turned to you and I began to say something, some remark, something with a little shape on
it that would let us both step back from the desk and look at it from a distance where it
would be sad instead of true —

and there is nobody there.

---

I am standing on the bank.

I do not know what I do next.

You have to understand — I have always known what I do next. That has been the whole of me
since before your grandmothers' grandmothers were a rumour. I knew the boy would come to
the water. I knew the girl would go into the road. I knew the shape of every day of this
for longer than there have been days to shape, and I told it to you in the past tense, all
of it, because the past tense is what a man speaks in when he already knows how it comes
out.

I am standing on the bank and the tense has gone out from under me.

He puts the pen down again. He comes round the desk. His knee is still bad; it has been bad
for four thousand years; he complains about it in exactly the tone he used when it was new.

"Sit down," he says. "You look like a bag of sticks."

I say: I told you not to hate me.

"I know."

I say: did you?

And he says, "No. Not for a day of it."

He says it the way you'd tell a man the time.

"That's the part you'll want to be sitting down for," he says. "You'd have got through the
other thing. Everyone gets through the other thing." And he takes my arm, the way you take
the arm of somebody very old, and he puts me on the crate by the awning where the handlers
sit to eat, and he goes back to the desk, because there are twelve hundred people coming in
on the four o'clock and somebody has to make the little marks.

I sit on the crate.

Behind me a dog whose name I do not know is asleep in a filing tray, and out on the water
the boat is coming in with its lamp lit, and I have absolutely no idea what happens next,
and I am going to have to find out the way everyone else does.

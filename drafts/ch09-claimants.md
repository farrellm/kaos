# Chapter Nine

## Claimants

I spent five weeks getting Eurydice of Heraklion into a room with the president of Krete. I
would like to take you through the work, because it was good work and because not one piece of
it is what got her in there.

First, though, the question you have been sitting on politely for two chapters, which is why I
did not simply do it myself.

I can walk into that building. I have walked into worse. I could have gone up the stairs
unseen and stood at the foot of that woman's bed and told her the whole of it in ninety
seconds, in a voice she would have remembered for the rest of her life, and I want you to know
that I considered it seriously and at length and on more than one bad afternoon.

Here is why it is worthless.

The content of the message is that the gods are lying. That is the entire message; everything
else in it is detail. And if the thing that tells you the gods are lying is a Titan standing in
your bedroom at four in the morning, then what you have learned is not that the gods are lying.
What you have learned is that *a god says so* — and you already have a building full of men in
robes who say the opposite, with better credentials and a longer standing, and you are now
being asked to choose between two supernatural authorities on a matter of doctrine, which is
precisely the position the priesthood has spent four thousand years arranging for you to be in
and precisely the position in which they win.

It has to come from a human being who has been there, or it is not the message. It is just a
different god.

I worked that out on the second day and I have never once revised it, and it is, I think, the
single most useful thing I contributed to that entire year.

Which meant that a woman in a borrowed coat had to get an appointment.

---

So: five weeks.

I found the chamberlain's secretary, who is the actual door in that building and always has
been, and I established what she wants, which is her sister's husband moved off a harbour
list. I found a supplier of writing paper with a grievance. I found the woman who does the
flowers for the west corridor and who has been in that palace since before Ari was born and
who talks to everybody, and I spent four days on her, and she was excellent, and none of it
went anywhere, because there was no vacancy in that diary for six weeks and the vacancy is the
thing and everything else is arrangement around it.

I was still working the flowers when it happened, is what I am saying. I want that on the
record before I tell you the rest.

---

Ari stood on the palace steps on the sixth day and told the city that a man everybody knew had
died in infancy had in fact been alive under that building for thirty years.

You cannot say that once and then say the other thing.

The other thing is *that does not happen*, and it is not a doctrine, it is the sentence every
administration on Earth relies on to get through a Tuesday. It is what you say to the woman
who is certain her son is in a cellar somewhere. It is what the desk says. And the president
of Krete had personally, publicly, in detail, with dates, removed it from the building's
vocabulary.

They started coming on the eighth day.

Somewhere over forty in the first month, and it did not slow down for a year. A man whose
brother went into the sea in the spring. Two sisters about a father. A woman who had been
told for seven years that her daughter had drowned at the Munis and who wanted the cells
opened, all of them, and who was not mad and had never been mad and had simply been given
permission by a head of state.

And the palace did what a palace does, which is that it invented a procedure.

There is a clerk. There is a form — I have seen it, it is two sides and it asks for the date
of death and the place of burial and the name of the coin bearer, which I would like you to
notice — and there is a Tuesday, and on the Tuesday the president sees six of them herself,
for a quarter of an hour each, because she decided in the second week that she was not going
to let anybody else be the person who said no.

Eurydice of Heraklion filled in the form.

She got the paper from a clerk on the harbour road, and she wrote down that she had died on
Olympia Day, and where she was buried, and under *coin bearer* she wrote her husband's name,
which I did not know at the time and which I would have advised against and which is the
reason her form went to the top of the pile, because the clerk recognised it.

Five weeks. A secretary, a paper supplier and a woman with the flowers.

She was in the room on the Tuesday. She was fourth of the six.

---

Here is what a quarter of an hour looks like from the other side of that table, because I was
in the corner of that room and I had been in the corner of it for three of them by then and I
had begun to understand what the job was.

Ari was very good at it. That is the part nobody expects. She was quick and she was kind and
she did not perform any of it — she asked the questions in the order they are on the form, and
she listened, and she did not let anybody finish a sentence they were going to be ashamed of,
and at the end of each one she said the true thing, which was that she was sorry and that
there was no reason to think it and that she was not going to open the cells.

Four of them cried. She let them.

She was, by the fourth of the morning, extremely tired, and Riddy walked in and said she was
Eurydice and had died in the street and had come back, and Ari said — pleasantly, without any
edge on it at all, in the voice of a woman who has said it three times before lunch —

"I know who Eurydice is."

And then she said that the song is sung in that city every night of the week, and that she had
been at the funeral, and that she was very sorry.

---

Riddy did not argue.

I have thought about that a great deal since. Six weeks earlier she would have argued; she
argued nine times in her first six days and lost all nine and had a man tell her it was not
funny, his sister loved her. She had learned the shape of it by then. She knew that a well
established fact is not damaged by a person standing in front of you contradicting it, and
that the person is, and that the argument is the thing that finishes you.

So she stopped, and she said thank you, and she got up, and she was at the door with the
clerk's hand more or less on it when she turned round and said:

*A line appears, the order wanes, the family falls, and kaos reigns.*

---

Nobody in that room understood what had happened except the two of them.

The clerk did not. The clerk was already writing *no further action* on the form, which he had
been doing since about the second sentence. I want it noted that there was no drama in it
whatsoever from the outside; a tired woman said four clauses to another tired woman, quietly,
from a doorway.

Ari said: "Say it again."

Riddy said it again.

Ari asked her where she had got it. Riddy said it was hers, that she had been given it as a
child, and that she had never said it out loud to a living person on Earth.

And that is unanswerable, and Ari knew it was unanswerable inside about four seconds, because
there is one thing every human being in this world knows about a prophecy and it is that
yours is yours. They are written for one person. No two are ever the same — I have told you as
much myself, more than once, and with some confidence, and I had it from the Fates and it was
what I believed.

There is no mechanism by which a woman off the harbour road has Ariadne of Krete's prophecy in
her mouth. Not a spy, not a leak, not a guess. Ari had carried that thing since before she was
two years old and had never once written it down.

She sent the clerk out.

---

They were in there for three hours and I stayed for all of it and I am going to give you the
useful parts only.

Riddy told her the whole thing, in order, and she told it well, which she had not managed with
her mother in the booth and had not managed on a crate in a square. Six weeks of failing at it
had made her good at it. She did the coin, and the water that would not rise, and the two
hundred years, and the divers, and what a diver is actually for. Then the Frame. Then the room
behind the door in the wall at the back of the building, and what is standing up in it, and
what is at the other end of the process, and who drinks it.

Ari did not interrupt her once.

Then Riddy gave her the other thing, which is the only thing she had that nobody else in the
world had, and I would like it recorded that she volunteered it and was not asked.

She told her what the prophecy means.

Ari has believed since before she could speak that hers was about her brother — that she had
fulfilled it in a cot before the age of two by smothering the wrong twin, that she was the line
and the waning and the falling and the rest of it. Six weeks earlier she had learned that she
had not done that. Which left her holding four clauses with nothing under them, and no way of
asking anybody, and no reason to think it was about anything except her.

Riddy told her that it is not about her. That there are at least three of them carrying it
word for word, that one of them is a dead man at the bottom of the world, and that she and he
had worked out in a room under the Earth what *the family* is.

It is not the house of Minos. It never was.

---

Then Ari did the arithmetic herself and I want to be exact that nobody handed it to her.

She had gone quiet for a while. And then she said — not to Riddy, to the table —

"Six weeks ago I put a coin in my brother's mouth."

Riddy said yes.

Ari said: "Is he still there?"

And Riddy, who had been dead and had stood in that room and had walked past several hundred
thousand people standing up in it, and who does not lie to make an afternoon easier, said yes.

That was the end of the meeting. There was some business after it, but that was the end of it.

---

Now. What I expected, and what happened.

I expected an announcement.

It was not a stupid expectation and I would make it again. The only instrument that woman had
ever used to any effect was the one she used on the sixth day, from those steps, in front of
the country — four days of priestcraft detonated with about six minutes of true statements,
and I had watched her do it and had admired it. I assumed she would do it again, larger. I
said so, to myself, on the stairs, and began composing what I would have advised.

She did not announce anything. She has still not announced anything, and it is two years, and
the coins are still going into the ground in that city at the ordinary rate.

What she did instead was ask for it in writing.

She sent for paper and she sat Riddy down at the small table by the window and she took her
through the whole of it again, slowly, from the beginning, and wrote it out in her own hand —
not a secretary, her own hand, over the better part of two more hours — and where Riddy was
not certain she wrote *not certain* in the margin, and where Riddy had it secondhand from a
diver or a handler she wrote the name of the diver or the handler.

I thought it was shock — something too large to be used, and a reach for the smallest
available action, and an announcement to follow in a week when she had slept.

It was not shock. It was the first sound piece of statecraft anybody in this account performed
against Olympus, and it was performed forty minutes after she found out there was an Olympus
worth performing against.

She had learned exactly one thing on the sixth day, and it was not that telling the truth
works. It was that telling the truth from those steps gets you two official accounts, a queue
of claimants and no mechanism for deciding anything. She was not going to spend the second one
the way she had spent the first.

So she made a document, with sources on it, that could be shown to a person in a room.

It has one word on the front of it, in her writing.

It is not *Renewal* and it is not *Olympus* and it is not *the Frame*.

It says *Glaucus*.

---

Riddy slept that night in a room off the west courtyard that Ari's people found for her, which
had a table in it and nothing else, and which she was still in when the leaves went.

She had been on Earth six weeks. She had said the true thing to a priest, an aunt, her own
mother and about a hundred other people, and had been agreed with by all of them and heard by
none, and on that Tuesday she was believed completely by the head of state of the country she
was standing in.

And the document went into a drawer, and nothing came of any of it for four months.

I did not understand that at the time either.

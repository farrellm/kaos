# Voice and Style

Craft notes for the novelization: how this world sounds, how it is narrated, and which of
its effects are load-bearing rather than decorative.

For working authors who have solved these same problems, and what to take from each, see
[10-INFLUENCES.md](10-INFLUENCES.md).

---

## The narrator

**Prometheus tells the story, in first person, directly to the reader.** He is the only
character who does. This is not framing — it is the spine of the show's irony, because he
is a participant with an agenda, and by the end an unreliable one.

His habits:
- **Second-person address.** He tells *you* what to remember, promises to come back to
  someone, and tells you when you already know what happens next.
- **Withholding on purpose.** He introduces Ari in Episode 1 with an instruction to
  remember her and does not explain for two more episodes.
- **Corrective.** He interrupts received myth to say the version you've heard is wrong —
  about his own punishment, about Renewal, about who Zeus was.
- **Flat cruelty followed by tenderness.** He can describe an atrocity in one clause and
  Charon's knee in the next.
- **Structural authority that collapses.** For seven episodes he speaks as though he
  authored events. In Episode 8 he narrates his own panic in real time. The narrator losing
  the plot is the season's final reveal, and a novelization should let the prose register
  it — the voice should thin out and start guessing.

**For Season 2:** the honest question is whether Prometheus is still the narrator, and
whether he can be trusted now that he knows he was a piece rather than a player. Any change
in narrator is a structural statement, not a stylistic one.

## Tonal register

**Comedy and atrocity share a scene, without a tonal bridge.** Zeus shoots a ball boy dead
between skeet clays and complains about coffee. The Furies serve popcorn before showing a
woman her brother's murder. The rule is that the funny thing is never a relief from the
terrible thing; it is the same thing, seen from the perspective of someone with power.

**Anachronism is played flat.** Answering machines, fax machines, subway trains, a
corporate onboarding video, a TV quiz, sunscreen, argan oil shampoo. Nobody remarks on it.
There is no wink. The modern furniture exists because gods with absolute power would of
course have voicemail and nobody would of course return their calls.

**Profanity is class-blind and constant.** Gods swear more than mortals. The most vicious
lines in the series are delivered casually, in endearments — Hera in particular weaponizes
"darling."

**Violence arrives without a beat of warning and is not aestheticized.** A truck. A
crossbow. A knife in a corridor. A kitten. The camera does not linger and the story does
not pause; a novelization should resist elegy in the moment and let the grief arrive
several pages later, which is how the show plays it.

## Dialogue idiom by speaker

| Speaker | Sound |
|---|---|
| **Zeus** | Mid-century American showbiz. Self-interrupting, nicknames, invented endearments ("Papooza"), grandiose abstractions crashing into crudeness. Repeats phrases to steady himself. |
| **Hera** | Precise, cold, English. Endearments as blades. Never raises her voice except in a set-piece. |
| **Poseidon** | Louche, tactile, unbothered. Talks about violence the way other people talk about lunch. |
| **Hades** | Apologetic middle management. Long run-ups to difficult sentences. Says "I love you" as a preamble. |
| **Persephone** | Dry, clipped, practical. The straightest talker among the gods. |
| **Dionysus** | Warm, fast, profane, easily hurt. The only god who says "please" to a mortal. |
| **Prometheus** | Wry, formal, affectionate, wounded. Old-world diction from a man who has had thousands of years to phrase things. |
| **Riddy** | Deadpan, defensive, quick. Deflects sentiment with a joke and then cannot sustain it. |
| **Caeneus** | Gentle, patient, self-deprecating about horrors. Asks questions rather than making claims. |
| **Ari** | Formal in public, foul in private. Rage held under pressure. |
| **Orpheus** | Effusive, theatrical, generous, needy. Declares love as punctuation. |
| **Minos** | Statesman in public, supplicant before gods, tender manipulator with his daughter. |
| **The Fates** | Overlapping triplets, delighted with themselves, technically truthful. |
| **The Furies** | Whispered repetition ("remember"), then chatty and domestic. |
| **Medusa** | Brisk, funny, no time for anyone. |
| **Charon** | Bitter, deliberately cruel, still keeping his word. |

## Recurring language

- **"Vero."** The liturgical affirmation, used as amen and as sign-off. It carries the
  whole state religion in two syllables and should be preserved untranslated.
- **"Blessed Olympia."** Public greeting on the holy day; also a way of saying nothing.
- **"Renewal."** The euphemism at the center of the lie. Capitalized as doctrine.
- **The four-clause prophecy**, repeated verbatim by four different beings. Its power is in
  the exactness of the repetition — it should never be paraphrased.
- **"Today's the day."** Cassandra's phrase to Riddy, echoed by Caeneus's mother to
  Caeneus. Two prophets, ten years apart, same sentence, same function.
- **"Fuck the gods."** Graffiti, blasphemy, criminal charge, Fury advice, and finally Ari's
  answer to her father. It escalates from vandalism to a political position.
- **"There are no mistakes here."** The underworld's administrative motto.
- **"Gods don't bleed."** Said as fact three times, and once as a taunt in a dream.

## Music

The show scores mythic action with recognizable popular music, and the selections work
against the image rather than with it — cheerful standards over atrocity, sincere ballads
over cruelty, disco over loneliness. Music is also plot: Orpheus is a musician, his song
for Riddy plays over her death, and his wordless performance at the Cave is the moment the
quiz turns real.

**For prose:** the effect that translates is not the songs themselves but the *ironic
cheerfulness of the surrounding world* — the tune on a car radio during a killing, the
buoyant PA announcement in the port, the peppy orientation video. Keep the world upbeat
and indifferent while the characters are destroyed inside it.

## Structural habits worth keeping

1. **Rewind and re-show.** Episode 3 retells Olympia Day from Ari's side. The same event
   from another angle is the show's default way of delivering exposition.
2. **Cold opens that seem unrelated** and turn out to be origin stories — Caeneus's murder,
   the Furies' bathroom suicide, Prometheus and Charon.
3. **Cross-cutting three strands** — Olympus, Earth, underworld — with thematic rhymes on
   the cut rather than plot ones.
4. **Endings on a hard reversal**, no denouement. Almost every episode ends on the beat
   after a revelation, not the beat after a reaction.
5. **Small objects carry the plot.** A watch. A coin. A cereal box toy. A cat. A bell.
   A bendy straw. The show tracks physical objects with more discipline than it tracks
   locations, and a novelization should do the same.
